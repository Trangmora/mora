15 restaurant TikTok content ideas that turn views into tables

Teach a stranger one thing about your food in the first two seconds, then tell them where you are. Here are 15 ideas by format, hooks to copy, a shot list for one filming session and the steps that turn a view into a visit.

By the Bunchasake team · Published 11 Nov 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways
  • TikTok mostly shows your video to strangers, so every video has to explain itself and say where the restaurant is.
  • Videos that teach a first-timer how to order, say or eat a dish usually work better than polished food shots, especially when your food is new to the viewer.
  • A series with the same opening line each week is easier to film and easier to remember than fifteen unrelated videos.
  • One quiet hour with one dish can give you five videos if you follow a shot list.
  • Views become customers only when the city, the street and the opening hours are spoken, written on screen and repeated in the comments.

The restaurant TikTok content ideas that work are simple: teach a stranger one thing about your food in the first two seconds, make them hungry, and tell them where you are. You do not need dancing, trends or a ring light. This guide gives you 15 ideas in five groups, written for any restaurant whose food is worth a little explaining, with hooks to copy, a shot list for one filming session and the small steps that turn a view into a table.

How TikTok is different from Instagram for a restaurant

On Instagram, most of the people who see you already follow you or have looked you up. TikTok works the other way round. It shows your video to people who have never heard of you, because they watched something similar. That changes three things.

  • The viewer knows nothing. They do not know your name, your city or what bún chả is. The video has to explain itself.
  • The first two seconds decide. A slow pan over the dining room is skipped. A sentence that starts a small story is not.
  • Talking beats polish. A cook explaining a dish in plain words, with kitchen noise behind, usually holds attention better than a silent, perfect plate.

If you already use our restaurant Instagram content ideas, keep them. This article does not repeat them. It covers the formats that suit TikTok: explaining, series, replies and local videos.

Three things to set up before you film

  • The account type. TikTok offers personal and business accounts, and the music and tools you can use differ between them and between countries. Read what applies to you at TikTok for Business before you choose. When in doubt, use your own kitchen sound and your own voice, which is always safe.
  • The bio. What you cook, the neighbourhood and the city, the days you are open. "Family kitchen in Footscray, Melbourne. Phở, bún bò Huế, bánh xèo. Closed Mondays."
  • The same name everywhere. A viewer who gets hungry leaves TikTok and types your name into a map. Make sure the name on TikTok matches the sign and your Google Business Profile, and that the hours there are right.

15 restaurant TikTok content ideas, grouped by format

Each idea has a hook, which is the line you say or write on screen in the first two seconds. Change the dishes to your own.

Teach a first-timer

  • 1. How to order on a first visit. Stand at the counter and name three dishes for someone who has never been. Hook: "New to this food? Order these three."
  • 2. Say it with me. The cook says a dish name slowly, a local staff member tries, everybody laughs. Hook: "How to say bún bò Huế, from someone who grew up there."
  • 3. What is on the herb plate. Lift each herb, name it, say which dish it goes into. Hook: "This plate is not decoration."
  • 4. This or that. Two bowls side by side: phở and bún bò Huế, or a fried spring roll and a fresh summer roll. Hook: "They look alike. They are not."
  • 5. Which sauce goes where. Line up the sauces on the table and match each to a dish. Hook: "Please stop putting hoisin in the broth before you taste it."

Sound and process

  • 6. One dish, start to finish. Bánh xèo from batter to fold in twenty seconds, real sizzle, no music. Hook, as text only: "Listen to this."
  • 7. The count. Show the day's work with your own real number. Hook: "This is how many dumplings two people fold before lunch."

People and family

  • 8. The lunch rush from the inside. Phone at chest height, tickets coming in, bowls going out. Hook: "Twelve thirty at a family restaurant."
  • 9. Mum tastes the new dish. One honest reaction, with subtitles. Hook: "We changed the recipe. Mẹ has opinions."
  • 10. The next generation explains. A son or daughter who grew up in the restaurant tells one short memory. Hook: "Things you learn growing up in a phở shop."

Series and replies

  • 11. Reply to a comment with a video. When someone asks "Is it spicy?" or "Where is this?", answer on camera. TikTok lets you reply to a comment with a new video in most regions. Hook: the comment itself, on screen.
  • 12. Dish of the week. Same opening line, same spot in the kitchen, a new dish each week. Hook: "Dish of the week, episode four: cơm tấm."
  • 13. Order for me. One situation per episode: a date, four colleagues, a child who refuses vegetables. Hook: "What to order for two people who cannot agree."

Local and seasonal

  • 14. The walk from the station. Film the route from the nearest landmark to your door. Hook: "Sixty seconds from Central Station to a bowl of phở."
  • 15. The calendar from home. The festivals of the place your cooking comes from, such as Tết or Mid-Autumn, and what your family eats on those days. Hook: "What our family eats on the first day of Tết."

Only say what is true in your kitchen. If the broth simmers for six hours, say six, not twelve.

One filming session, five videos

Pick a quiet hour, one dish and one person who is comfortable talking. Then film in this order.

  1. The explanation. The cook holds the finished dish and says what it is in two sentences. This is your "teach a first-timer" video.
  2. The process. Six short clips of the dish being made, each five seconds, with the real sound.
  3. The detail. One close shot of the step nobody sees, with one sentence over it.
  4. The eating. A staff member shows how to eat it, including the sauce and the herbs.
  5. The directions. Walk out of the door, turn the camera to the street and say where you are and when you are open.

Hold the phone upright, wipe the lens and stand near a window. For light, angles and steam, see our restaurant menu photography tips.

How to turn views into customers

A video can be watched by many people who live in another country. That is normal and it is not a failure. Your job is to make sure the nearby ones know they are nearby.

  • Say the place out loud. "We are on King Street in Newtown." Then put the city in the text on screen and in the caption too, so that people watching without sound, and people searching, can find it.
  • Add the location when your account offers that option.
  • Write a useful caption. Dish, neighbourhood, city, opening days. "Bún chả in Kreuzberg, Berlin. Open Tuesday to Sunday."
  • Answer every "where is this?" with the street and the hours, even when the answer is in the bio.
  • Ask at the till. "How did you hear about us?" Write the answers in a notebook. This is the only number that matters.

The same file can go to Instagram Reels and Facebook. Upload the original from your phone, not a download that carries another app's logo. The tools in Meta for Business let you schedule both from one place. For the wider plan beyond social media, read how to market a restaurant.

Which language should you speak in the videos?

Speak the language of the people you want to walk in. In most cities that is the local language, with the dish names kept in their original language and written on screen with their accents. Add subtitles, because many people watch without sound.

Common mistakes

  • Copying a trend that has nothing to do with food. It may get views, but nobody learns what you cook.
  • Filming guests without asking. Ask first, and leave children out unless a parent clearly agrees. Rules on filming people differ by country.
  • A look that changes every week. The same opening line, the same corner of the kitchen and the same lettering help people recognise you. If the restaurant has no clear name, story or look yet, that comes first, and it is what Bunchasake does in its brand and marketing work for restaurants.

Frequently asked questions

How often should a restaurant post on TikTok?

Two or three videos a week is a pace a family team can hold. One filming session with the shot list above covers more than a week. A steady rhythm matters more than volume, so choose what you can keep up in your busiest month.

Do I need to appear on camera?

No. Hands, a voice and the food are enough for most of these ideas. A face helps people remember you, though, so if someone in the family or on the team is comfortable talking, let them be the regular presenter.

My videos get views from other countries. Is that wasted?

Not wasted, but not customers either. Say the city and street in the video, write them on screen and in the caption, and add the location where the option exists. Then judge by what guests tell you at the till, not by the view count.

Can I post the same video on TikTok and Instagram?

Yes. Film once, keep the original file and upload it separately to each platform. Change the caption a little, since Instagram followers already know where you are and TikTok viewers usually do not.
