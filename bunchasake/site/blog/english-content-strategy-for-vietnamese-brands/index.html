English content strategy for brands going global, for teams whose first language is not English

Start with the pages closest to the purchase, not with a blog. Choose one reader, fix the money pages, write a one-page voice guide, then publish helpful articles at a pace your team can keep.

An English content strategy for brands going global does not start with a blog. It starts with the pages closest to the purchase: the homepage, the pricing or product pages, and the pages that remove doubt. Choose one reader, fix those pages, write a one-page voice guide, then publish helpful articles at a pace your team can keep.

This guide is written for software companies and online shops whose team writes English as a second language, and it covers brand and content only. Terms of service, privacy policies, tax and any other legal, customs or certification question need qualified advisers in the market you sell to.

Choose one reader before one word

"International customers" is not a reader. A reader is a person you can describe.

An invoicing tool built in Hà Nội might choose the office manager of a small design agency in Australia, who does the invoices on Friday afternoon and wants to go home. A linen clothing brand from Hội An might choose a woman in the US who buys a few good pieces a year and reads the fabric label.

Write a reader card with four lines:

  1. Who they are, and where.
  2. The problem, in their own words. Not "invoice management inefficiency" but "I waste my Friday chasing payments".
  3. What they compare you with, including doing nothing.
  4. What worries them about buying from a company far away that they have never heard of.

Every page you write should answer line two and calm line four.

An English content strategy for brands going global starts with the money pages

A new visitor does not read your blog and then decide to trust you. They land on a product or pricing page, look for reasons to leave, and stay if they find none. So write in this order.

OrderSaaSE-commerceJob of these pages
1Homepage, pricing pageHomepage, best-selling product pagesSay what it is, who it is for and what it costs
2Sign-up screens, welcome emails, help articlesShipping, returns, size guide, order emailsRemove doubt at the moment of buying
3Use-case pages, honest comparison pages, about pageCategory pages, about page, customer reviewsAnswer "why you and not the others"
4Guides, product updates, newsletterGuides, care instructions, newsletterBring new readers and keep old ones

Row two is the one most often skipped. The website is polished, then the welcome email and the error messages read like a rough translation, or the shipping and returns page is missing.

Formats matter on these pages too: currencies, dates, sizes, measures. They are covered in our cross-border localization checklist.

Sound like a person, not a brochure

Good English content from a team writing in its second language is not about perfect grammar. It is about dropping the habits of the traditional company profile, which do not travel.

HabitBeforeAfter
Praising yourselfWe are a prestigious company with enthusiastic and professional staff.Two people answer support, and you get a reply the same working day.
Empty quality wordsHigh quality products at reasonable prices.Linen woven in Nam Định, pre-washed so it does not shrink.
Stacked abstractionsA comprehensive digital solution platform for enterprises.Send an invoice in two clicks. See who has paid.
Stiff lettersDear Valued Customer, please kindly be informed that...Hi Anna, your order shipped today.

The "after" lines are examples of form. Write only what is true for your own product.

Then put the rules on one page, the voice guide:

  • Three words for how you sound. For example: clear, friendly, exact.
  • We say, we do not say. Five pairs, taken from your own pages.
  • American or British English. One, everywhere.
  • A glossary. One name for each feature, material and plan. If the button says "workspace", the help article does not say "project room".

With this guide, several writers, a freelancer and an AI assistant can sound like one company. Why writing natively beats translating line by line is explained in our article on bilingual marketing, one brand in two languages.

What to publish after the money pages

Do not start from a list of keywords. Start from questions customers already ask. You have four free sources:

  • your support inbox and chat history
  • notes from sales calls and demos
  • reviews of your product and of competitors
  • the suggestions a search box shows when you type the customer's problem

Turn each real question into one article that answers it fully. Four types work well for small teams:

  1. How-to. "How to invoice a client in another currency." "How to wash linen so it lasts."
  2. Honest comparison. You against the obvious alternative, including where the alternative is the better choice.
  3. Use case. How one kind of customer uses the product, step by step. Use real customers only with their permission, and never invent one.
  4. Behind the product. How it is built or made, by whom, where. This is the content nobody else can write.

Write for people first, not for search engines. Google's own pages on creating helpful, people-first content and its SEO Starter Guide are the official places to read before you plan. Who writes, and how AI fits in

The person who knows the product best often writes English slowly. The person who writes English well often does not know the product. Split the job:

  1. The expert gives the raw material. In their own language if that is faster: notes, a voice message, a screen recording.
  2. A first draft in English. By a bilingual writer or an AI assistant, following the voice guide and glossary.
  3. The expert checks the facts. Features, prices, materials, promises.
  4. A native English editor fixes the language. A freelancer for a few hours a month is enough to start.
  5. One person approves and publishes.

AI does the fast work, people make the decisions. An unedited AI draft is fluent, generic and sometimes wrong about your own product, so it never goes out unread.

One more check if you sell to the US: claims and customer testimonials have rules there. The official place to read about them is the Federal Trade Commission's business guidance, and a lawyer confirms what applies to you.

A three-month plan to copy

MonthWorkDone when
1Reader card. Voice guide and glossary. Rewrite homepage and pricing or top product pages.Five native readers can say what you sell and what it costs without help.
2Rewrite the doubt-removing pages and all automatic emails. Add an about page with real names and places.A test sign-up or test order shows no rough English from start to finish.
3List twenty customer questions. Publish the first articles. Set a rhythm you can keep.Each article answers one real question and links to one product or pricing page.

How to judge progress: compare with your own previous month, not with someone else's numbers. Watch whether sign-ups or orders come from the English pages, whether sales questions get more specific, and whether support stops receiving questions a page should have answered.

Common mistakes

  • Starting with the blog. Articles send readers to a homepage that still cannot explain the product.
  • Translating your home-market site. The structure and the proof were built for a buyer at home.
  • Publishing a lot, then stopping. Two good articles a month for a year beat twenty in the first month.
  • Different names for the same thing. The reader thinks you have three features where you have one.

Frequently asked questions

Should we write in our own language first and then translate?

Collect the facts in your own language if that is faster for your experts, but write the English page as an English page, from the reader card and not from a finished text in another language. Translation keeps the structure of the original, which is the part that needs to change.

American or British English?

Follow your first reader. If most customers are in the US, use American English. If they are in the UK, Australia or much of Europe, British English is the usual choice. What matters most is choosing one and staying with it.

Can AI write our English content?

It can write drafts, and it is very useful for that. A person who knows the product must check the facts and a native editor must check the language before anything is published.

Do we need a blog at all?

Not at the start. Clear product and pricing pages, and help pages that answer real questions, are already content that works. Add articles when someone can keep the rhythm.
