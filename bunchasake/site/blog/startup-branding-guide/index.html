Startup branding before your first launch: name, positioning, brand kit, page and voice

Before launch, a startup does not need a big brand. It needs a name it can keep, one clear paragraph about who it is for, a small brand kit, one page that explains the offer and a voice the founders can keep up. Here is the order to do it in.

Startup branding before your first launch is a short list of decisions, not a big identity project. Check that the name is one you can keep, write down who the product is for and what it replaces, build a brand kit that fits on one page, publish one page that explains the offer, and agree how you sound. Do them in that order, because each one depends on the one before.

This guide covers the brand side only. Company registration, trade marks, contracts and tax are legal questions, and they belong with a qualified adviser in the country where you operate.

What startup branding means before launch

A brand is what people remember and repeat about you when you are not in the room. Before launch, the job is not to look big. It is to make sure the first people who meet the product hear the same simple thing, on a launch page, in a founder's post or in a demo.

Think of two founders building a booking tool for small physiotherapy clinics, with a working product and a launch date. What they need from branding is modest and urgent: a name the clinics can find again, a sentence a clinic owner can repeat to a colleague, a logo that works as an app icon, and a page that says what the tool does. A thick brand book can wait. A clear sentence cannot.

Step 1: check the name before anyone opens a design tool

Founders often fall for a name, buy the domain and order a logo the same week, then find out the name is hard to say or already used in their field. Check first, design second, with five quick tests:

  1. The phone test. Say the name once to someone on a call. Can they type it into a search box without asking how to spell it?
  2. The search test. Search the name together with your product category, in the app stores and on the social platforms you will use. Are you clearly different from what comes up?
  3. The handle test. Can you get the same or a close username on the channels that matter to you, and a web address people will not mistype?
  4. The meaning test. Ask a few native speakers in each market you plan to sell in what the name reminds them of. If it has accents or tone marks, check how it looks without them.
  5. The growth test. Will the name still fit if you add a second product or a second country?

These tests tell you whether a name works as a brand, not whether you may use it and register it as a trade mark. That is a legal question that differs by country: ask a qualified trade mark adviser before you launch or pay for a logo. If you plan to sell in Europe, the official place to read about EU trade marks is the EU Intellectual Property Office. If you want protection in several countries, read about the WIPO Madrid System, then let your adviser tell you what applies to you.

Step 2: write the positioning in one paragraph

Positioning is the decision about who the product is for, what it replaces and why it is the better choice for that person. It comes before design because a designer cannot draw a decision you have not made. Fill in this paragraph and rewrite it until every founder agrees with every word:

For [the customer], who [the problem, in their words], [name] is a [category] that [the main result]. Unlike [what they use today], it [the difference that matters to them].

For the physiotherapy example: "For small physiotherapy clinics, whose front desk spends the morning on the phone moving appointments, [name] is a booking tool that lets patients move their own appointment by text. Unlike practice software built for hospital groups, it can be set up in an afternoon."

This paragraph is for the team. It will not appear on the website word for word, but every headline and pitch will come from it. If your product is software, our guide on how to position a SaaS product goes deeper into choosing the category and the alternatives.

Step 3: build a minimum brand kit

A minimum brand kit is the smallest set of pieces that lets anyone on the team, including a freelancer, make a slide, a post or a page that looks like the same company. Copy this table and fill in the middle column.

PieceEnough for launchCan wait
Name and short descriptionThe tested name, plus one sentence that says what the product isA naming system for future products
LogoA wordmark and a small symbol that still reads as an app icon or a round profile picture, each in colour and in one colourAnimated logo, many layout versions
ColoursOne main colour, one accent, a dark and a light neutral, with text that stays readable on each backgroundExtended palette, seasonal colours
TypographyOne or two typefaces that work on screen and are free or properly licensedA custom typeface
VoiceThree words for how you sound and five "we say, we do not say" pairsA full tone guide for every channel
ImagesOne rule, for example: real product screens, no stock photos of handshakesAn illustration system, a brand film
TemplatesProfile picture, social header, one post template, one slide template, an email signatureA full template library

Test colour contrast early. Light grey text on white looks elegant in a mock-up and is hard to read on a phone outdoors. The W3C's Web Content Accessibility Guidelines are the official reference for readable contrast.

Step 4: one launch page with one job

Before launch, you do not need a full website. You need one page that helps the right visitor understand the offer and take one next step: join the waitlist, book a demo or buy.

  • Headline: what it is, in plain words. "Online booking for small physiotherapy clinics" says more than "The future of care".
  • Sub-line: who it is for and the result they get.
  • Three short blocks: how it works, or the three things it does better than what they use today.
  • Proof you actually have: a short demo, real screenshots, the founders' names and why you built it, early users who agreed to be quoted. Never invent praise.
  • One call to action, repeated at the bottom of the page.

Then ask three people who match your customer to look at the page for a few seconds, close it, and tell you what the product does and for whom. If their answers differ from your positioning paragraph, fix the headline first. When you are ready to grow this page into a full site, work through our startup website checklist.

Step 5: a voice the founders can keep

In the first year, the founders write most of the words: the launch post, onboarding emails, replies to the first users, error messages. So the voice should be the best version of how the founders already talk to a customer, not one nobody can keep up.

Write it down in three parts:

  1. Three words. For example: clear, warm, exact. Add one line on what each means in practice.
  2. Five pairs. We say: "Your appointment is now on Thursday at 10." We do not say: "Your appointment modification request has been successfully processed."
  3. A short glossary. One name for each feature and plan, used the same way on the site, in the app and in support replies.

Test the voice where it is hardest: a welcome email, an error message and a reply to an annoyed user. For using that voice in public as a founder, read our guide to founder-led content on LinkedIn.

A pre-launch brand checklist to copy

  • The name passes the phone, search, handle, meaning and growth tests.
  • A qualified adviser has checked whether you can use and register the name.
  • Every founder agrees with the positioning paragraph, word for word.
  • The logo reads at app-icon size and in one colour.
  • The launch page passes the few-seconds test with three target customers.
  • The welcome email and error messages follow the voice and the glossary.
  • The brand kit page is saved where everyone, freelancers included, can find it.

Common mistakes

  • Designing before deciding. The logo comes before the founders agree who the product is for, so it gets redone.
  • A name nobody can spell. Clever spellings and invented words lose people between hearing the name and searching for it.
  • Skipping the legal check. Renaming after launch means a new domain, new handles and customers who learned the old name.
  • Borrowed proof. Invented testimonials, press logos used without permission or made-up user numbers destroy trust the moment someone checks.

Frequently asked questions

How much branding does a startup need before launch?

Enough that every touchpoint says the same thing, which means the five steps in this guide. The rest can wait until customers have helped shape your message.

Should we hire a designer before we have our positioning?

It works better the other way round. Without knowing who the product is for and how it is different, a designer can only work from taste. Bring your positioning paragraph to the first meeting.

Do we need to register a trade mark before launch?

That depends on your country, your plans and the name itself, so it is a question for a qualified adviser. Ask before you pay for a logo, so you do not build a brand on a name you may have to give up.

Can we use AI to come up with a name or a logo?

AI is useful for many name ideas and quick visual directions. People still decide, the name still needs the five tests, and an adviser should confirm your rights to anything generated before you use it.
