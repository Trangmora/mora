Omnichannel brand presence: taking your brand to the places your customers already go

An omnichannel brand presence means your brand looks, sounds and works the same on every channel your customers use, from your website to Instagram to the local media they follow. Choose channels by where your customers already are, and if you already have channels, read their data before planning anything new.

By the Bunchasake team · Published 11 Nov 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways
  • Reach comes after identity, because spreading a brand people do not yet recognise across many channels only spreads confusion.
  • Pick channels by where your customers already spend time, not by which platform is new or which one a competitor uses.
  • Each channel has a different job, so the same brand can show a menu on the website, a short video on TikTok and a booking reminder in a chat app.
  • If you already have channels, read their data, customer behaviour and past campaign results before planning a new campaign.
  • Fewer channels kept alive beat many channels left half empty, because an abandoned profile tells customers the business may be closed.

An omnichannel brand presence means customers meet the same brand wherever they look for you: the same name, logo, colours and voice on your website, your social media profiles, your Google listing and the local media they read. It is the fourth step of our process, called reach, and it starts once the brand has an identity people can recognise. The goal is not to be everywhere. It is to be where your customers already go, with each channel doing the job it does best. If you already have channels running, this step begins by reading what they tell you.

Why reach comes after identity

Reach is step four of six. By now the founder's story has been heard, the brand's position and story are written, and the identity (logo, colours, type, voice) has been agreed and packed into guidelines, as explained in the brand identity design process. Only then does it make sense to go wide.

Think of a family restaurant that opens a TikTok, a new Instagram and a website in the same month, each made by a different person in a different style. A customer sees a video on TikTok, searches the name, finds an Instagram that looks like another place and a website with an old menu. They are not sure it is the same restaurant. Reach multiplies whatever the brand already is. If the identity is clear, every channel strengthens it. If not, every channel adds noise.

Which channels, and what each is good for

Below are the channels we look at most often with clients. Platform features and audiences change over time and differ by country, so treat this as a starting point and check what your own customers actually use.

Most small businesses do not need all of these. A salon in a strip mall may do best with a website, a Google listing, Instagram and one chat app. A brand selling abroad to business buyers may need a website, LinkedIn and YouTube. The useful question is: where do the customers in our positioning already spend time, and what are they doing there?

One brand, many channels: keeping it consistent

Omnichannel does not mean posting the same thing everywhere. It means the brand is recognisable everywhere while each channel does its own job. A few rules help:

  • Same name, handle and profile picture on every channel, so people know they found the right account.
  • Same facts everywhere. Opening hours, address, phone and prices must match. A wrong hour on one channel costs a visit.
  • Same voice, different length. A TikTok caption is shorter than a website page, but both should sound like the same person, following the voice rules in your guidelines.
  • Every channel points somewhere. A profile should lead to the next step: book, order, visit or message.
  • One plan, many outputs. Plan content once and adapt it per channel. Our guide to multichannel posting from one plan shows how.

Already have channels? Read the data first

Many owners come to this step with channels already running: an Instagram with years of posts, a Facebook page, maybe some boosted posts or a past promotion. Before planning anything new, we read three things: the channel data, how customers behave and what happened in past campaigns. Then we advise on a strategy for new campaigns. You can start the same reading yourself with the checklist below. Each platform shows its numbers in its own insights or analytics section, and the names change, so look for the closest equivalent. Meta for Business and TikTok for Business explain where to find them.

Checklist: reading the channels you already have

  1. List every channel. Include old or forgotten accounts. Note who has the login and when it was last updated.
  2. Check the basics. Are name, logo, hours, address and links correct and the same everywhere?
  3. Look at reach, not just followers. How many people actually saw your recent posts compared with how many follow you?
  4. Find your best posts. Sort the last few months by saves, shares, comments and messages. What do the top posts have in common: a topic, a format, a person, a time?
  5. Find your weakest posts. What do they share? That is what to stop doing.
  6. Read the audience. Where do followers live, which language do they use, when are they online? Does that match the customers in your positioning?
  7. Read the comments and messages. Which questions come up again and again? Those are content ideas and gaps in your website.
  8. Trace the path to a sale. Do people book or order from the channel, or do they message first? Where do they drop off?
  9. Review past campaigns. For each one, write down the goal, what was posted or paid for, what it cost and what happened in the shop or the bookings. Note what you would repeat.
  10. Check the website. In your website analytics, which pages do people visit and which channels send them?
  11. Ask the team. Staff often know which posts customers mention at the counter.
  12. Write three conclusions. One thing to keep, one thing to stop, one thing to try. That is the start of the next campaign.

We do not run ads or post daily on clients' behalf. Our job in this step is to decide with you where the brand should be, what each channel is for and what the next campaigns should aim at. Your own team, or the social media automation system we set up for them, carries it out.

How reach works with us

For a new brand, we map where the customers in the positioning spend time, choose a small set of channels, and set each one up with the agreed identity: profiles, bios, cover images and links that lead to the next step. For a brand with channels already running, we start with the reading above, then advise on a strategy for new campaigns: which channels to keep, fix or close, what each should say and what to measure. Common mistakes

  • Opening every channel at once. Start with the few your customers use and do them well.
  • Leaving old profiles live. A forgotten page with last year's hours sends customers to a closed door. Update it or close it.
  • Copying the same post everywhere. A wide photo with a long caption works on Facebook and fails on TikTok.
  • Judging a channel by followers alone. Messages, bookings and visits matter more than the follower count.
  • Starting a new campaign without reading the last one. Past results are the cheapest advice you will get.

Frequently asked questions

How many channels does a small business need?

Usually fewer than owners expect: a website or Google listing as the base, plus the one to three social platforms your customers use most. Add more only when the first ones run well.

Do I need a website if I have Instagram?

It is worth having one. Social platforms can change their rules or reach at any time, while a website is the place you own, where your full menu, prices, story and booking can always live.

What if my old channels look different from my new identity?

Update the profile picture, cover, bio and links first so they match the guidelines. Old posts can stay; what matters is that new visitors see the current brand.

Will you post for us every day?

No. We plan where the brand goes and advise on campaigns. Your team posts, and if you want help with the daily work we can set up an automation system your team runs.

What data should I prepare before this step?

Access to your channels' insights, any website analytics, and notes on past promotions: what you did, when, what it cost and what happened in the business afterwards.
