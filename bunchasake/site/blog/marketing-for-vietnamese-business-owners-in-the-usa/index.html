Marketing for Vietnamese business owners in the USA, without hiring a local agency

Most of what brings customers to a salon or shop in an American neighborhood is free and can be done by the owner in about two hours a week. Here is what to do, in what order, and where outside help is worth paying for.

By BunchasakeUpdated 11 Nov 20268 min read

Key takeaways
  • A neighborhood business in the USA grows on four things, which are being found on the map, looking trustworthy, being recommended and being remembered.
  • You do not need a monthly agency contract to cover the basics. An owner can keep listings, reviews and customer follow-up going in about two hours a week.
  • Ask every happy customer for a review, never pay for one and reply to all of them. Honest reviews are the strongest local marketing you have.
  • Your best partners are the businesses that share your parking lot, because their customers already drive past your door.
  • Pay for outside help where one piece of work lasts for years, such as your name, sign, menu and brand.

Marketing for Vietnamese business owners in the USA is mostly local, and most of it can be done without a local agency. If you run a nail salon, a restaurant, a bakery or a shop, your customers live or work within a short drive. They find you on a map on their phone, check your reviews, look at a few photos and decide. This guide shows how to handle that yourself, step by step, and where a one-time investment makes more sense than a monthly contract.

What local marketing in America comes down to

Forget the marketing vocabulary for a moment. A neighborhood business needs four things to happen.

  1. Be found. When someone nearby types "nail salon near me" or "pho", you appear, with the right address and hours.
  2. Look trustworthy. Recent reviews, real photos, a clear price list or menu, a name that matches the sign.
  3. Be recommended. Customers tell coworkers and neighbors, online and at school pickup.
  4. Be remembered. People who came once come again, because you made it easy.

American customers are used to checking before they go. They read reviews, they expect posted prices, and they notice a reply from the owner. That works in favor of a small business that is honest and looked after.

Many owners are called every week by companies offering to "get you on the first page of Google" for a monthly fee. Before you sign anything, look at what the work actually is.

To be plain about it: Bunchasake does not sell ad management, SEO or Google Business Profile management. We include those jobs here because you can do them yourself.

If you do talk to an agency, ask three things. Who will own the accounts? What exactly will be done each month? Can I cancel at any time? Walk away from anyone who promises a ranking, will not answer plainly, or says they are calling "from Google".

Step 1: Get your listings right

Claim your Google Business Profile. It is free, and it is what shows on Google Maps and in search. Fill in the business name exactly as it is on your sign, the right category, hours including holidays, phone, a booking or menu link, and at least ten real photos: the storefront from the parking lot, the inside, your work.

Then claim your page on Yelp for Business. In many American cities people still check Yelp for food and beauty services, and the page often exists whether you claimed it or not. Yelp's sales team may call to sell advertising. Claiming the page is free, and you can say no to the rest.

Make sure your name, address and phone number are written the same way everywhere, including Apple Maps and Facebook. A suite number that appears on one listing and not on another confuses map apps and customers alike.

Step 2: Earn reviews the honest way

Reviews carry more weight with American customers than anything you say about yourself. A simple routine is enough.

  • Ask at the happiest moment. When a customer is admiring her nails or praising the broth, say: "Thank you, that means a lot. If you have a minute, a Google review really helps a small business like ours."
  • Make it easy. Put a small card with a QR code at the register that opens your review page.
  • Ask everyone, not only the people you expect to be kind.
  • Reply to every review within a few days. Thank the good ones by name. Answer the bad ones calmly and offer to fix the problem.

Do not buy reviews, do not write your own, do not have relatives who have never visited post them, and do not offer a discount in exchange for five stars. Review platforms remove these and can penalize the listing, and there are federal rules on fake and paid-for reviews. The FTC business guidance is the official place to read about advertising and review rules. If you are unsure, ask a qualified adviser.

Step 3: Work your plaza and your neighborhood

Many Vietnamese-owned businesses in the USA sit in a strip mall, sharing a parking lot with a gym, a dry cleaner, a dentist and a coffee shop. Those neighbors are your best partners.

  • Swap cards. Walk in, introduce yourself, leave a small stack of cards or coupons and take theirs.
  • Offer something to their staff. A lunch special for the dental office next door, or a weekday discount for the gym's front desk team. Staff talk to customers all day.
  • Join local online groups. Neighborhood groups on Facebook and Nextdoor are where people ask "Does anyone know a good nail place?" Follow each group's rules, answer when someone asks, and let customers mention you.
  • Say yes to schools and teams. A gift card for a school raffle or a small sponsorship for a youth team puts your name in front of local families.
  • Use your window. Hours, prices or the menu, and a clear "Walk-ins welcome" if that is true, readable from a parked car.

Step 4: Bring customers back

A returning customer costs nothing to find.

  • Rebook before they leave. "Would you like the same time in three weeks?" is the most effective sentence in a salon.
  • Keep a customer list. Name, phone or email, and what they like. A notebook works. A booking app works better.
  • Send reminders with permission. Appointment reminders by text are normal in the USA. Marketing texts and emails have rules about consent and opting out, so use a recognized booking or messaging tool that handles this, ask customers before adding them, and check with a qualified adviser if you are unsure.
  • Reward loyalty simply. A stamp card is still understood by everyone.

The two-hour week

Copy this list and tape it by the register.

  • 20 minutes: reply to every new review and message.
  • 20 minutes: take ten photos of your best work or dishes this week.
  • 30 minutes: schedule two or three posts from those photos.
  • 10 minutes: check hours and holiday closures on Google and Yelp.
  • 20 minutes: visit one neighbor or reply in one local group.
  • 20 minutes: look at the calendar and prepare for the next occasion.

Common mistakes

  • Competing on price alone. There is always a cheaper shop. Customers stay for cleanliness, consistency and how they are treated.
  • Buying followers or reviews. It is easy to spot and can cost you the listing.
  • Using other people's photos. Pictures taken from the internet or from another salon mislead customers and can bring a copyright complaint. Use your own.
  • A name that differs from place to place. One name on the sign, another on Google, a third on the receipt.
  • Signing a long contract after a cold call. Take the paper home, read it, and ask someone you trust.
  • Skipping the legal questions. Licenses, signage permits, taxes and promotion rules differ by state and city. Ask a qualified adviser. The U.S. Small Business Administration is the official starting point for owners.

Where outside help is worth it

The weekly routine works better when the foundation is solid: a name people can say, a sign they can read from the road, a menu or price list that looks like it belongs to the same business. Frequently asked questions

How much should I spend on marketing?

There is no single right figure. Start with what is free: listings, reviews, photos and neighbors. Then spend on the things customers see every day, such as the sign and the menu or price list. Only try paid ads once those are in order, with an amount you can afford to lose while you learn.

Do I need a website if I already have Google and Facebook?

A simple one helps. It is the one place you fully control, and it gives your listings something to link to. One page with what you do, prices or menu, hours, address and a way to book is enough for most local businesses.

Is Yelp still worth the effort?

Claim your page and keep the information correct, because people in many cities still check it for restaurants and beauty services. Whether to pay for Yelp advertising is a separate decision, and you can leave it until the basics are working.

My English is not strong. Can I still do this myself?

Yes. Most of this work is photos, correct information and short replies. Write a set of standard replies once, have a relative or staff member who grew up here check them, and reuse them.

Should I run Google or Facebook ads?

Not first. Ads send people to your listing and your door, so those have to be ready. When they are, test a small local campaign for a few weeks, ask new customers how they found you, and keep it only if it brings in more than it costs.
