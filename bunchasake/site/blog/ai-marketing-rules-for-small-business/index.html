7 rules for using AI in marketing so a small business does not lose trust

AI makes content much faster, and it makes mistakes much faster too. The seven rules below are the minimum fence for using AI without trading away your customers' trust.

By the Bunchasake team · Published 26 Nov 2026 · 6 min read

Key takeaways
  • The seven rules for using AI in marketing are human approval, facts from your own source, real product photos, no fake reviews, customer privacy, saying when it is a machine, and respect for copyright.
  • AI is not responsible for what it writes; when a wrong post goes out under your name, customers blame you, and they are right to.
  • Every number, price, date and time in a post must be in your own document; where something is missing, mark it for checking instead of letting AI guess.
  • Never put customers' names, phone numbers, photos or health details into an AI tool, and never ask AI to write reviews or praise on a customer's behalf.
  • When AI gets something wrong, remove or correct it at once, be honest with the people affected, then add a rule so it does not happen again.

Rules for using AI in marketing should exist before you let AI write its first post. AI makes content much faster, and it makes mistakes much faster too: a wrong price, a promise that is not true, a picture that is not your product, repeated across five channels within minutes. The seven rules below are the minimum fence. They do not slow you down. They keep the most valuable thing a small business has, its customers' trust, from being traded away.

Why you need rules for using AI in marketing

  • AI is not responsible. When a wrong post goes out under your name, customers blame you, and they are right to.
  • Mistakes multiply. A person who writes something wrong gets one post wrong. A system gets it wrong on every channel, every day, until someone notices.
  • AI is wrong with great confidence. Where information is missing, it fills the gap with something that sounds reasonable.
  • Trust is slow to build and quick to lose. A neighbourhood business lives on regulars and recommendations.

7 rules for using AI in marketing

1. A real person reads before anything is published

Nothing reaches a customer before a person has read it. The approver checks four things: prices, dates and times, names, and any sentence that sounds like a promise. In a small shop the approver is the owner. In a chain, write down who approves which kind of content.

2. Facts come only from your own source

Every number, price, opening hour and ingredient must be in the document you give the AI. Give it one instruction: where information is missing, write [CHECK] and do not guess. Do not use phrases such as "studies show" or "90% of customers" when you have no real source.

3. Product and result photos must be real

AI-generated illustrations, greeting cards and backgrounds are fine. The dish, the set of nails, the room and the before-and-after must be your own real photos. Customers compare what they saw with what they receive. When an AI-generated image could be mistaken for a real one, say so.

4. No fake reviews, no fake praise

Do not ask AI to write reviews, testimonials or admiring comments on a customer's behalf. Do not trade gifts for good reviews. This is a matter of ethics, and in many places a matter of law. See the FTC's business guidance if you operate in the United States, and the review rules in the Google Business Profile Help Center.

5. Protect your customers' privacy

Do not paste customers' names, phone numbers, addresses, photos or health details into an AI tool. Draft with placeholders such as [name]. Read the data settings of the tool you use. If you want to post a customer's photo, ask first, even when the customer is delighted.

6. Say so when a customer is talking to a machine

A chatbot should introduce itself as an automated assistant in its first line, and there must always be a way through to a real person. Customers accept talking to a machine. What they do not accept is having it hidden from them. The method is in our guide to the AI chatbot for customer messages.

7. Respect copyright and other people's likeness

Do not use AI to create images with a celebrity's face, another person's voice, characters, logos or music you have no right to use. Something made by AI is not automatically free of anyone's rights. Where you are unsure, ask a qualified professional.

The checklist before publishing

QuestionIf the answer is "no"
Has a person read the whole post?Do not publish yet
Do the prices, dates and times match the master document?Correct them from the document
Are the product and result photos real?Replace them with real ones
Is the post free of promises you are not sure you can keep?Remove the promise
Is it free of customers' names, photos or details used without permission?Remove them, or ask permission
Would you be willing to read this aloud in front of a regular customer?Rewrite it

The last question is the most useful. If you would feel awkward reading it aloud, customers will find it forced when they read it.

A one-page AI policy for the team

As soon as two or more people use AI, write one page. Five points are enough.

  1. Which tools may be used. Name them, and who manages the account.
  2. What they are used for. Drafting, rewriting, translating, summarising, making illustrations.
  3. What they are not used for. Answering complaints, quoting prices that are not on the list, writing reviews, creating product images.
  4. What data never goes in. Customers' personal details, internal information, contracts.
  5. Who approves, and who to tell when something goes wrong.

This page sits beside the voice document described in our guide on teaching AI your brand voice. One page says how AI writes, the other says what AI may and may not do.

When AI gets it wrong: handle it within an hour

  1. Remove or correct it at once. On every channel where it was published.
  2. Be honest with the people affected. Customers who saw the wrong price get the right one and an apology. Do not blame the machine. To the customer, it is your business speaking.
  3. Find the cause. It is usually one of two: the document was incomplete or out of date, or nobody approved.
  4. Add a rule. So that mistake does not happen again.

What the law says

Rules on advertising, consumer protection, personal data and disclosing the use of AI differ between countries and are changing quickly. This article is not legal advice. The seven rules above are a minimum standard of honesty, and they usually point the same way as the law. For exactly what applies where you operate, ask a qualified adviser. Business owners in Europe can start with the Your Europe business portal.

On the search engine side, the emphasis is that content must be useful to the reader, however it was written. See Google's guidance on helpful, reliable content.

Common mistakes

  • Trusting it because it reads smoothly. Fluent sentences do not mean correct facts.
  • Skipping approval when busy. The busy moment is exactly when mistakes happen.
  • Using AI images to "save a photo shoot". Customers arrive and see something different.
  • Pasting a customer's whole conversation into AI for a reply. Remove names and private details first.
  • Many users, nobody responsible.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to tell customers a post was written by AI?

For an ordinary post that you approved and take responsibility for, what matters is that it is correct and useful. For chatbots, and for images that could be mistaken for real ones, say so clearly. Rules differ by place, so where you are unsure, ask a qualified adviser.

Can I use AI-generated images in advertising?

Illustrations and decorative images are fine. Pictures of products, dishes, service results and before-and-after shots must be real, because customers will compare them with what they receive.

Is it safe to paste a customer's message into an AI assistant to draft a reply?

Remove the name, phone number and any private details first, and read the tool's data settings. Health information and identity documents should never go in.

Who should approve what AI writes?

Someone who knows the business and has the authority to decide. In a small shop that is usually the owner. In a chain, head office approves the voice and promotions, and branch managers confirm local facts.

What do I do when AI publishes the wrong price?

Correct it at once on every channel, give the right price and apologise to customers who saw it. Then find out whether the document was incomplete or nobody approved, and add a rule so it does not happen again.
