How to market a Vietnamese restaurant to the whole neighbourhood, not only the community

Start with what you are known for, make the menu easy for a first-timer, get right the basics people check on Google, and talk to your neighbours in their language. Here are the steps, with a 90-day plan.

By BunchasakeUpdated 11 Nov 20267 min read

Key takeaways
  • The hard part of marketing a Vietnamese restaurant abroad is reaching local diners who have never ordered the food, not the community that already knows it.
  • Being known for one thing, such as a signature dish, a region or a family recipe, is easier to remember than a menu of a hundred items.
  • A menu with short explanations and a line on how to eat the dish takes away the fear of ordering wrong.
  • Vietnamese keeps the community close and English or the local language speaks to the neighbours, so most restaurants need both.
  • Count new faces, repeat visits and what people say they came for, and you will know what is working.

How to market a Vietnamese restaurant abroad comes down to one idea: make it easy for someone who has never eaten bún chả to walk in, order with confidence and come back with a friend. The community already knows your food. The street does not, yet. This guide goes through the steps in order and ends with a 90-day plan. It assumes a family team with a few hours a week, not a big budget.

How to market a Vietnamese restaurant: the real challenge is local diners, not only the community

Many Vietnamese restaurants abroad open with the community behind them. Friends come, word travels through the temple, the church and the Asian grocery, and the first months feel busy. Then it levels off. The community is only so large, and many families cook the same dishes at home.

The growth is in the people who walk past every day: office workers, students, parents from the school down the road. They are curious, but they hesitate. They do not know what to order, how to say it or how to eat it. Your marketing has one job, which is to remove that hesitation.

Start with your story and what you are known for

Nobody remembers "a Vietnamese restaurant". They remember "the place with the charcoal bún chả" or "the family from Huế". Choose what you want to be known for and repeat it everywhere.

  • One signature dish. The one you would cook for a guest you want to impress. Put it first on the menu, in the window and in your photos.
  • A region. Hanoi, Huế, Saigon, the Mekong Delta. "Northern-style phở with a clear broth" tells a newcomer more than "authentic".
  • A family recipe. Whose it is and what has never changed. One true sentence is enough.

Write the story in three sentences and use the same words on the menu, the website and the Google listing. Turning that story into a name, a look and a voice that match is what Bunchasake does in its brand and marketing work for restaurants.

Make the menu readable to newcomers

"Bún chả Hà Nội" and a price is clear to you and a closed door to your neighbour. Keep the Vietnamese name, accents included, and add one plain line under it. A sound guide helps too, such as "fuh" for phở.

  • Phở bò tái: rice noodle soup in slow-simmered beef broth with thin slices of rare beef. Herbs and lime on the side.
  • Bún chả: grilled pork in a warm dipping sauce, with rice noodles and herbs. Dip the noodles into the bowl, a little at a time.
  • Bánh mì thịt nướng: crisp baguette with grilled pork, pâté, pickled carrot and daikon, coriander and chilli.
  • Cà phê sữa đá: strong drip coffee over sweetened condensed milk and ice. Stir before you drink.

Four more things help a first-timer:

  • A "New here?" box with three dishes to start with.
  • How to order and eat it: what the plate of herbs is for, which sauce goes with which dish, how big a portion is.
  • What is in the food: peanuts, shellfish, fish sauce, gluten, and which dishes can be made vegetarian. Allergen rules differ by country, so follow your local ones.
  • One sentence per dish for staff, so anyone at the counter can answer "What is that?" with a smile.

The basics customers check first

Before anyone visits, they look you up, usually on Google Maps. What they find there decides whether they come.

  • Google listing. Claim your free Google Business Profile and check the name, address, phone, category and menu link.
  • Hours. Correct for every day, holidays and Tết included. Wrong hours cost you a customer and often earn a bad review.
  • Photos. Upload your own: the front door so people recognise it, the room, your five best dishes in daylight, the menu.
  • Reviews. People read the recent ones and look at how you answered. Depending on your country, claim your page on Yelp for Business or Tripadvisor for owners too.

Then make sure the same name, address and hours appear on Instagram, Facebook, your website and any delivery apps.

Bilingual communication: two audiences, two languages

Vietnamese keeps the community close: the Tết menu, a new dish from home, a thank-you to the aunties and uncles who have come since the first day. English, or the local language where you are, speaks to everyone else: the sign, the menu explanations, the Google listing and most posts.

A simple rule: local language first, a short Vietnamese line second. Do not print a machine translation of the menu. Ask a native speaker to read it once. Our guide to bilingual marketing for Vietnamese businesses abroad goes deeper.

A simple social media routine

A Vietnamese restaurant social media strategy can fit on a sticky note. Choose one main platform, Instagram or Facebook depending on where your customers are, and keep this rhythm:

  • Three posts a week: one dish, one behind the scenes, one about the people or the story.
  • Stories on service days: today's special, tables still free.
  • Messages answered the same day.

The neighbourhood: how to attract customers to a Vietnamese restaurant

Most customers live or work a short walk or drive away. Go and meet them.

  • Offices. Take a printed lunch menu and a small tasting to nearby receptions. Offer one easy group deal: order by 11, ready at 12.
  • Schools and clubs. Support the school fair or the local football team with food or a voucher. Parents remember.
  • Events. A stall at the street festival with two dishes only, and a card with your address in every bag.
  • Partnerships. The café, the gym, the nail salon next door: swap flyers, share each other's posts, try one joint offer.
  • The window. A sign readable from across the street: what you serve, one photo, the lunch price.

Reviews and how to respond

Ask at the right moment, when a guest says they enjoyed the meal. A small card with a QR code at the till does the rest. Ask everyone the same way. Do not offer a discount or a free drink in exchange, which review platforms do not allow, and never write your own.

Then answer every review, briefly.

  • A good review: thank them by name and mention the dish. "Thank you, Anna. Glad the bún chả was a hit."
  • A bad review: wait an hour, then stay calm. Thank them, apologise for the specific problem, say what you changed and invite them to contact you directly. Do not argue in public. Your reply is really written for the next person reading.

What to measure, in plain terms

You do not need a dashboard. Keep a notebook by the till and write down three things each week.

  • New faces. Staff can simply ask, "Is this your first time here?"
  • Repeat visits. Who came back: regulars you know by name, the Friday lunch group.
  • What they came for. Ask "How did you hear about us?" and note the answer: Google, Instagram, a colleague, the sign.

After a month you will see which effort brings people in. Do more of that and drop the rest.

A 90-day plan

WhenFocusWhat to do
Days 1 to 30FoundationsWrite your three-sentence story and choose the signature dish. Rewrite the menu with short explanations. Fix the Google listing, hours and photos. Use the same details everywhere.
Days 31 to 60RoutineStart three posts a week and Stories on service days, in two languages where it fits. Put the review card at the till and answer every review. Teach staff one sentence per dish and the "first time here?" question.
Days 61 to 90NeighbourhoodVisit nearby offices with the lunch menu. Join one local event. Set up one partnership. Read the notebook and keep what brought new faces.

This is a working order, not a promise of results. Every street is different.

Common mistakes

  • "Authentic" as the whole message. Every restaurant says it. Say what is specific to you.
  • A hundred dishes and no guidance. A newcomer needs three good choices, not a hundred.
  • Paying for ads before the basics are right. Ads only send more people to a listing with the wrong hours.

Frequently asked questions

Should I change my recipes to suit local tastes?

That is your decision, but you rarely need to change the dish itself. Keep it as you would cook it at home and explain it well. Give choices at the table instead: chilli on the side, herbs on a separate plate, a milder option for children.

Do I need a website, or are Google and Instagram enough?

Start with the Google listing, because that is usually where people look first. A simple one-page website with the menu, hours, address and a way to book is still worth having, since it is the one place where you control everything.

How much should a small restaurant spend on marketing?

There is no right number. Most steps in this guide cost time, not money: the story, the menu, the listing, the reviews, the visits to neighbours. Do those first, and spend only once your notebook shows what is working.

How long does it take to see more customers?

Nobody can honestly promise a date. Correct hours, better photos and a readable sign can make a difference quickly, while a reputation in the neighbourhood builds over months. Keep counting new faces and repeat visits, and judge by that.
