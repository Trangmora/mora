A small business marketing checklist for Australia

You do not need a new campaign. You need to know which basics are missing. This checklist walks you through your own business the way a new customer meets it, from the phone screen to the front door.

By the Bunchasake team · Published 11 Nov 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways
  • Check your business in the order a customer meets it: the search result, the reviews, the words, the shopfront, then the community around you.
  • Mark every item as done, half done or not yet, and fix the half-done items first because they are the cheapest wins.
  • The seasons and school terms in Australia run differently from the northern half of the world, so plan your content around the calendar outside your door.
  • Signage, promotions, price display and customer messages all have local rules, so confirm them with a qualified adviser before you print or send.

This small business marketing checklist for Australia covers five areas an owner can check in one afternoon: the online listing, reviews, language, signage and community channels. It works the same for a nail salon, a restaurant, a bakery or a shop. Each item is a yes or no question. You will not find rules or numbers about Australia here, because those change and differ by state and council. You will find what to look at, in what order, and what to ask an adviser before you spend money.

How to use the checklist

Pick a quiet hour. Take your phone, stand across the road from your shop and pretend you have never been inside. Then go through the five areas below in order, because that is the order a new customer meets you: they search, they read reviews, they read your words, they arrive at the door, and later they hear about you from someone else.

Give each item one of three marks:

  • Done. It is correct today and someone is responsible for keeping it correct.
  • Half done. It exists but is out of date, incomplete or different from one place to another.
  • Not yet. It does not exist.

Fix the half-done items first. They cost the least and pay back the fastest.

1. The listing: can a stranger find you and trust what they see?

Picture a bánh mì bakery on a suburban shopping strip. A worker from the building site nearby searches "banh mi near me" at half past eleven. What appears decides where he eats. The same is true for someone searching "nails near me" on a Saturday morning. Check these points on your own business.

  • You control the listing. You have claimed your Google Business Profile with an email address the business owns, not a former employee's or a relative's personal account.
  • The name matches the sign. Same spelling, same order of words. If the sign says "Sen Nails and Beauty", the listing does not say "Sen Nail Spa".
  • The category is right. Choose the category that describes what most customers come for. The Google Business Profile Help Center explains how categories and other fields work, and the options can change, so look again once or twice a year.
  • Hours are true, including public holidays. Public holidays differ between states and territories. Before each one, set special hours so nobody drives to a closed door.
  • Other places agree. Your Facebook page, Instagram bio, website and any delivery or booking apps you use show the same name, address, phone and hours.

2. Reviews: are you asking, and are you answering?

  • You have a way to ask. A small card at the counter with a QR code to your review page, and one sentence staff are comfortable saying: "If you were happy today, a short review really helps a small shop like ours."
  • You ask everyone the same way. Do not ask only the customers you expect to be kind, and do not offer a discount or gift in return for a review. Platforms have rules about this and so may the law, so ask an adviser if you are unsure.
  • Every review gets a reply. Thank the good ones by name and mention what they had. For a poor one, stay calm, say sorry for the experience, state one fact if needed and invite them to contact you directly.

3. Language: do your words fit the people reading them?

Many owner-run businesses in Australia serve two groups at once: the wider local public and a community that shares the owner's first language. If many of your regulars speak Vietnamese, Cantonese, Greek or another language at home, both groups need to feel the business is talking to them.

  • Your English uses local words and spelling. Listen to how customers ask for things and copy that on your menu and posts. If your text was written by a cousin in another English-speaking country, read it aloud to a local regular and ask what sounds odd.
  • Original names keep their spelling and get a short description. "Bún bò Huế, spicy beef and lemongrass noodle soup." The name stays yours, and the newcomer knows what to order. The same goes for a named treatment on a salon price list.
  • You have chosen where your second language goes. Usually in community groups, in messages to customers who prefer it and on posts around festivals such as Lunar New Year. The shopfront and the listing lead with English.

For how to write for both groups without translating word for word, see our guide to bilingual marketing for small businesses.

4. Signage and shopfront: does the street know what you sell?

  • Readable from a passing car. The name and what you are, in a few large words. "Phở Mận, noodle house" or "Sen, nails and beauty" beats a full menu or price list printed on the window.
  • Sign and door agree with the web. The sign shows your current name, and the hours on the door match the hours online.

Before you change a sign, add a footpath board or light anything up, ask your landlord and your local council what is allowed. Rules differ from place to place, and a sign you have to take down is money lost twice.

5. Community channels: do the people near you know you exist?

Your language community

  • You know which Facebook groups, WhatsApp, WeChat or Zalo groups and community pages the people who share your language in your city actually read, and you have read their rules about business posts.
  • You show up at community moments such as Lunar New Year and Mid-Autumn events, with a stall, a sponsorship or simply your staff in uniform.
  • You have a simple way for these customers to message you, for example WhatsApp Business or the app your regulars already use. Do not guess. Ask ten customers which app they prefer.

The local neighbourhood

  • You know the schools, offices and sports clubs nearby, and you have one neighbour business to swap recommendations with.

Our guide on how to reach customers who share your language goes deeper into the community side.

One more check: the calendar is upside down

If you or your content helper learned marketing from pages in Asia, the United States or Europe, remember that the seasons in Australia are reversed. Summer falls over December and January, so Christmas and Lunar New Year arrive in hot weather, and winter comes in the middle of the year. A bubble tea shop that copies a "cosy winter drinks" post from an American account in December looks lost.

So plan your content around the local seasons, school terms and holidays in your state, and place your offers in the weeks you know are quiet.

What to confirm with an adviser

This guide deliberately does not state Australian law. Rules differ by state, territory and council, and they change. Before you act, ask an accountant, a solicitor, your industry association or your local council about:

  • registering and protecting your business name;
  • what your signs, footpath boards and window displays may show and where;
  • how prices must be displayed and what an advert may claim;
  • running competitions, giveaways and discounts;
  • sending marketing texts, emails or messages, and keeping customer details.

Common mistakes

  • Doing the fun part first. A new logo or a paid advert before the listing and hours are right sends people to a wrong first impression.
  • Copying a relative's marketing from another country. The words, the seasons and the rules are not the same. Borrow the idea, then check each detail locally.
  • Checking once. Hours, staff and menus change. Repeat the afternoon every three months.

Frequently asked questions

How long does this marketing checklist take?

The check itself takes about an afternoon for a single shop. The fixes take longer: do the five-minute ones the same day.

Should my posts be in English or in my community's language?

Lead with English where the public finds you, such as the listing, the shopfront and your main feed, and use your community's language where that community gathers and around cultural moments. The best guide is your own customer list: look at who comes in each week and write for them.

Which social platform should a small business in Australia use?

Use the one your customers already open, and you find that out by asking them at the counter. Start with one platform you can keep up every week, and add a second only when the first has become routine.

Can I offer a discount for a review?

Do not, unless a qualified adviser has confirmed it is allowed and the platform's own rules permit it. Asking every customer politely, with a QR code at the counter, is safer and builds reviews that sound real.
