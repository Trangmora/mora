Cross-border marketing, what Vietnamese companies must localize before going global

Localize in six layers before you launch abroad. Words, numbers and formats, images, proof, service and channels. Legal, customs, certification and tax are a seventh layer that belongs with qualified advisers.

By BunchasakeUpdated 11 Nov 20267 min read

Key takeaways
  • Cross-border marketing rarely fails on strategy. It fails on a misread price, a date in the wrong order or a slogan that means nothing in English.
  • Localize in six layers, in this order of thinking. Words, numbers and formats, images, proof, service, channels.
  • Translate manuals, localize product pages, and rewrite slogans and stories from the idea, not from the Vietnamese sentence.
  • Fix first what can cause a wrong order or a lost payment, then what blocks trust, then what only polishes.
  • Legal, customs, certification and tax are not marketing tasks. Ask qualified advisers in the target market.

Cross-border marketing rarely fails because of a big strategic error. It fails on small things: a price read as ten times higher because of a dot, a delivery date in the wrong order, a slogan that sounds proud in Vietnamese and empty in English. The fix is to localize in six layers before launch: words, numbers and formats, images, proof, service and channels.

This checklist covers marketing and communication only. Legal, customs, certification and tax questions are a separate layer, and they need qualified advisers in the market you are entering.

Translate, localize or rewrite

Not every text needs the same treatment. Sort your material into three piles before anyone starts working.

  • Translate. Texts where accuracy matters more than charm: specifications, manuals, policies. A careful translation, checked by someone who knows the product, is enough.
  • Localize. Texts that sell: product pages, packaging, emails, sales sheets. The meaning stays, but examples, measures, tone and order change to fit the reader.
  • Rewrite. Texts that carry feeling: the slogan, headlines, the brand story. Start again from the idea, in the new language.

Vietnamese slogan: "Uy tín tạo nên thương hiệu."

Translated: "Prestige creates the brand."

Rewritten from the idea: "We keep our word. Ask any customer."

The translated line is correct and says nothing. The rewritten one says what the owner meant.

What cross-border marketing must localize, in six layers

1. Words

  • Brand and product names. Can a native speaker say them, and do they carry an unwanted meaning? How do they look without Vietnamese marks?
  • The variety of the language. American or British English. Formal or informal address in languages that have both, such as German or Italian. Choose once and keep it.
  • A glossary. Twenty or thirty terms that every writer and translator must use the same way: product names, features, materials, job titles.
  • Vietnamese words you keep. Phở, áo dài and nước mắm can stay, with a few plain words of explanation the first time they appear.

2. Numbers and formats

This is the layer that costs money when it goes wrong.

  • Thousands and decimals. In Vietnam, 1.500 means one thousand five hundred. An American reads it as one and a half. Check every price, weight and quantity.
  • Currency. Show prices in the customer's currency, or say clearly which currency you mean.
  • Dates. 03/04 is March in one country and April in another. Write the month as a word.
  • Measures and sizes. Metric or not, and local clothing and shoe sizes, with a chart of real measurements.
  • Contact formats. Phone numbers with the country code, addresses in an order a foreign courier can read, office hours with a time zone.

3. Images

  • People and settings. Can the customer see themselves using the product? A dining table set for a Vietnamese family meal tells a different story from a small kitchen in Berlin.
  • Colours, symbols and numbers. Meanings travel badly. Ask a local person what your main image makes them think of.
  • The calendar. A Tết campaign needs explaining to people who do not celebrate it. Check which holidays and seasons matter to your customer, and remember that seasons are reversed in the southern hemisphere.
  • Text inside images. Banners and infographics with Vietnamese text baked in must be remade, not captioned.

4. Proof

What convinces a customer in Vietnam may mean nothing abroad. A national award needs one line saying what it is. Reviews should come from customers in that market as soon as you have them, real and unedited. Show certification marks only if you hold the certificate and an adviser has confirmed it is recognized there.

5. Sales and service

  • Payment. Offer the methods people in that market already trust. Ask local contacts which ones.
  • Delivery and returns. Cost, time and what happens when something goes wrong, written on the page before the customer asks.
  • Contact channel. Email is safe almost everywhere. Chat apps differ by country. Where customers use WhatsApp, set up WhatsApp Business instead of a personal number.
  • Reply hours and language. Say when a person answers, and in which language. Then keep to it.
  • Automatic messages. Order confirmations, invoices and error messages are often forgotten and stay in Vietnamese.

6. Channels and search

The social platform your customer uses, and the words they type into a search box, are both local. Do not guess the words. Ask five people in the market how they would search for a product like yours, and use their words in titles and descriptions. The basics of how a site is found are explained in Google's SEO Starter Guide.

The layer that is not marketing

Privacy notices, cookie banners, required label content, product claims, contracts, customs, certification and tax are not marketing decisions. They differ by product and by country, and a mistake there cannot be fixed with better copy. Use qualified advisers. Official starting points for reading are the Your Europe Business portal for the EU and the U.S. Small Business Administration for the United States.

The marketing job is to leave room for what the advisers require, and to present it clearly on the pack and the website.

What to do first when you cannot do everything

  1. Anything that can cause a wrong order or a lost payment. Prices, currency, sizes, measures, delivery information, checkout and order emails.
  2. Anything that blocks trust. Who you are, where you are, how to reach you, and proof that others have bought.
  3. Anything that polishes. The slogan, campaign images, the second social channel.

One market done fully beats five done halfway. The country guides for European markets and the US market show how the same checklist plays out in each place.

The five-person test

Before launch, find five native speakers in the target market who are close to your customer. Do not explain anything. Give them your page or pack and ask:

  1. Say the brand name aloud.
  2. In one sentence, what is this and who is it for?
  3. How much does it cost, and when would it arrive?
  4. What would stop you from buying?

Write down what they say, word for word. Fix the three problems that come up most often, then test again with new people.

Common mistakes

  • Publishing machine translation unread. A machine or an AI assistant is fine for a first draft. A native person must read it and decide.
  • Localizing the homepage only. The customer then meets Vietnamese at the checkout, in the confirmation email or on the invoice.
  • One "international" version for everyone. English with euro prices, American sizes and Vietnamese office hours fits nobody.
  • No master copy. When the product changes, three language versions drift apart. Keep one source document and update the others from it.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between translation and localization?

Translation changes the language. Localization also changes the examples, measures, formats, images and tone, so the text reads as if it was written for that customer in the first place.

Is English enough for every market?

English is usually enough to start conversations with trade buyers. People buying for themselves prefer their own language, so plan for it on packaging, product pages and customer service in each country you take seriously.

Can we use AI or machine translation?

Yes, for drafts and for internal understanding. Anything a customer sees should be read and corrected by a native speaker who knows the product, because a fluent sentence can still say the wrong thing.

How many markets should we localize for at once?

One. Finish the checklist for one market, learn from the first customers, and reuse what you learned for the second.
