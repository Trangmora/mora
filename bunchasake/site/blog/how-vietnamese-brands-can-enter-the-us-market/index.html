How to enter the US market: the brand and content side for growing brands

Do not try to enter America. Choose one door and one customer behind it, then prepare an American version of your brand for that customer. This guide covers brand and content only, not legal, customs, certification or tax.

By the Bunchasake team · Published 11 Nov 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways
  • The US shares one language but it is many markets. Choose one door first, whether a community that already knows your product, a niche of online buyers, or trade buyers.
  • American copy says what the product does for the buyer first, and where it comes from second.
  • Write in American English with American measures, and keep only the claims you can prove.
  • A buyer far away needs service promises in writing, covering delivery time, returns and who answers.
  • Legal, customs, certification and tax questions belong with qualified advisers, not with a brand guide.

The short answer to how to enter the US market as a growing brand or exporter: do not try to enter "America". Choose one door, one kind of customer behind it, and prepare an American version of your brand for that customer. That means plain American English, claims you can prove, service promises in writing, and proof from real buyers.

This guide covers the brand and content side only. Legal, customs, certification and tax questions are outside it. They depend on your product, your company structure and the state you sell in, and they need qualified advisers such as a US lawyer, a customs broker and an accountant.

How to enter the US market through one door

Because the whole country reads English, it is tempting to treat the US as one market and write one message for everybody. In practice a brand gets in through a door, and each door has a different customer standing behind it.

DoorFirst customerWhat they need to see first
A community that already knows the productFamilies who grew up with this kind of product, often people from your own home country, and the shops and restaurants that serve themA familiar name, their first language and English side by side, and where to buy it nearby
A niche of American buyers onlinePeople looking for one specific thing: specialty coffee drinkers, home cooks, someone furnishing a small apartmentA product page that explains the product to a person who has never seen it, honest reviews, clear shipping and returns
Trade buyersImporters, distributors, retailers, restaurant groups, brands looking for a manufacturerA company profile, specifications, samples, and emails answered within a working day

The door changes almost everything you write. Take a family fish sauce maker from Phan Thiết. For the first door, the bottle can stay close to what it is today, because the customer grew up with it. For the second door, the label and the product page have to explain what fish sauce is, how it tastes and which dish to try first. For the third, nobody reads the label yet. They read the specification sheet.

Pick the door where you already have something: a relative with a shop, a buyer who asked for samples, a few online customers who found you on their own.

Prepare an American version of your brand

Lead with the benefit, then the origin

A company profile written for the home market often starts with the founding year, the mission and the awards, and reaches the product on page three. American copy usually runs the other way: what this does for me, then who makes it.

Before: "Established with the mission of bringing our nation's finest products to the world, our company is proud to introduce our high-quality coffee line."

After: "Cold brew coffee bags from Đà Lạt. Drop one in water at night, pour smooth coffee in the morning."

American English, American measures

English written for a British or European reader looks slightly foreign in the US. On every page, label and sales sheet, check:

  • Spelling. "Color" and "flavor", not "colour" and "flavour".
  • Measures. Ounces and pounds, inches and feet, degrees Fahrenheit. For recipes, cups and spoons.
  • Prices and dates. Prices in US dollars. Dates with the month written as a word, so nobody misreads them.
  • Sizes. US clothing and shoe sizes differ from Asian and European ones. Give a size chart with real measurements instead of only S, M and L.

The wider list of what to adapt, for any country, is in our cross-border localization checklist.

Claims you can prove

Words such as "natural", "organic", "handmade", "eco-friendly" and "clinically tested" are promises, not decoration. The US has rules on advertising claims, endorsements and reviews, and the official place to read about them is the Federal Trade Commission's business guidance. Whether a particular claim is allowed for your product is a question for a lawyer.

The brand habit is simple. For each claim on your pack and website, write down the proof next to it. No proof? Cut the claim and say something specific instead: the ingredient, the method, the steps done by hand.

Service promises in writing

A buyer ordering from a company on the other side of the world has three worries: when it will arrive, what happens if it arrives broken, and who to talk to. Answer all three on the page, before they ask.

  • An honest delivery time, including the slow case.
  • A returns and refund policy in plain words.
  • One contact channel, with the hours when a person replies, in a US time zone.

Promise only what your operation can keep.

Proof from real buyers

Ask your first customers for honest reviews, and show them as they are. Never write your own, and never offer a gift for a positive review. For trade buyers, proof is a sample that arrives on time and a reference they are allowed to call.

Your name and your story in American mouths

Ask a few Americans who do not speak your language to read your brand name aloud. If they hesitate, help them. A short pronunciation note under the name, the way many phở restaurants write "say: fuh", costs nothing.

If the name uses accents, tone marks or another alphabet, decide once how it is written in plain letters, because search boxes, invoices and web addresses will write it that way. Keep the marks in the logo if you like, and use the plain version everywhere else.

Whether the name can be registered and protected in the US is a legal question for a US trade mark attorney. If you plan to protect it in several countries, the official place to read about the international route is the WIPO Madrid System.

For the story itself, the method is the same one we describe in our guide to taking your brand into European markets: a place smaller than a country, a person with a name, a method you can explain. One thing is particular to the US. If your family has members there who are part of the business, say so. A daughter in Houston who handles customer service is part of the brand, and a reason to trust it.

Where to show up first

  1. Your own website, with pages written for the US. The story, the policies and the contact details live here.
  2. One marketplace or one retailer. Each marketplace has its own rules, fees and listing format, and they change, so read the current terms before you commit.
  3. One social channel. The one your first customer already uses.
  4. Trade contacts. Trade fairs in your category, business associations from your home country in the US, and your country's trade promotion agency, such as VIETRADE.

If you are thinking about setting up a company in the US, the U.S. Small Business Administration is the official place to start reading. The decision itself belongs with a lawyer and an accountant.

Common mistakes

  • Translating the home-market brochure. The order, the tone and the proof are built for a different reader. Start from what the American customer needs to know.
  • "No. 1", "best quality", "prestigious". Big words without proof make a new brand look less serious, not more.
  • Pretending to be American. An eagle, a flag and an invented English founder name will not survive the first customer who looks you up.
  • Opening every door at once. A community grocery, an online shopper and an importer need three different messages. Do one well.

Frequently asked questions

Should we start with a community that already knows the product, or with mainstream buyers?

Start where you already have a contact or a customer. A community that grew up with the product is often the easier door for food and familiar products, but that message needs rewriting for buyers who have never seen the product.

Do we need a US company before we work on the brand?

Whether and when to set up a US company is a legal and tax question for qualified advisers. The brand work, such as the name test, the copy and the website, can start before that decision and will be needed either way.

Can we reuse the English website we made for Europe?

Use it as a starting point, not as it is. Change spelling, measures, prices, sizes and service information to the American versions.

Should we hide where the brand comes from?

No. Say it clearly, as part of a specific story with a place, a person and a method.

How is entering the US different from entering Europe?

On the brand side, Europe asks you to choose a country and a language, while the US asks you to choose a door and a customer inside one language. Rules, labels and taxes differ too, and belong with advisers in each market.
