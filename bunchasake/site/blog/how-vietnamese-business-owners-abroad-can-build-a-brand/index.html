How to market a Vietnamese business abroad, starting with the brand

Two shops with the same quality are not chosen equally. The one people choose has a story they can remember and repeat. Here is how an owner-run business builds that, step by step.

By the Bunchasake team · Published 11 Nov 2026 · 7 min read

If you are asking how to market a Vietnamese business abroad, start by building the brand. Decide what you stand for, who you serve and how the place should feel, then make every spot where a customer meets you say the same thing. Posts, offers and adverts work much harder once that is in place. Without it, you are sending people to a business they will not remember.

Having a business is not the same as having a brand

A business is what you sell, where, and at what price. A brand is what people remember about you, and what they say when a friend asks where to go.

Picture two nail salons in the same strip mall. Four technicians each, the same polish brands, almost the same prices, and the work is equally good. One is full every Saturday. The other waits for walk-ins.

The difference is rarely skill. The full salon has a name customers can say, a look they recognise from the car park, and a story they can repeat: "The owner used to be a nurse, so she is strict about clean tools." The other salon is "the nail place next to the laundromat".

Two phở restaurants of equal quality work the same way. One simply sells phở. The other tells you the broth is the owner's mother's recipe from Nam Định, and the sign, the menu and the staff all say so. People do not buy a product. They buy the story behind it.

Many Vietnamese owners abroad took over a shop, kept the old sign and competed on price. That works until a cheaper shop opens down the road. A brand is the part of your business a competitor cannot copy.

The four questions that define a brand

You do not need marketing vocabulary. You need honest answers to four questions, written in the language you think in, the way you would tell a friend.

1. Why did you start?

Give the real reason, not a slogan. "I could not find bánh cuốn like my mother's anywhere in this city." "I worked in other people's salons for years and wanted one where nobody is rushed."

2. Who is it for?

Describe one person, not "everyone". "Women from the offices nearby who want a quiet hour." "Families who want a big table and food that comes out fast." Once you know, decisions about price, hours and decoration get easier.

3. What makes you different?

It has to be true, it has to matter to that customer, and you must be able to show it. "Good quality and friendly service" does not count, because everyone says it. Use this test: could the shop next door put your sentence in their window? If yes, keep digging. "We make our own rice noodles every morning" passes.

4. How should it feel?

Choose three words. Calm, clean, unhurried. Or loud, generous, family-style. Both are fine, but they lead to very different shops, and mixing them confuses people.

Turning the answers into a name, a look and a voice

The name. A good name is easy for local customers to say, easy to spell after hearing it once, and still yours. A short Vietnamese word such as Sen, Mây or Lúa can do all three. If your name is long or hard to pronounce, add a plain description under it: "Mây, nails and spa". Before printing anything, check that no similar business nearby uses the name, and ask a qualified adviser about registering it.

The look. One or two colours, one or two typefaces and a simple logo, all chosen from your three words. Calm and unhurried does not look like bright red with five fonts. Test the logo small, as a profile picture, and large, on the sign.

The voice. This is how the business talks, in writing and in person. Most owners abroad need it in two languages, which we cover in our guide to bilingual marketing for Vietnamese businesses abroad.

The touchpoints that matter first

A touchpoint is any place a customer meets your business. For an owner-run shop abroad, five of them do most of the work. Fix them in this order.

  1. The sign. Your name and what you are, readable from across the street. If it still carries the previous owner's name, change this first.
  2. The menu or price list. The same colours and typefaces as the sign, clear groups, and what you are proudest of at the top.
  3. The Google listing. Claim your Google Business Profile and enter the name exactly as on the sign, the right category, correct hours and real photos. For many local customers this is the first thing they see. Our guide to Google Business Profile for nail salons shows the set-up.
  4. Instagram. On an Instagram business account, the logo as the profile picture, a bio that says what you are and where, and posts that look like they come from the same place as the sign.
  5. How staff greet people. It costs nothing and customers remember it longest. Agree on one greeting that fits your three words: "Hi, welcome to Mây. Is this your first time with us?"

Staying consistent

Brands are built by repetition. Customers need to meet the same name, colours and story many times before it sticks, and you will be bored of it long before they notice.

  • Put the rules on one page: logo, colours, typefaces, the three words, the greeting. Keep it at the counter and in the staff group chat.
  • Make two or three post templates and reuse them.
  • Choose one person who approves anything printed or published.

When to refresh instead of starting over

Refresh when the business is healthy and regulars know the name, but the look is dated or nothing matches. Refresh too when something real has changed: a new menu, a second location, children taking over from parents. Keep what customers recognise, usually the name and sometimes a colour, and tidy up the rest.

Start over when the name itself is the problem: customers cannot say it, it is confused with another business, it came with the shop along with old reviews, or the business is no longer what the name promises.

Common mistakes

  • Starting with the logo. A logo drawn before the four questions are answered is decoration.
  • Thinking "Vietnamese" is the difference. It is, until a second Vietnamese restaurant opens on your street. Say which region, whose recipe, what you do that others do not.
  • Paying for adverts before the basics are right. An advert that leads to a confusing sign and an empty Google listing buys a first impression you would rather not make.

Frequently asked questions

My shop is small. Do I really need a brand?

Yes, and a small shop has an advantage: the owner is there every day, so the story is real and easy to tell. A brand for a small business is simply a clear answer to why someone should choose you, repeated on the sign, on the menu and by the staff.

Should I keep a Vietnamese name or choose an English one?

Keep a Vietnamese name if it is short and local customers can say it after hearing it once. If it is long or often mispronounced, shorten it or add a plain description underneath, so the name stays yours and people still know what you sell.

I took over a shop with an existing name and sign. Should I change it?

Change it if the name carries poor reviews, is confused with another business or says nothing about what you now offer. If regulars know and like it, keep the name and refresh the look and the story around it instead.

Can I build a brand without a big budget?

Yes. The four questions, the worksheet and the staff greeting cost nothing. Spend first on what customers see most, usually the sign and the menu or price list, and do the rest one at a time.
