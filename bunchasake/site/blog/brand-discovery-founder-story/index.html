The brand discovery process starts with the founder's story

A brand discovery process begins by listening to the person who started the business: why they started, what they believe in, what worries them and what they really want. Answer the questions in this guide in writing today, and you will have the raw material every later step of a brand is built on.

By the Bunchasake team · Published 11 Nov 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways
  • A brand discovery process starts with the founder, because the reasons a business exists are the material every logo, post and sign later has to express.
  • The four things to uncover are why you started, what you believe in, what you hope and worry about, and what you really want from the business.
  • The most useful answers are specific memories and real customer moments, not adjectives like quality, friendly or professional.
  • Discovery ends with a short written summary the founder reads and approves, so nothing is designed on a guess.

The brand discovery process is the first step of building a brand, and it starts with one person: the founder. Before anyone talks about logos, colours or posts, someone has to listen to why you started the business, what you believe in, what keeps you awake at night and what you really want the business to become. Those answers are the raw material. Everything that comes later, from the name on the sign to the caption under a photo, is a way of retelling them.

This guide explains what happens in a discovery conversation, why it matters more than it looks, and how you can start today with a notebook and an hour of quiet. It is the first of six steps in the way we build brands; the second is brand positioning and competitor analysis.

Why a brand starts with the founder's story

People do not buy a product only for what it is. They buy the story behind it: who made it, why, and what that says about them when they choose it. Two nail salons on the same street may offer the same services at similar prices. The one a customer recommends to a friend is usually the one with something to say: "the owner trained in her mother's salon and is fussy about cuticles", or "they never rush you".

That something comes from the founder. In a small business, the owner's values decide how the phone is answered, which supplier is chosen and what happens when a customer is unhappy. A brand that ignores this ends up as decoration: a nice logo that promises one thing while the shop floor does another. A brand built from the founder's real story feels consistent, because it describes what is already true.

There is a practical reason too. Every later decision in a brand project needs a referee. Should the colours be calm or loud? Should the voice be playful or formal? Should you chase the lunch crowd or the dinner crowd? When the founder's beliefs and goals are written down, these questions have an answer you can check against, instead of a matter of taste.

What happens in a brand discovery conversation

At Bunchasake, step one is called Listening. It is a 60-minute conversation, online or in person, with the founder. We read whatever the founder has already sent us before the call and prepare questions, but the aim is not to fill in a form. It is to hear the story in the founder's own words, including the parts that do not sound like marketing.

The conversation usually moves through four areas:

  1. Why you started. The moment, the person or the frustration that made you open the doors.
  2. What you believe in. The rules you keep even when they cost you money.
  3. Your hopes and worries. Where you want the business to be, and what you are afraid could go wrong.
  4. What you really want. Not the polite answer, the true one: more time with your children, a second location, a business your nephew can take over, or simply to stop competing on price.

After the conversation, we write a short summary of what we heard and send it back. The founder reads it, corrects it and approves it before we move to the next step. That approval matters. It is the founder, not the studio, who decides whether the story has been understood.

Questions you can answer today

You do not need to wait for anyone to start your own brand discovery. Take a notebook, put your phone in another room and answer these questions in full sentences. Write the way you would speak to a friend. If an answer feels too personal, write it anyway; you can decide later what to share.

Why you started

  • What were you doing just before you opened this business? What made you leave it?
  • Was there a single moment when you decided? Describe the day, the place, who was with you.
  • What did you see other businesses in your trade doing that you wanted to do differently?

What you believe in

  • What is one thing you refuse to do, even if a competitor does it and earns more?
  • Tell the story of a time you lost money to do the right thing for a customer.
  • Which of your habits would surprise a new employee in their first week?

Hopes and worries

  • If the business goes well, what does an ordinary Tuesday look like three years from now?
  • What worries you most about the business right now? Be honest: rent, staff, a new competitor, your own energy.
  • What do you wish customers knew about your work that they never ask about?

What you really want

  • Is this business meant to grow, to stay small and excellent, or to be sold or passed on one day?
  • Which customers do you enjoy serving most? Describe one real person, without their name.
  • If you could only be known for one thing in your neighbourhood or market, what would it be?

How to turn your answers into a founder story

Once you have answers, read them back the next day and underline every sentence that contains a specific detail: a name, a place, a smell, a number of hours, a habit. Those details are your brand. Sentences full of general words like "quality", "passion" or "customer focus" can be crossed out; every competitor says them.

Then try to fill in this simple frame, which we use as a first draft and not as a final slogan:

I started [business] because [moment or frustration]. I believe [one belief], so we always [one habit customers can see]. The people we love serving are [real customer], and what I want is [true goal].

Here is how it might read for three different owners:

OwnerSpecific detail foundDraft founder story
Nail salon with four technicians in a strip mallOwner sterilises tools in front of every client, a habit from her first jobI opened this salon because I was tired of seeing tools reused without a thought. We clean everything where you can see it, and we take the time a careful manicure needs.
Family noodle restaurant with a lunch crowdBroth starts at five every morning, recipe from the founder's fatherMy father taught me that the broth decides everything. We start it before sunrise so office workers can have a proper bowl in a short lunch break.
Small furniture maker selling abroadEach piece is finished by the same two craftsmenWe started because good wood was being turned into cheap furniture. Every table is finished by two people who sign the underside.

None of these is a tagline yet. They are honest descriptions that the next steps, positioning, identity and the rest, will sharpen and retell. If you want to see how a story becomes a name, a menu and a sign, the restaurant branding guide follows that path for a family restaurant.

Common mistakes in brand discovery

  • Starting with the logo. A logo designed before the story is known is a guess. It may look good and still say the wrong thing.
  • Giving the polite answer. "We want to grow" is not a goal. "I want to stop working Sundays within two years" is, and it changes the whole plan.
  • Copying a competitor's story. If your answer could appear on any salon or restaurant website, it is not your story yet.
  • Hiding the worries. Fears about rent, staff or a new competitor are useful. They show what the brand must protect against, and they will come back in later steps.
  • Making promises you cannot prove. If you want to say "organic", "handmade" or "eco-friendly", make sure it is true and check the rules for your market. The FTC business guidance on advertising is a good place for owners in the US to read about honest claims, and other countries have similar rules.
  • Doing it alone in your head. Write it down. A story that only exists in your memory cannot guide a designer, a new employee or an AI assistant.

What you get at the end of step one

The result of a good discovery is not a document full of jargon. It is a short, approved summary of who the founder is, why the business exists, what it stands for and where the founder wants it to go. In our projects, nothing moves to positioning until the founder has read that summary and said yes.

If you are writing a business plan at the same time, your answers will also help there; the U.S. Small Business Administration has official guides for planning a small business, and many countries offer similar free resources. Frequently asked questions

What is a brand discovery process?

It is the first stage of building a brand, where you uncover why the business exists, what the founder believes, who it serves and what the founder wants it to become. Design and marketing come after it, not before.

How long does the discovery conversation take?

With us, step one is a 60-minute conversation, online or in person. Before the call we read what you have sent and prepare questions, and after it we send a written summary for you to approve.

What should I prepare before the conversation?

Nothing formal. It helps to answer the questions in this guide in writing, and to bring examples of customers you love serving, photos of your business and anything you already use, such as a menu, a price list or your social media pages.

I am not a good storyteller. Does that matter?

No. The founder's job is to answer honestly, in their own words and in the language they are most comfortable in. Turning those answers into a clear brand story is the work of the next steps.

Do I need discovery if my business already has a logo?

Yes, if the brand no longer feels like you or customers seem confused about what you offer. Discovery shows whether the current identity still fits the story, so you can decide what to keep and what to refresh.
