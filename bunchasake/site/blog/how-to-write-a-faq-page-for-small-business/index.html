How to write an FAQ page for a small business: one set for your website, chatbot and AI

A good FAQ page is about thirty questions customers really asked, each with a short, correct answer that includes a number. Write it once and you can use it in four places: your website, your message replies, your chatbot, and as the source that helps Google and AI assistants understand you.

By the Bunchasake team · Published 26 Nov 2026 · 6 min read

Key takeaways
  • A good FAQ page has about thirty questions customers really asked, taken from the inbox, comments and the counter, not questions you made up.
  • A good answer responds in its first sentence, covers one point, includes a specific number and is no longer than three sentences.
  • The six groups to cover are basic facts, services, prices and payment, booking, policies, and safety concerns.
  • The same set serves the website, saved replies in the inbox, the chatbot's knowledge document, and is the kind of content AI assistants quote easily.
  • When a price or opening hour changes, update the FAQ the same day, because an old wrong answer is worse than no answer.

How to write an FAQ page for a small business fits in one sentence: copy down about thirty questions customers really asked, then answer each one briefly, correctly and with a number. Most FAQ pages are weak because they do the opposite: the owner invents the questions and answers them with advertising copy. This guide shows where to find the questions, the six groups to cover, how to write an answer, a thirty-question template to fill in, and how to use the same set in four places.

One FAQ set, used in four places

  • The website. Customers find the answer themselves at midnight, without messaging you.
  • The inbox. Each answer becomes a saved reply, so every member of staff answers the same way. See our guide to social media inbox automation.
  • The chatbot. The FAQ set is the core of its knowledge document, as our guide to the AI chatbot for customer messages explains.
  • Search and AI assistants. A clear question with a direct answer is the kind of content that gets quoted most easily. It is point three in our guide to what GEO is.

So it is worth writing properly once, and keeping as your single source.

Where to find the questions

  1. The last three months of your inbox. Copy the customers' exact words, abbreviations included.
  2. Comments under your posts. "How much?", "Do you deliver?".
  3. The counter and the phone. Ask staff to write down every question for one week.
  4. Customer reviews. What people praise or criticise is often what the next person will ask.
  5. Search terms. If you have a website, look in Google Search Console for question-style phrases that brought people to your pages.

Do not invent the questions. "Why choose us?" is not something customers ask. "Is there parking?" is.

The 6 groups of questions on an FAQ page

GroupWhat the customer wants to knowExample
Basic factsHow to reach you"What are your opening hours, and are you open on Sunday?"
Services, productsWhether you do what they need"What is the difference between gel and dip?"
Prices and paymentHow much, and how to pay"How much is a gel manicure?"
Booking, orderingWhat they must do to be served"Do you take walk-ins?"
PoliciesWhat happens when plans change"Do I lose my deposit if I cancel?"
Safety concernsWhether there is anything to worry about"Are your tools sterilised?"

For the last group, answer with facts about how you work and give no medical advice. For questions about allergies or conditions, invite the customer to talk to you directly and to ask a qualified professional.

How to write an answer

  • Answer in the first sentence. Yes, no, or the number. The explanation comes after.
  • One point per question. A two-part question becomes two questions.
  • Be specific. "From 25 dollars", "about 45 minutes", "24 hours ahead". Not "fair prices" or "quick service".
  • Use the customer's words. If customers say "gel polish", write "gel polish".
  • Three sentences at most. If it needs more, link to a separate page.
  • Promise nothing you are not sure of.

A 30-question template to fill in

GroupFive questions to cover
Basic factsAddress and how to find you. Opening hours and closed days. Parking. The fastest way to reach you. Languages spoken.
Services, productsWhat you do. What you do not do. How two often-confused services differ. How long each takes. Whether you take large groups.
Prices and paymentPrices from. When there is a surcharge. Payment methods. Whether a deposit is needed. Gift vouchers.
Booking, orderingHow to book. How far ahead. Walk-ins. Arriving late. Changing an appointment.
PoliciesCancellation. Refunds and returns. Guarantee or fixes. Children and pets. Delivery.
ConcernsHygiene and sterilisation. Materials and ingredients. Who it suits and who it does not. What to do if you are not happy. How customer information is kept.

You do not need all thirty at once. Fifteen correct, specific answers are better than thirty answered for the sake of it.

Where to put the FAQ on your website

  • A page of its own. Name it clearly, such as "Questions and answers", with a link in the footer.
  • Three to five questions on each service page. Put the questions about that service right there.
  • Each question is a heading. Real text, not a screenshot or a PDF. Search engines and AI assistants read text.
  • Show the date it was updated.

One thing to know so your expectations are right: since 2023, Google shows expanded FAQ results only for a small number of government and health websites, according to Google's announcement. So the reason to write an FAQ page is not a special box on Google. It is that customers can read it, a chatbot can use it, and AI assistants have a clear answer to quote. The other foundations for a website are in Google's SEO Starter Guide.

Keeping the FAQ correct

  • One single source. One master document. The website, inbox and chatbot all copy from it.
  • Change it the same day. Prices, hours, policies.
  • Add new questions every month. Take them from what the chatbot could not answer and from new questions in the inbox.
  • Reread the whole page every quarter. Remove questions nobody asks any more.

In a chain, each branch may have different hours and services, so say clearly which site an answer applies to, as our guide to marketing for multi-location chains describes.

Common mistakes

  • Self-praising questions. Nobody asks "What makes us different?".
  • "Please contact us" for everything. Customers open the FAQ precisely so they do not have to contact you.
  • Roundabout answers. Three sentences of introduction before the point.
  • Copying someone else's. Their policies are not yours.
  • Writing it and leaving it. Old prices and old hours are what lose customers.

Frequently asked questions

How many questions should an FAQ page have?

About fifteen to thirty is enough for a small business. What matters is that customers really ask them, and that each has a specific answer.

Should prices be on the FAQ page?

Yes, at least a starting price or a range. Price is what customers ask about most, and "contact us for prices" usually sends them away.

Does an FAQ page help a website rank on Google?

It helps the page answer exactly what customers search for, which is the base of SEO. But do not expect a special FAQ box on Google, because since 2023 Google shows that format only for a small number of government and health websites.

Should questions about complaints and refunds be included?

Yes. Stating your policy and how to reach you when something is wrong reassures customers. If you hide it they will still ask, only when they are already upset.

Can I use AI to write the FAQ page?

Yes, for organising and tightening the wording. The questions must come from real customers, and every fact such as prices, hours and policies must be supplied and checked by you.
