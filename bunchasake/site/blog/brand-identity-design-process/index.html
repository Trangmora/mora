The brand identity design process: how a logo, colours, type and voice get agreed and written down

The brand identity design process turns an agreed position and story into things people can see and hear: a logo, colours, typefaces and a voice. Each part is decided together with the founder, then written into brand guidelines so the brand stays the same for years.

By the Bunchasake team · Published 11 Nov 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways
  • Identity comes third, after listening and positioning, because a logo designed before anyone knows who the brand is for is a guess.
  • A brand identity has four parts that must agree with each other: logo, colours, typefaces and voice.
  • Every identity decision should be explained by the position and the story, so the founder can approve it for a reason, not only because it looks nice.
  • Brand guidelines are the part that lasts, because they let a new staff member, printer or designer use the brand correctly without asking.
  • Test the identity where it is hardest to read, such as a small profile picture, a one-colour stamp or a sign seen from across the street.

The brand identity design process is the step where a brand stops being an idea and becomes something people can recognise: a logo, a set of colours, typefaces and a way of speaking. It works best when it comes after two earlier decisions, who the brand is for and what story it tells, and when the founder agrees each part before the next one starts. At the end, everything is packed into brand guidelines, so the identity can be used the same way for years, by anyone. This article explains what happens in this step, why it matters for a small business and how we run it.

Where identity sits in the six steps

Identity is the third of six steps. Before it, we listen to the founder's story and then work out the brand's position: who it is for, how it differs from the competitors around it and which direction it should take. The brand story is written at the end of positioning. Identity takes that story and gives it a face and a voice. After identity comes reach, where the brand goes out to the channels customers already use, which we explain in how to build an omnichannel brand presence.

The order matters. A logo drawn before the position is clear is a guess based on taste. It may be pretty, but nobody can say whether it is right, because there is nothing to measure it against. When the position and story already exist, every design choice can be checked with one question: does this say what we agreed the brand is?

Who is in the room

We work directly with the founder and with the person in charge of the brand day to day, if that is someone else: a manager at a restaurant, the owner's son or daughter who runs the Instagram, the partner who handles the shop. The founder knows why the business exists. The person in charge knows how the brand is actually used: what gets printed, what gets posted, what customers ask. Both views are needed, and the guidelines will be handed to that second person, so they should help shape them.

The client approves at the end of the step, as with every step. But approval is not a single yes at the end. Each part of the identity is agreed on its own, in order, so that nobody is asked to judge a finished package they had no say in.

The four parts, agreed one by one

1. Logo

A small business logo has a hard job. It must work as a round profile picture on a phone, on a shop sign, on a paper bag, on a receipt and sometimes stamped or embroidered in one colour. We usually start from what the position needs the logo to say, then show directions with the reasoning written next to each one, so the founder can choose for a reason. A good test: shrink it to the size of a profile picture and look at it from arm's length. If you cannot read it, customers scrolling past will not either.

Before a name or logo is used widely, it is worth checking that nobody else has registered something similar. You can search the EU Intellectual Property Office or read about international registration through the WIPO Madrid System. Whether and where to register is a legal question for a qualified adviser.

2. Colours

Most small brands need fewer colours than they think: one main colour, one or two supporting colours and a neutral for text and backgrounds. The colours should come from the story. A family restaurant built on a grandmother's recipes may want warm, homely tones; a modern nail studio that sells calm may want soft, quiet ones. We agree what each colour is for, not only which colours they are, so the team knows which one goes on a button and which one never touches a price list.

3. Typefaces

Usually two: one with character for headings and one that is easy to read for everything else. We check that both work in every language the business uses, including accents and special characters, and that they are available where the team will actually type: in a design app, on the website, in the menu file.

4. Voice

Voice is how the brand talks: warm or formal, playful or calm, short or detailed. It is the part owners most often skip, and the part customers notice every day, in captions, replies to reviews, the sign on the door. We write the voice as a few plain rules with examples, such as "We say 'see you Friday', not 'we look forward to your visit'." If the business speaks to customers in two languages, the voice is written for both, so the brand sounds like the same person in each.

What goes into brand guidelines

Guidelines are where the agreed identity is packed for the long term. They do not need to be a thick book. For a small business they should be short enough that a new staff member actually reads them. Here is what we put in, as a checklist you can use for your own:

SectionWhat it containsWho uses it
The brand in one pageWho the brand is for, how it differs, the short version of the storyEveryone, especially new staff
LogoMain version, simple version, one-colour version, minimum size, clear space, what not to doPrinters, sign makers, designers
ColoursEach colour with its codes for screen and print, and what it is used forAnyone making a post, menu or flyer
TypefacesHeading and body fonts, sizes, where to get them, a fallback fontWhoever edits the website or menu
VoiceThree to five rules, words to use and avoid, example captions and repliesWhoever writes posts and answers messages
Examples in useA post, a story, a business card, a sign or bag, shown correctlyEveryone, as a reference
FilesWhere the logo and templates are stored and which file to use whereThe person in charge of the brand

The test of good guidelines is simple: could a printer you have never met make your business card correctly without calling you? If yes, the guidelines work.

How decisions get agreed with the founder

Founders often worry about two things: that they will be shown something they hate and pushed to accept it, or that they will have to make design decisions they do not feel qualified to make. A good process avoids both.

  1. Start from the agreed story. Every presentation opens with the position and story the founder already approved, so the conversation is about fit, not taste.
  2. One part at a time. Logo first, then colours, then type, then voice. Each builds on the one before.
  3. Reasons next to every option. "This direction feels like a neighbourhood place; this one feels more premium." The founder chooses between meanings, not just shapes.
  4. Show it in real use. A logo alone on white looks different from a logo on a shop sign or a phone screen. Seeing it in place makes the decision easier.
  5. Write down what was agreed. After each part, the decision goes into the guidelines draft, so nobody has to remember it later.
  6. Approve the whole step. At the end, the founder sees the complete identity and guidelines together and signs off before we move to reach.

AI tools help with the fast work, such as producing variations or checking how a logo looks at many sizes. The decisions are made by people: the founder, the person in charge and us.

Common mistakes

  • Starting with the logo. Without a position, there is no way to know if the logo is right.
  • Too many colours and fonts. Every extra choice is one more way for the brand to look different on every post.
  • Forgetting voice. A brand that looks consistent but sounds different in every caption still feels like several businesses.
  • Guidelines nobody opens. If they are too long or stored where the team cannot find them, they do not exist.
  • Testing only on a big screen. Most customers will meet your logo small, on a phone.

Frequently asked questions

Can I keep my existing logo?

Often, yes. If the logo still fits the brand's position, we can keep it and build the colours, type, voice and guidelines around it, or refresh it lightly so it works better at small sizes.

Do I need to know about design to approve each part?

No. Each option is explained in terms of the story and the customers you agreed on, so you are choosing what the brand should say, not judging design technique.

Who should take part besides the founder?

The person who will use the brand day to day, such as a manager or whoever runs your social media. They will follow the guidelines, so they should help shape them.

What do I receive at the end of this step?

The agreed logo files, colours, typefaces and voice rules, packed into brand guidelines with examples of the brand in use, ready for the reach step.

Does the identity need to work in two languages?

If you speak to customers in more than one language, yes. Typefaces should support every language's characters and the voice should be written for each, so the brand sounds like one person.
