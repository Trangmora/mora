Marketing for multi-location chains: keep one brand voice and give every branch its own content

Marketing for a multi-location chain is a two-layer problem: the brand layer speaks with one voice everywhere, and the branch layer tells the story of its own neighbourhood. AI helps with the second layer without a marketing team at every site.

By the Bunchasake team · Published 26 Nov 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways
  • Marketing for a multi-location chain has two layers: the brand layer keeps one voice and one shared set of facts, and the branch layer talks about each neighbourhood.
  • Prices, opening hours and promotions must live in a single table that every channel, every branch and the AI all read from.
  • Each branch needs a short profile with its address, hours, unique features, the character of the area and real photos, so AI writes for that place instead of just swapping the name.
  • Head office approves the voice and promotions, branch managers confirm local facts, and complaints are always answered by a real person at the branch.
  • Open a separate page for a branch only when someone there answers messages and there is enough local news to post every week.

Marketing for a multi-location chain is a two-layer problem. The brand layer speaks with one voice everywhere. The branch layer tells the story of its own neighbourhood: which corner it is on, what is on today, who is behind the counter. With one shop, the owner does both. At three, five or ten sites nobody can keep up, and the two layers start to drift apart. This guide shows how to organise those two layers, and where AI can carry the load so you do not need a marketing team at every site.

Three ways things break as a chain grows

  • Every branch posts in its own way. Good photos at one, rushed snapshots at another, a different tone at each. Customers stop seeing one brand.
  • The main page posts regularly and the branches are silent. Everything is general, and nobody knows what the branch near them offers.
  • The facts disagree. The main page announces a promotion that a branch does not honour. Google says open until 10pm and the door closes at 9pm. This is the break that loses customers fastest.

All three share one root: the chain has grown, but the method is still the method of a single shop.

What it talks aboutThe story, shared products, campaigns, valuesThe place, the people, items or services found only there, neighbourhood news
Who decidesHead officeThe branch manager, within the frame head office sets
What keeps it consistentThe brand voice document and the visual identityThe branch profile and approved templates

The brand layer is built once and then maintained. How to write the voice document is in our guide on teaching AI your brand voice. The rest of this article is about the branch layer, which takes the most effort. To see how real chains kept one voice across many sites, read the stories of Pizza 4P's and Cong Ca Phe.

One shared page, or a page for every branch

ChannelSuggestion for a small chain
Google Business ProfileOne profile per location, because customers look up directions site by site. Each site's name, hours and phone number must be its own.
FacebookStart with one shared page. Some platforms offer a way to manage several locations under a main page; the features and conditions change over time.
Instagram, TikTokOne account for the brand, with the branch location tagged on each post. Split only when a site has enough stories of its own.
WebsiteOne page per branch: address, hours, map, real photos, what is unique there.

One rule to decide by: open a separate page for a branch only when someone there answers messages, and there is enough local news to post at least weekly. An abandoned branch page does more harm than having none. You create and manage each site's profile at Google Business Profile, and you will find pages and permissions in Meta's business tools.

The branch profile: what AI needs to write for each site

Give AI only the branch name and it writes the same post with the name swapped. For a post to truly belong to that site, each branch needs a half-page profile.

  • Hard facts. Address, opening hours, phone number, parking, the way in.
  • People. The manager's name and a few faces customers often meet, if they agree to appear.
  • What is unique. A dish, a service or a corner found only here.
  • The area. Near offices, near a school, residential, tourists. When it is busy.
  • Real photos. The front, the inside, the team. Retake them every quarter.
  • The customers' language. If a site's customers speak another language, say so.

The branch manager is the best person to write this profile. It takes about half an hour, and only needs editing when something changes.

One master post, a version for each branch

Take a three-site restaurant chain launching a new dish this month. Head office approves one master post. AI uses each branch profile to write three versions.

BranchFrom the profileAngle of the version
The site near officesBusy at lunch, customers in a hurryThe new dish is in the lunch set, and how fast it arrives
The site near a schoolYoung customers, in groupsSharing portions, the big table
The residential siteFamilies, evenings and weekendsDinner for the whole family, parking available

Three posts with the same voice, the same price and the same photo of the dish. What differs is the reason to visit that particular site. Each manager rereads the facts about their own site before it is published. How to write one idea for several channels is in our guide to multichannel posting from one plan.

The approval flow for a chain

Type of contentWho draftsWho approves
Brand posts, campaignsAI, following the voice documentHead office
Branch postsAI, from the branch profile and photos the manager sendsThe manager confirms the facts, head office approves the voice
Prices, promotionsTaken only from the shared fact tableHead office
Replies to complaintsA real person at the branchHead office is informed

Once the chain is used to this, head office no longer needs to approve every branch post. It approves templates and spot-checks a few posts each week.

One source of truth for prices, hours and promotions

This is the first thing worth doing. Create a single table with one row per branch: opening hours, closed days, the price list in force, which promotion applies where, and from which date to which date.

Everything else reads from this table: posts, the chatbot that answers messages, the Google profiles, the website. When a price changes, you change it in one place. Nobody, including the AI, may use a number that is not in the table.

Messages and reviews for each branch

  • Ask which site the customer means. The chatbot's first question should be "Which branch are you asking about?", and it then answers from that branch's row in the fact table. See our guide to the AI chatbot for customer messages.
  • Hand over to the right person. Questions about bookings and complaints go straight to that site's manager, not through head office.
  • Google reviews. Each site has its own reviews. Reply in the same voice, but the person who knows what happened is that site's manager.

Measure branch by branch

Figures for the whole chain hide the site that is struggling. Each week, look at every site separately: the number of messages, the number of direction requests and calls from its Google profile, and which of its posts were saved and shared. Compare each site with itself last month, not the city-centre site with the one that just opened. How to read these numbers is in our guide to 5 social media metrics to check weekly.

Common mistakes

  • Opening a separate page for every branch from day one. Six months later half of them are abandoned.
  • Swapping the branch name in the same post and publishing. Customers see at once that it is a copy.
  • Promotions that do not say where they apply. Customers arrive at a site that does not honour it, and leave annoyed.
  • Handing everything to branch managers with no frame. Each one writes in a different voice.
  • Head office answering complaints for the branch. Slow, and without the facts on the ground.

Frequently asked questions

At how many branches should a chain start making content for each site?

From the second site you should already have a branch profile and at least a few local posts each month. Customers choose the site nearest to them, so they need to know what that site offers.

Should we use one shared Facebook page or one per branch?

Start with one shared page. Open a separate page only for a branch that has someone answering messages and enough local news to post weekly. Google Business Profile is different: each location needs its own profile.

Who should approve a branch's posts?

The branch manager confirms local facts such as hours, dishes and photos. Head office approves the voice and anything about prices and promotions. Once the routine is established, head office only needs to approve templates and spot-check.

Does AI know what is specific to each branch?

No, unless someone tells it. That is why each branch needs a short profile and real photos sent in by its manager. The AI writes from those.

What is different for a franchise?

Who may post what, and under which name, is set by the franchise agreement. The two-layer model still works, but review the agreement and ask a qualified adviser before assigning permissions.
