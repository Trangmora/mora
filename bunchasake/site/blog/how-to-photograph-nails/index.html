How to take nail photos with a phone that look like your real work

Stand by a window, prepare the hand, use one plain background and take the same five shots of every set. Good nail photos come from a routine, not from an expensive camera.

By the Bunchasake team · Published 11 Nov 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways
  • Soft daylight from a window at the side does more for a nail photo than any phone upgrade or filter.
  • Thirty seconds of hand preparation, cuticle oil, a lint-free wipe and a relaxed pose, decides whether the photo looks professional.
  • One background and one photo spot used for every set make a salon's page recognisable before anyone reads the name.
  • Edit for accuracy, not for beauty: the polish colour in the photo should match what the client sees on her own hand.
  • A six-second clip of the hand turning slowly in the light shows chrome, cat-eye and glitter better than any still photo.

If you want to know how to take nail photos with a phone, the short answer is this: stand next to a window, wipe the lens, prepare the hand, put it on one plain background, and take the same few shots every time. The phone in your apron pocket is good enough. What is usually missing is a routine that takes two minutes and that every technician in the salon can repeat.

This guide gives you that routine. It is a do-it-yourself guide: at Bunchasake we build brands and content systems, and we do not do photo shoots, so everything below is meant for you and your team to do at the nail table.

Set up the phone once

  • Wipe the lens before every photo. Dust, acetone haze and hand cream on the lens are the most common reason salon photos look foggy.
  • Use the back camera at its normal 1x setting, or 2x if your phone has it. The wide-angle lens stretches fingers and makes nails look short.
  • Turn the flash off. Flash flattens the shape and puts a hard white dot on every nail.
  • Tap and hold on a nail to lock the focus, then slide your finger to make the picture a little brighter or darker. The exact gesture differs between phones, so try it on yours.
  • Switch off beauty modes and built-in filters. They blur skin and shift colours.

Light: the part that decides most of the result

Nails are small, shiny and curved. That makes them mirrors. Whatever light is above them will show up on them, so choosing the light is most of the job.

  • Window light from the side. Put a small table one or two steps from a window. Light should come from the left or right of the hand, not from behind the phone. If the sun shines straight in, hang a thin white curtain or move back until the shadows turn soft.
  • Fill the dark side. Stand a white card, a menu turned over or a white towel on the side away from the window. It bounces light back and softens the shadow between fingers.
  • Turn off the lamp above. Ceiling lights and the table lamp mix with daylight and turn skin yellow or grey. One kind of light at a time.
  • In the evening, use one lamp with a white, daylight-type bulb and soften it with baking paper or a thin white cloth held a safe distance from the bulb. Photograph from the same spot every time so the colour stays consistent.

Different finishes need small adjustments:

  • Glossy gel: turn the hand slowly until the highlight becomes one clean line along the nail instead of a blob in the middle.
  • Chrome and cat-eye: these need a small, direct light to come alive. A single lamp or a phone torch held by a colleague, moved slowly, shows the effect.

Prepare the hand in thirty seconds

Do this after the top coat is cured and before the client stands up:

  1. Brush away dust and wipe each nail with a lint-free pad.
  2. Add a small drop of cuticle oil and rub it in fully. Wipe off extra oil, or the skin will shine more than the nails.
  3. Ask the client to shake her hand loose and breathe out. Tense fingers show in the photo.

Five hand poses that flatter nails

  • The soft claw. Fingers curled gently towards the palm, thumb tucked beside the index finger. Shows all five nails and the shape.
  • The resting hand. Hand flat on a rolled towel with fingers slightly apart and relaxed. Good for natural nails and short sets.
  • Holding something. A polish bottle, a small cup, the edge of a sleeve. Give the hand a job and it relaxes.
  • Two hands stacked. One hand resting over the other. Shows a full set in one frame.
  • The side profile. One finger shown from the side at the level of the nail. This is the photo that proves a clean apex and a neat free edge on extensions.

Background and props

Choose one background and keep it at the photo spot: a sheet of matte card, a piece of tile, a plain towel or a small board in a neutral tone. Pick a colour close to your salon's own colours so the photos match the menu and the sign. Our guide to nail salon logo and branding explains how to choose those colours.

  • Use one prop at most: the polish bottle, a flower, a ring. The nails are the subject.
  • Check the edges of the frame for the nail lamp cable, used cotton, a coffee cup or another client's bag.

Angles and framing

Three angles cover almost everything. From directly above for the whole set. From about forty-five degrees for shape and shine. From the side at nail level for structure. Hold the phone with both hands, rest your elbows on the table, and take three or four frames of each shot. Choose the sharpest one later, when the client has left.

Simple editing on the phone

The editor built into your phone's photo app is enough. Work in this order and stop early:

  1. Crop and straighten. Vertical shapes fill more of the screen on Instagram and TikTok. Preferred sizes change from time to time, so check the app or the Instagram Help Center when a post looks cut off.
  2. Brightness. Lift it slightly if the photo is dark. Keep detail in the highlights on the nail.
  3. Warmth. If skin looks yellow or blue, move the warmth slider until the background looks like it does in real life.

The rule for editing is honesty. Do not change the polish colour, reshape nails or smooth skin until it looks like plastic. A client who books "that exact red" and gets a different red will tell you, and sometimes she will tell Google. Compare the edited photo with the real hand or the bottle before posting. Skip heavy filters and large watermarks; a small logo in one corner is enough if you want one.

Short video: six seconds is enough

  • The slow turn. Phone still, hand rotating slowly from side to side under the light. Best for gloss and chrome.
  • The light sweep. Hand still, a colleague moves a small light across the nails. Made for cat-eye.

Film upright, keep your elbows on the table, and record a little longer than you need so you can trim the start and end. For ideas on what to do with these clips, see nail salon Instagram content ideas and nail salon TikTok content ideas.

Ask first, then save and reuse

Ask every client before you photograph, and say where the photo may appear: "May I take a photo of your nails for our Instagram and Google page?" If the answer is no, respect it without discussion. Keep rings, tattoos and faces out of the frame unless the client is happy to show them. Rules about images and privacy differ by country, so if you are unsure, ask a qualified adviser.

Then make the photos easy to find. Create one shared album for the salon, and add each set on the same day with the technician's name and the style in the caption. Once a week, pick the best few and add them to your Google Business Profile, where people searching nearby see them before they ever open Instagram. A consistent background, colour and logo mark are what turn good photos into a recognisable page. That part is brand work, and it is what we do: see brand and marketing for nail salons.

Common mistakes

  • Photographing under the nail lamp or ceiling light. Colours shift and every nail gets a harsh reflection.
  • A different background each day. The page looks like it belongs to ten different salons.
  • Only photographing the dramatic sets. Most clients book short, simple nails. Show those too, done well.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a new phone or a special camera?

No. Any reasonably recent phone takes clear nail photos in good light. A clean lens, a window and a steady hand improve the result far more than a newer model.

Should I buy a ring light or a light box?

Start with a window and a white card, which cost nothing. If your salon has no daylight or you work late, one lamp with a daylight-type bulb, softened with a white cloth, is a sensible first purchase. A ring light is handy for video but leaves a visible ring on glossy nails.

How do I photograph chrome or cat-eye nails?

Use a small, direct light and movement. Ask a colleague to move a phone torch slowly across the nails while you film, then take a still frame from the best moment or post the clip itself.

How many photos should I take of each set?

Follow the shot list: about five photos and one short clip, with three or four frames of each so you can pick the sharpest. That takes around two minutes once the routine is familiar.
