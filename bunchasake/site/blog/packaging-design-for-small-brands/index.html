Packaging design for small brands: what the pack must say and how it should feel

Good packaging does three jobs: it protects the product, tells a stranger in seconds what is inside and why it is yours, and looks like the rest of your brand. What the label must say by law is a separate job for qualified advisers.

By the Bunchasake team · Published 11 Nov 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways
  • Packaging has three jobs: protect the product, explain it in seconds and look like the rest of your brand.
  • Plan the reading order of the front: brand, what it is, why it is different, then the variant.
  • Choose materials for the product and the journey first, and for looks second. A beautiful box that arrives crushed is bad branding.
  • One template with fixed places for the logo, name and colour makes every new product look like part of the family.
  • Required label content, claims and symbols differ by product and country. Ask a qualified adviser before you print.

Packaging design for small brands is not about an expensive box. It is about three jobs done well: protecting the product, telling a stranger in a few seconds what is inside and why it is yours, and looking like the same brand as your shop, website and social pages. A plain jar with a clear, well-made label can do all three better than a custom box that says nothing.

This guide is for a coffee roaster, a candle maker, a family bottling chilli sauce or a small skincare line, and it covers the brand side of the pack. What the label is legally required to say, such as ingredients, allergens, quantity, warnings, origin and recycling marks, depends on the product and the country, and belongs with qualified advisers.

Packaging design for small brands: the three jobs

  1. Protect. The product arrives whole and stays fresh, dry or unspilled until it is used.
  2. Explain. On a shelf, your pack gets a glance. Online, it is a small picture on a phone. Either way, people need to understand what it is almost at once.
  3. Belong. The pack looks like your sign, your website and your Instagram. When someone sees it at a friend's house, they know it is yours.

What the pack must say, in the right order

A shopper reads a pack in an order whether you plan it or not, so plan it. On the front:

  • Brand name. Clear, but not always the biggest thing.
  • What it is, in plain words. "Chilli garlic sauce", "Hand cream", "Whole bean coffee". Many small brands make this the smallest text on the pack, which is backwards.
  • One line of difference. "Made in small batches from our family recipe."
  • The variant. Flavour, scent, strength or size, easy to tell apart.

On the back or side: two or three sentences of story, how to use it, where to find you, and everything your adviser says is required.

Weak front: a large logo, a pattern and "No. 2" in small letters.

Clear front: the logo, then "Chilli garlic sauce, medium hot", then "Small batch, family recipe".

Three quick tests

  • Arm's length. Put the pack on a shelf and step back. Can you read what it is?
  • Thumbnail. Photograph it and look at the picture small on your phone. Can you still tell the product and the variant?
  • Five seconds. Show it to someone who does not know your product, then ask what it is and who it is for.

If you sell in more than one country or to customers who read different languages, decide which language leads on the front and keep the others orderly on the back. Which languages are required is a question for your adviser. Our cross-border localization checklist covers the marketing side.

Leave room for what the label legally needs

A common packaging problem in small brands is not ugly design. It is a beautiful design with no space left for the required text, which then gets squeezed in at a size nobody can read, or the whole job is printed twice.

  • Get the required text before design starts, as a written list from your adviser, per product and per country.
  • Design around it. Give it a fixed, readable area in your template.
  • Be careful with claims. Words such as "natural", "organic", "eco-friendly", "handmade" or "made in" can be regulated. Check each one.
  • Have the adviser approve the final proof, not an early draft.

Official places to start reading are the Your Europe Business portal for the EU and the FTC's business guidance on claims in the United States. Skincare and cosmetics have their own rules, with official pages from the U.S. FDA on cosmetics and the European Commission on cosmetics. These are starting points, not a replacement for advice.

Choosing materials

Decide in this order and you avoid most expensive mistakes:

  1. The product. Does it need protection from light, air, moisture, heat or oil? Is it fragile?
  2. The journey. A shelf, a courier's van or both? Will it be stacked?
  3. The feel. Kraft or white, matte or gloss, heavy or light. This is where the brand speaks.
  4. The practical side. Minimum orders, storage space, lead times and how easily you can reorder the same thing.

Order samples, fill them with real product and send a parcel to yourself. For a greener pack, start by using less material and avoiding mixes that are hard to separate. An environmental line you print is a claim, so check it first.

The unboxing moment

For online orders, decide what the customer sees in order: the outer mailer, then a card or tissue, then the product facing up. The card says thank you, gives one line of story, explains how to use the product and says where to find you. A photo of a correctly packed order above the packing table keeps it the same whoever packs. Product pages, reviews and email are covered in our guide on how to build an e-commerce brand.

Consistency across a product range

Your fifth product should look like it comes from the same family as your first. Build one template:

  • The logo always in the same place and size.
  • One or two typefaces, the same as on your sign and website.
  • The product name always in the same spot and style.
  • One colour per variant, chosen once and written down. A tea brand might use the same box for every tea and change only the colour band and the name.

Keep a packaging file with final artwork, colour references, material details, printer contacts and the approved proof for each product, so a reorder matches the last batch. The same thinking for a physical shop is in our guide to retail store branding.

Common mistakes

  • The logo is the biggest thing and the product name the smallest. Customers cannot tell what they are looking at.
  • Designing before the text is final. Late changes squeeze the layout or force a reprint.
  • Ordering custom boxes too early. If the product, price or name changes, the boxes are wasted.
  • A new style for every product. The range looks like five brands on one shelf.
  • Packs that are hard to open. Frustration becomes the first feeling of the brand.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need custom printed packaging to look professional?

No. A stock jar or box with a well-designed label, used the same way on every product, looks professional. Custom printing makes sense later, when the product, price and design are settled.

What does my label have to say by law?

It depends on the product and the countries where you sell. Ask a qualified adviser for a written list before design starts, and use the official pages linked above only as a starting point for reading.

Should I put my website or social handle on the pack?

Yes, one clear way to find you, the one you keep up to date. A website lasts longer than any single social account.

Is eco-friendly packaging worth it for a small brand?

Using less material and simpler materials often saves money as well. Be careful with the words you print about it, because environmental claims need checking like any other claim.

How do I keep packaging consistent as I add products?

Use one template with fixed places for the logo, name and variant colour, and keep a packaging file with the final artwork and details for every product.
