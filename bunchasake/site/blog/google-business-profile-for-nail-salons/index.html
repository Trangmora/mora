Google Business Profile for nail salon owners, a do-it-yourself guide

Your Google Business Profile is what people see when they search for nails nearby. Claim it, fill in each section truthfully, add real photos and answer every review.

By BunchasakeUpdated 11 Nov 20267 min read

Key takeaways
  • A Google Business Profile is free, and a nail salon owner can claim, verify and manage it without an agency.
  • Use your real name as it appears on the sign, choose Nail salon as the primary category and keep name, address and phone identical everywhere.
  • Real photos of your storefront, your salon and your own work, added regularly, do more than any wording.
  • Ask every client for a review, never reward or fake one, and reply to all of them, including ratings with no text.
  • Google changes its features often, so follow what your own dashboard shows.

Your Google Business Profile is the free listing that shows your salon in Google Search and Google Maps, with your hours, photos, reviews and a way to book. When someone nearby searches for a manicure, it is often the first thing they see, before your website or your Instagram. This guide to Google Business Profile for nail salon owners goes through each part in order, so you can set it up and look after it yourself.

Two notes before you start. First, Google often renames, moves or removes features, and some exist only in certain countries or categories. We describe each one in general terms: follow what your own dashboard shows. Second, this is a do-it-yourself guide. Bunchasake does not sell Google Business Profile management.

Google Business Profile for nail salon owners, in ten steps

1. Claim and verify the profile

Search for your salon on Google Maps. If a listing already exists, look for an option such as "Claim this business" or "Own this business?". If there is none, add your business. Either way, sign in with a Google account the owner controls, not a technician's or a relative's personal account, and add a second manager you trust as a backup.

Google then asks you to prove the business is real. It chooses the method, which may be a phone call, a text, an email, a video or a postcard. Do what the screen asks. If it asks for a video, be ready to show the street, your sign and the inside of the salon. If you took over the salon from a previous owner, ask them to transfer the profile to you as part of the handover, and do not create a second listing for the same address.

2. Choose the primary and secondary categories

The primary category tells Google what you are. For most readers it is "Nail salon", picked from Google's own list. Add secondary categories only for services you really offer and want to be found for, for example a waxing or lash category if you have a room and a technician for it. A short, accurate list is better than a long, hopeful one.

3. Keep name, address and phone consistent

  • Name. Exactly what is on your sign: "Lily Nails & Spa", not "Lily Nails & Spa Best Gel Pedicure Near Me".
  • Address. Include the suite or unit number, and check that the map pin sits on your door, not in the middle of the parking area.
  • Phone. A number that someone answers during opening hours.

Then make these three identical on your website, Instagram bio, Facebook page, booking system and business cards. An old phone number left on one page sends clients to a dead line.

4. Set hours and holiday hours

Enter your real hours. If you stop taking walk-ins before closing, say so in your description. Use the special or holiday hours setting for public holidays, for Tết if you close, for family trips and for any day you shut early. A client who drives over and finds a locked door rarely gives a second chance. Check your hours at the start of every month.

5. List services with plain descriptions

If your dashboard has a services section, add each service with the name clients use, one plain sentence and, if you wish, a price or a "from" price. Write for someone who does not know nail vocabulary:

  • "Gel manicure. Nail shaping, cuticle care and gel polish, dried under a lamp so you leave with no wet nails."
  • "Dip powder. A strong, long-wearing colour finish, good if regular polish chips quickly on you."

Use the business description the same way: two or three sentences on what you are known for, where to park and whether you take walk-ins. No lists of keywords.

6. Add photos, and keep adding them

  • Outside. The storefront in daylight, taken from where a first-time visitor would arrive.
  • Inside. Reception, manicure tables and pedicure chairs, clean and tidy.
  • Real work. Close-ups of nails your team did, in natural light, across your range: plain gel, French, nail art, pedicures.
  • The team. Technicians at work, with their permission.
  • The price menu. Only if it is readable and current.

Skip stock photos, heavy filters and other artists' designs, and ask clients before posting anything that shows a face. As for how often, a small steady habit works best, such as a few new photos every week or two.

7. Post updates

Most profiles can publish short updates: a new service, a seasonal design set, a change in hours. One photo and two sentences are enough. You can reuse what you post elsewhere, and our nail salon Instagram content ideas will keep you supplied.

8. Add the booking link

Look for a field for an appointment or booking link and paste in the address of your booking page. Some booking systems connect to Google directly, so that a "Book" button appears on the profile. Your provider can tell you whether theirs does. If you do not take online bookings, make sure the phone number is right and that your description says "walk-ins welcome". Then test the link on your own phone.

9. Watch the questions and answers

Some profiles show a section where anyone can ask a question, and anyone can answer. If yours has one, check it often and answer as the owner: "Yes, we take walk-ins until one hour before closing." "Parking is free in front of the salon." Google has been changing this feature, so if you do not see it, put the same answers in your description, services and updates.

10. Ask for reviews, and reply to every one

Your dashboard gives you a link, and usually a QR code, that opens the review form. Print it on a small card for each station. Ask when the client is admiring the finished nails, ask everyone, and let people write on their own phone in their own words.

Then reply to every review. Here are three short templates to adapt.

Happy client. "Thank you, [name]. We are so glad you love your [service], and [technician] will be happy to hear it. See you next time."

Unhappy client. "Hi [name], thank you for telling us. We are sorry your visit was not what it should have been, and we would like to put it right. Please call us on [phone] and ask for [owner or manager]."

Rating with no text. "Thank you for the rating, [name]. We hope to see you again soon."

Never argue in public and never mention private details of a visit. If a review is fake or abusive, use the report option and let Google decide.

What not to do

  • Keyword-stuffed business names. Adding "best", a list of services or the city to your name breaks Google's rules and can get a profile suspended.
  • Fake reviews. Reviews written by you, your staff or your family, or bought from someone, can be removed, and in many countries they also break consumer law (in the US, see the FTC business guidance).
  • Rewards for reviews. Google does not allow discounts, gifts or prize draws in exchange for a review. Other platforms have their own rules, so read them before you ask there.
  • Duplicate listings. One salon at one address gets one profile.
  • Set and forget. A profile with wrong hours and two-year-old photos works against you.

Why the brand behind the profile matters

A profile can be complete and still be forgettable. What makes people stop and choose is a name they can tell apart from the salon two streets away, photos with one consistent look and a description that says what you are known for. Frequently asked questions

Is a Google Business Profile free for a nail salon?

Yes. Creating, verifying and managing a profile costs nothing. Be careful with calls or emails that claim to come from Google and ask for payment to keep or improve your listing.

Can I delete a bad review?

No. You can report a review that is fake, offensive or about the wrong business, and Google decides whether it stays. For a genuine complaint, the best answer is a calm reply and a real fix.

I bought the salon or changed its name. Should I create a new profile?

If the salon carries on as the same business, ask the previous owner to transfer the existing profile and then update the details. If you are opening a truly new business in the same unit, Google may treat it differently, so follow the guidance in your dashboard or in the Google Business Profile Help Center. Either way, avoid two live listings for one salon.

Should I reply to reviews in English or Vietnamese?

Reply in the language the client wrote in. In an English-speaking country that is mostly English, and in Germany or Italy it is the local language, so translate the templates above. Keep the wording short, and ask someone to check it if you are unsure.
