How to run local ads for a restaurant, and when it is worth it

Ads are worth trying when the free basics are in place, you have empty seats at known times and one clear thing to offer. Start with one small test near your restaurant and judge it by counting new guests, not likes.

By the Bunchasake team · Published 11 Nov 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways
  • An ad only sends people to look at your restaurant, so fix the profile, photos, menu and reviews before you pay for attention.
  • Advertise one specific offer for the hours when you have empty seats, to people who live or work close enough to come.
  • Set a small total you can afford to lose, run one test for a fixed period, and change only one thing at a time.
  • Judge an ad by counting the new guests who mention it, then compare what you spent with what those guests are worth to you.
  • If a test brings no new guests, stop it, and treat the money as the price of finding out.

If you want to know how to run local ads for a restaurant, start with the question behind it: do you need ads at all? An ad does one thing. It puts your restaurant in front of people nearby who were not looking for you. What they do next depends on what they find when they tap: your photos, your menu, your reviews, your hours. This guide explains when paying for that attention makes sense, how to run one small test yourself, and how to tell whether it worked by counting guests.

To be clear about our role: Bunchasake does not run ads, and does not sell SEO or Google Business Profile management. This is a do-it-yourself guide. Ad platforms change their buttons, formats and rules often, so we describe them in general terms. Follow what your own account shows. You will find no prices or "typical results" here, only a method for working out your own.

When local ads are worth it, and when they are not

Ads are worth testing when all four of these are true.

  1. The free basics are done. Your Google listing is complete, your photos are your own, the menu online is current and you reply to reviews. If not, start with Google Business Profile for restaurants and the restaurant local SEO checklist.
  2. You have room for more guests. If Friday and Saturday dinner are already full, an ad that brings people on Friday only creates a queue and slow service. Ads should fill the quiet times: Tuesday dinner, weekday lunch, the gap after the lunch rush.
  3. You have one specific thing to say. A new opening, a weekday lunch set, a holiday menu, catering for offices, a new regional dish. "We exist" is not an offer.
  4. You can afford to lose the test money. The first tests are how you learn. Some will bring nothing.

Where a restaurant can advertise locally

Each option reaches people in a different moment. In general terms:

  • Search and map ads. Your restaurant appears when someone nearby searches for food. These reach people who are already hungry and choosing. You usually pay when someone taps or calls. Setup takes more care, and the ad leans on your Google listing, which you manage through Google Business Profile.
  • Facebook and Instagram ads. A photo or short video shown to people within a distance of your address. These reach people who are scrolling, not searching, so the picture has to make them hungry. The tools are explained at Meta for Business.
  • Other options. Promoted videos on TikTok, paid placement inside delivery apps, or a flyer for the offices within walking distance. The same test method applies to each.

Pick one. A family restaurant running three kinds of ads at once cannot tell which one brought the guest.

How to run local ads for a restaurant: one small test

Step 1. Choose one goal and one offer

Write it as a sentence: "More guests at weekday lunch, with the phở and iced coffee lunch set." The offer does not need to be a discount. A set, a new dish or a limited menu gives people a reason to come now without teaching them to wait for cheaper prices.

Step 2. Choose who sees it

Almost every platform lets you limit an ad to an area around your address. Think about how far people really travel for your kind of meal. For a quick lunch, that means the offices and homes within a short walk or drive. For a weekend dinner, people come from further. Start narrow. Beyond location, keep the audience broad at first: adults in the area.

Step 3. Make the ad from what you already have

  • One real photo or a short video of the dish, steam and all, taken in daylight. No stock images, no heavy text on the picture.
  • Two or three lines of text. What it is, why it is good, when and where. "Northern-style phở with broth simmered overnight, plus iced coffee with condensed milk. Weekday lunch set, 11.30 to 2.30. Five minutes from Central Station."
  • One action. Get directions, call, book, or order. Choose the one that matches your goal and make sure the page behind the button works on a phone.
  • A phrase to mention. "Say 'lunch set' when you order." This is how you will count, so tell everyone who takes orders.

Step 4. Set the budget and the dates

Decide the total you are willing to spend on the whole test before you open the ad tool, and write it down. Choose an amount whose loss would not hurt the business. Enter it as a total budget with an end date, or as a daily amount with an end date, so the ad stops by itself. Two weeks is a reasonable first test for a restaurant, because it covers each weekday twice.

Judge results by counting new guests

The ad tool will show you numbers: how many people saw the ad, how many tapped. These tell you whether the ad is being noticed. They do not tell you whether anyone came to eat. For that, you count at the till. Use three simple signals together.

  • The phrase. Each time a guest mentions it, the staff make a mark.
  • The question. Ask new faces "How did you hear about us?" and mark the answer.
  • The comparison. Look at covers or receipts for the advertised time slot during the test, next to the same weekdays in the weeks before. Note anything else that could explain a change: weather, school holidays, a street closure.

At the end, do one calculation with your own numbers. Divide the total you spent by the number of new guests who came because of the ad. That is your cost for one new guest. Then compare it with what a new guest is worth to you: what a typical table leaves after the cost of the food, and how often a guest who likes you comes back. If one new guest costs less than they bring you over a few visits, the ad is paying. If it costs more than a guest could ever bring, it is not.

What to do after the first test

  • It brought guests at a cost you accept. Run it again, and change one thing only: the photo, or the area, or the offer. That way you learn which part matters.
  • People tapped but nobody came. The ad is fine, and something after the tap is not. Check the page behind the button, the hours, the menu, the offer itself.
  • No new guests at all. Stop, and put the effort back into free work for a while.

Rules to respect

Everything in an ad must be true: the dish as served, the hours, the terms of the offer. Rules about advertising alcohol, discounts, competitions and health claims differ by country and sometimes by city. In the United States, the FTC's business guidance is the official place to read about advertising rules. For anything you are unsure about, ask a qualified adviser.

Common mistakes

  • An area that is too wide. People an hour away will like the photo and never come.
  • Judging by likes. Likes do not pay rent. Count guests.
  • Staff who were not told. The count fails, and so does the welcome.

The ad is the last step, not the first

An ad can only repeat what your restaurant already says about itself. When the name, the story, the menu and the photos are clear, a two-line ad is easy to write and easy to believe. Frequently asked questions

How much should a restaurant spend on local ads?

There is no correct figure that fits every restaurant. Choose a total for one test that you could lose without harm, run it for a fixed period, and count the new guests it brings. Your own cost per new guest, compared with what a guest is worth to you, tells you whether to spend more, less or nothing.

Should I boost a post or use the full ads tool?

Boosting a post that already did well is the simplest way to begin, and it is enough for a first test. The full tool gives more control over area, placement and budget, and takes longer to learn. Whichever you use, set the area, the end date and the total yourself, and do not accept the default settings without reading them.

How do I know a guest came because of the ad?

You will not know for every guest, and you do not need to. A phrase to mention, a question to new faces and a comparison with the same days in earlier weeks give you a count that is good enough to make a decision.

Should the ad be in the local language or a second language?

Write it in the language of the people you want at that time slot. For office workers at lunch, use the local language and explain the dish. For a holiday menu or a regional speciality aimed at a community that shares a language, such as Vietnamese or Spanish speakers, an ad in that language makes sense.
