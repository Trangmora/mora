B2B content marketing for exporters and manufacturers selling to Europe and North America

Foreign buyers check you before they reply. Build the few pages they look for, write short case notes, post what you know, and follow up by email like a person. Here is how to do it yourself.

By the Bunchasake team · Published 11 Nov 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways
  • In B2B, content does not close the deal. It answers the buyer's questions before the first call and makes your company easier to trust.
  • A foreign buyer needs a handful of website pages: what you make, who you make it for, how you work, proof, and a simple way to ask for a quote.
  • A case note with no client name and no big numbers is still useful if it describes a real problem and how you solved it.
  • Posts written by a real person at your company, about things that person knows, do more than polished company announcements.
  • Good follow-up email is short, personal and useful. It is not a newsletter sent to a bought list.

B2B content marketing for exporters is simpler than it sounds: put on your website the answers a foreign buyer looks for, show a few real pieces of work, share what you know where buyers spend time, and follow up by email like a person. You can do all four with your own team.

This is a do-it-yourself guide for manufacturers, exporters, software teams and service firms that sell to businesses in Europe and North America from their home country. It does not promise leads. Content makes it easier for the right buyer to say yes. It does not replace sales work, trade fairs or a good product.

How a foreign buyer checks a supplier

Picture a purchasing manager at a German furniture retailer. She receives an email from a workshop in Bình Dương she has never heard of. Before she replies, she will probably search the company name, open the website, look for the people behind it on LinkedIn, and perhaps ask a colleague.

She is not looking for beautiful writing. She is trying to answer a few plain questions. Is this a real company? Do they make what I need, at the quality and volume I need? Have they worked with buyers like me? Will they be easy to deal with? What happens if something goes wrong?

Every piece of content you publish should help answer one of those questions. If it does not, it can wait.

The website pages buyers need

You do not need a large website. You need a small one where nothing important is missing. Use this table as a checklist.

PageQuestion it answersWhat to put on it
HomeWhat do you do, and for whom?One sentence saying what you make and who buys it, a real photo of your work, links to the pages below
Products or capabilitiesCan you make what I need?Product types, materials, processes, sizes, what you can customise, what you do not make
How we workWhat is it like to order from you?Steps from enquiry to delivery, sampling, quality checks, how you communicate, who the buyer talks to
Quality and certificatesCan I trust the output?Only certificates you actually hold, with scope and date, and how you check quality day to day
Case notesHave you done this before?Three to five short stories of real work (see below)
AboutWho are you?When and why the company started, the people, real photos of the site, the full address
Contact or request a quoteHow do I start?A short form, a named person, email, phone with country code, working hours and time zone

A few rules make these pages work for a reader in Rotterdam or Toronto:

  • Be specific. "High quality products at competitive prices" could be anyone. "Solid acacia outdoor furniture, made to order for retailers and hotel projects" is a company.
  • Use real photos. Your workshop, your team, your product. Stock photos of smiling people in an office make a buyer doubt the rest.
  • Write in plain English. Short sentences, units the buyer uses, no slogans. Have a fluent reader check the main pages.

Case notes: proof without big claims

A full case study with a client logo and impressive numbers is hard to get. Many B2B clients do not allow it. A case note is smaller and almost always possible: a short, honest account of one job.

Use this structure, one or two sentences each:

  1. The buyer. Type of company and country or region. "A hotel fit-out contractor in northern Europe" is enough if you cannot use the name.
  2. What they needed. The actual problem, including the difficult part.
  3. What you did. The steps, the decisions, what you changed in your normal process.
  4. What happened. Only what you can stand behind: delivered on the agreed date, passed the buyer's inspection, reordered. If you have no measured result, describe the outcome in words.
  5. What you learned. One line. This is the part that makes a buyer trust the rest.

Always ask the client before publishing, even without their name. Never invent a number or a quote. Three plain case notes that are true are worth more than one that sounds too good.

LinkedIn-style posts that buyers actually read

In Europe and North America, many business buyers use LinkedIn the way businesspeople in many Asian markets use Zalo, WeChat or Facebook: to see who someone is. Posts work best from a real person, such as the founder or the export sales lead, with the company page as a supporting home. Platform features change, so check the current options when you set up.

Four kinds of post you can write from what you already know:

  • A buyer's question, answered. "A client asked why sample lead time differs from production lead time. Here is how it works in our factory."
  • How it is made. One step of your process, with a photo from the floor. Why you do it that way.
  • A lesson from a job. Something that went wrong and what you changed. Honest posts are remembered.
  • People. The quality lead who has been with you since the start. Buyers work with people, not logos.

Write the way you would explain it to a visitor walking through your site. Keep each post to one idea. Choose a rhythm you can keep for a year, even if that is one post a week, over a burst that stops after a month. Email follow-up that sounds like a person

Most B2B relationships move forward by email. The moments that matter are after an enquiry, after a trade fair, after a sample is sent and after a quiet period.

A simple pattern:

  1. Reply fast, answer fully. Answer the question asked, attach what was requested, and say what happens next.
  2. Follow up with something useful. A relevant case note, a photo of the sample in progress, a short answer to a question they are likely to have next.
  3. Check in once, politely. If there is no answer, one short message asking whether the timing has changed. Then stop until you have a real reason to write.

An example after a trade fair:

Subject: The acacia bench samples we discussed in Cologne

Dear Ms Weber, thank you for stopping by our stand on Tuesday. You asked whether we can make the bench in the 150 cm size. Yes: the drawing and specification are attached. Shall I send a sample to your Hamburg office? Best regards, Minh, Export Sales.

One caution. Rules about commercial email and personal data differ between countries and are outside this guide. Do not buy lists. Write to people who contacted you or gave you their card, and ask a qualified adviser before any larger mailing. The Your Europe Business portal is the official EU starting point for doing business there, and the FTC business guidance is the equivalent for the United States.

A first-month plan you can copy

  • Week 1. Write down the ten questions buyers ask most. Check which ones your website answers today.
  • Week 2. Rewrite the home page sentence and the products or capabilities page. Replace stock photos with your own.
  • Week 3. Write two case notes. Ask the clients for permission. Fix the contact page: named person, time zone, short form.
  • Week 4. Update the founder's or sales lead's LinkedIn profile. Publish the first two posts from the list above. Write three follow-up email templates and save them.

What B2B content marketing for exporters can and cannot do for lead generation

Content rarely brings a large buyer to your door on its own. What it does is make every other effort work better. The buyer you met at a fair, found through the Vietnam Trade Promotion Agency, or contacted on a marketplace will look you up. Good content means that when they do, they find reasons to continue.

Common mistakes

  • Translating your home-market brochure. Buyers abroad ask different questions. Start from their questions, not your existing text.
  • Claims with no proof. "Leading" and "world-class" are ignored. A photo of your inspection table is not.
  • Hiding the people. No names, no faces and a generic email address read as a risk to a foreign buyer.
  • Treating Europe and North America as one audience. Units, spelling, expectations and buying habits differ. See our guides to taking your brand into European markets and entering the US market.

Frequently asked questions

How long does B2B content marketing take to show an effect?

There is no honest fixed answer. B2B buying is slow, and content supports sales conversations more than it creates them. Expect the first signs in the quality of conversations, such as buyers arriving better informed, before you see anything in order volume.

Do we need a blog?

Not at the start. The core pages and a few case notes matter more. Add articles later, when you have real buyer questions to answer and someone who can keep writing.

Should the website be in English only, or also in German, French or other languages?

Start with good English, which most business buyers in Europe and North America can read. Add another language when one country becomes a real focus, and have a native speaker write or check it. A poor translation does more harm than no translation.

Who in the company should write the content?

The people who talk with buyers and the people who make the product. They know the real questions and answers. Someone with good English can then edit for clarity. AI tools can help with drafts and editing, but a person who knows the facts must check every line.
