Nail salon logo and branding: name, logo, colours, menu and signage

A salon brand is the name, logo, colours, menu and sign saying the same thing to the same client. Decide who the salon is for first, then build the five pieces in that order.

By the Bunchasake team · Published 11 Nov 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways
  • Decide who the salon is for and what it is known for before anyone draws a logo, because every later choice depends on that answer.
  • A good salon name is easy for a local client to say, spell and search after hearing it once.
  • A logo is ready when it still reads clearly as a small round profile picture and in one colour on a sign.
  • The service menu is the most read piece of the brand, so group it, name services plainly and keep the printed and online versions identical.
  • One name, one logo and one set of colours used everywhere do more for a salon than a clever design used in three different ways.

Nail salon logo and branding is not one design job. It is five decisions that have to agree with each other: the name, the logo, the colours and lettering, the service menu, and the sign above the door. When they agree, a client who saw your Instagram recognises the storefront from the car park. When they do not, the salon looks like three different businesses, and people quietly trust it less.

This guide walks through the five pieces in the order that saves money, with examples for an owner-run salon and a checklist you can copy. If you want the wider thinking behind a brand first, read how a small business can build a brand, then come back here for the salon details.

Before the logo: who is the salon for?

Most owners start with the logo because it is the visible part. Start one step earlier. Write short, honest answers to three questions:

  1. Who is the main client? Office workers on a lunch break, mothers from the school nearby, students who want nail art, older clients who come for pedicures every three weeks.
  2. What should they remember the salon for? Careful, clean work. Detailed hand-painted art. Speed without an appointment. A quiet, calm room.
  3. How should the visit feel? Friendly and lively, or calm and private. Affordable and quick, or slow and pampering.

The name: easy to say, spell and search

If the salon already has a name clients know, think carefully before changing it. Reviews, the Google listing and word of mouth are attached to that name. A change is worth it mainly when the name is shared with other salons nearby, hard for local clients to say, or tied to a previous owner.

For a new name, test it against this list:

  • Say it on the phone. Tell a friend who speaks a different language the name once and ask them to type it. If they cannot, clients will not find you after a recommendation.
  • Keep it short. Two or three words fit on a sign, a profile picture and a booking link. "Sen Nails" works. "Sen Luxury Nails Spa and Beauty Lounge" will be cut off everywhere.
  • A word from your own language can be a strength. Short Vietnamese words without difficult tone marks, such as Sen, Mai, Lan or An, are easy for anyone to say and give the salon a story to tell. Decide early whether you write the name with or without diacritics, and stay with it.
  • Search the area. Look on Google Maps for the same or a similar name in your city. Two "Lucky Nails" a few streets apart share each other's phone calls and reviews.

Whether a name can be registered or protected as a trade mark is a legal question that depends on your country. Ask a qualified adviser before you invest in a sign. Readers in Europe can see how EU trade marks work at the EU Intellectual Property Office.

The logo: what a working salon logo needs

A salon logo has a hard job. It must be readable from a moving car, in a small circle on a phone, in one colour on a receipt, and in gold foil on a gift card. Design it for the hardest places first.

  • Name first, symbol second. For a local salon, clear lettering of the name does most of the work. A symbol is useful as a profile picture, but it cannot replace a readable name.
  • Avoid the obvious pictures. A polish bottle, a hand with long nails or a generic lotus are used by many salons, so they do not help anyone tell you apart. If you use a lotus because the salon is called Sen, draw it in a way that is yours.
  • Test in one colour. If the logo only works with a gradient or shadow, it will fail on a window sticker and a stamp.

Whoever makes the logo, ask for the files in writing: a vector file (SVG, AI or PDF) that the sign maker can enlarge, PNG files with a transparent background, and versions in full colour, black and white.

Colours and lettering

Pick a small palette and write it down:

  • One main colour that people will connect with the salon: a deep green, a warm terracotta, a soft plum.
  • One or two supporting colours for highlights and backgrounds.
  • One neutral, such as off-white, cream or charcoal, for text and space.

Two checks before you commit. First, hold the colours next to the things you cannot change cheaply: the pedicure chairs, the floor, the wall behind the reception desk. Second, check that text in your colours is easy to read. Pale pink lettering on white is a common choice and a common regret.

For lettering, two typefaces are enough: one for headings and one for body text and prices. If you write bilingual material, make sure both include every accented letter your languages use, Vietnamese included, or accents will appear in a different font and the menu will look broken. Record the colour codes and font names on one page. That page is the start of your brand guidelines.

The service menu is part of the brand

  • Group services the way a client thinks: manicures, pedicures, extensions and enhancements, nail art, add-ons, removal. Put the most booked services at the top of each group.
  • Use plain names. "Gel manicure" is found and understood. If you like a signature name, add a plain one beside it: "Sen Signature Pedicure (pedicure with scrub, mask and massage)".
  • Show prices honestly. If a price depends on length or design, write "from" and say what changes it. Surprises at the till undo the whole brand.
  • Think about language. The menu is in the language of your local clients. If many clients read another language, add a second version or a smaller second line rather than mixing two languages in one sentence.
  • Keep one source. The printed menu, the wall board, the website, the booking system and the services on your Google Business Profile should carry the same names and prices. Update them on the same day.

Signage: the brand people see from the road

For a salon in a strip mall, the sign is still the first advertisement. A few rules decide whether it works:

  • Name plus the word that says what you do. "Sen Nails" tells a driver everything. A symbol alone tells them nothing.
  • Contrast over decoration. Dark letters on a light background or the opposite. Check the design in daylight and after dark.
  • Keep the window clear. Hours, phone and the booking link belong on the door. A window covered in price lists hides the clean room behind it.

Shopping centres and local councils often have rules on sign size, lighting and placement. Ask your landlord and the local authority before you order.

Use it the same way everywhere

A brand becomes real through repetition. Use the same name spelling on the sign, on Google, on Yelp for Business and on Instagram. Use the same small mark as every profile picture. Common mistakes

  • Buying a logo before deciding the client. The result looks nice and says nothing.
  • Different names in different places. "Sen Nails" on the sign, "Sen Nail and Spa" on Google and "sen.beauty" on Instagram look like three salons.
  • A menu that grew by tape. Handwritten price changes and stickers tell clients the details are not looked after.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a new logo, or can I keep the one I have?

Keep it if clients recognise it and it reads clearly on the sign and as a small profile picture. Often the logo is fine and the real problem is that the colours, menu and listings do not match it. A refresh that tidies and aligns what you already use is usually kinder to regulars than a full change.

Should the salon have a name in my own language?

It can, if local clients can say and type it after hearing it once. Short words such as Sen or Mai work well and give you a story to tell. Decide one spelling, with or without diacritics, and use it everywhere.

What should come first on a small budget?

The pieces every client meets: a clear name, a readable logo, the sign, the service menu and matching online listings. Uniforms, bags and decorated walls can wait until those five agree with each other.

Can I protect my salon name?

Possibly, but the rules differ by country and depend on the name itself. That is a legal question, so ask a qualified adviser before you spend on signage and printing.
