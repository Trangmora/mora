A restaurant content calendar for one month, ready to copy

Write down the dates of your month first, give each week one theme, and keep the same three posting days. Below is a 4-week table to copy, a year of dates worth marking and a one-hour planning checklist.

By the Bunchasake team · Published 11 Nov 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways
  • A restaurant content calendar starts with the restaurant's own dates, such as closures, specials and holidays, not with a list of post ideas.
  • One theme per week means you film one subject from several sides instead of hunting for a new idea each day.
  • Three fixed posting days, Stories on service days and one Google update a week are enough for a family team.
  • One planning hour a month and one filming afternoon every two weeks keep the calendar alive when service is busy.
  • When a week goes wrong, move a post to another day, and never post something that is no longer true.

A restaurant content calendar is one page that says what you will post, on which day and where, for the next month. It works when it follows the rhythm of the restaurant: the days you are open, the specials, the holidays, the slow Tuesday. This guide shows how to build one in an hour, gives you a 4-week table to copy and lists the dates worth marking for the year.

What a restaurant content calendar needs, and what it does not

It does not need software. A sheet of paper on the office wall or a shared note on the phone is fine. It needs four things.

  • Dates. What is actually happening in the restaurant this month.
  • A theme per week. One subject, so that filming is simple.
  • Fixed slots. The same posting days every week, each with a job.
  • A name next to each task. Who films, who writes, who presses post.

Our restaurant Instagram content ideas include a short two-week plan for Instagram alone. This article goes one step further: a full month, across the channels a restaurant uses, tied to real dates.

Step 1: write down the restaurant's month

Before any post idea, take the calendar on the wall and mark what is already decided. Most of your content is hiding in this list.

  • Days closed and changed hours. Public holidays, a family trip, a private event.
  • Food dates. A new dish, a seasonal special, the day the summer rolls come back, the weekend you make bánh chưng.
  • Local dates. School holidays, a street festival, a match day, the first cold week.
  • Community dates. The festivals your guests and your family keep, such as Tết, Mid-Autumn or a temple or church festival nearby.
  • Your slow and busy days. If Tuesday lunch is always quiet, that is the day a lunch offer should be seen. If Saturday is full, Saturday posts should not push for more bookings.

Example: a family phở restaurant looks at November and writes: closed on the 11th, bún riêu special starts on the 4th, first frost expected, office Christmas lunches to be booked from mid-month. That is already four posts and a theme.

Step 2: give each week one theme

A theme is one subject you show from several sides. It removes the daily question "what do we post today?" and lets you film a week in one sitting. Four themes that suit almost any month:

  • Week 1: one dish. The signature, or this month's special. Show it finished, being made and being eaten.
  • Week 2: the people. The cook, the family, the person at the counter.
  • Week 3: the newcomer. How to order, how to eat it, what to try first.
  • Week 4: the neighbourhood and what is next. Regulars, a neighbouring shop, and next month's dates.

Step 3: fix the slots

Choose three posting days and keep them all year. For most restaurants that are closed on Monday, Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday work well. Then give each slot a job.

  • Tuesday: the appetite post. A video or photo that makes people hungry and starts the week's theme.
  • Thursday: the useful post. Something the reader can use: how to eat it, how to order for the office, what is in the dish.
  • Saturday: the human post. A face, a story, a thank-you.
  • Service days: Stories. Today's special and whether there are tables.
  • Once a week: the Google update. One new photo or a short update on your Google Business Profile, where your profile offers that option, and a check that the hours are right.

What goes where

You do not need different content for every channel. You need the same content cut to fit.

  • Instagram. All three weekly posts and the Stories.
  • Facebook. The same three posts. This is often where older regulars and community groups are. If many of them speak another language, such as Vietnamese, add a line in that language when it fits. The scheduling tools in Meta for Business can queue Instagram and Facebook together.
  • TikTok. The Tuesday video, with the city said out loud and written on screen. For formats that suit it, see our restaurant TikTok content ideas, and TikTok for Business for account options.

A year of dates for a restaurant

Copy this list into your wall calendar once, keep the festivals that belong to your kitchen and your guests, then add the local dates of your own country. Exact days change each year and from country to country, so check them.

  • January and February. Lunar New Year, if your kitchen or your guests celebrate it: closing days, a festive menu, orders for bánh chưng or party trays, a greeting in your own language. Valentine's Day for couples' tables.
  • Spring. Fresh rolls and lighter dishes return. Mother's Day, which falls on different dates in different countries. Easter and school breaks.
  • Summer. Cold noodles, iced coffee, chè, outdoor seats. Tourists who need the walk-from-the-station video. Your own holiday closure, announced early.
  • Autumn. Back to school and back to the office, so lunch offers. Community festivals such as Mid-Autumn, with mooncakes and lanterns.
  • Winter. Phở and hotpot weather. Group bookings for year-end meals. Holiday hours, posted at least two weeks ahead on every channel.

The planning hour: a checklist to copy

Once a month, one hour, in the last week of the month:

  1. Mark next month's closures, specials, local and community dates.
  2. Choose the four weekly themes and write them at the top of each week.
  3. Fill the three slots for each week with one line each.
  4. Write a name next to every line: who films, who writes, who posts.
  5. Circle the posts that must be exact, such as hours and closures, and decide who checks them.
  6. Look back at last month: which posts made guests mention them at the till? Keep those.

Every two weeks, one quiet afternoon: film the six feed posts for the next two weeks. Photos of dishes can be taken during the same session.

Every Monday, fifteen minutes: read the week's lines, change anything that is no longer true, write the captions.

When the plan meets a real week

  • Move, do not skip. If Tuesday's video is not ready, post it on Wednesday. The theme still holds.
  • Keep two spare posts. A good dish photo and a review with your reply, ready for the week when someone is ill.
  • Take down what is no longer true. If the special sold out or the hours changed, edit or remove the post the same day.

Common mistakes

  • Planning thirty different ideas. It looks impressive and dies in the second week. Four themes are easier.
  • A calendar only one person understands. If the daughter who made it goes on holiday, posting stops. Put it where the team can see it.

Frequently asked questions

How far ahead should a restaurant plan its content?

One month in outline and two weeks in detail is a workable balance. The month gives you themes and dates, and the two weeks are what you actually film and write. Planning further ahead in detail rarely survives a real kitchen.

How many posts a week does a restaurant need?

Three feed posts, Stories on the days you are open and one Google update are enough for most family restaurants. Choose the number you can keep in your busiest month, since stopping and starting does more harm than a modest, steady rhythm.

What do I do when the menu or the hours change mid-month?

Change the calendar first, then check every scheduled post that mentions the old dish or the old hours and edit or delete it. Update the Google listing the same day, because that is where most people check before they leave home.

Which language should the calendar and the posts be in?

Write the calendar itself in whatever language the team shares. For the posts, use the local language first and add a short line in your community's language when the post concerns them, such as a Lunar New Year menu or a dish from home.
