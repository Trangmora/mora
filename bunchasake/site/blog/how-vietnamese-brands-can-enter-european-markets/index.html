Going international

How to enter European markets, a brand and communication guide for growing brands

Pick one country and one customer, then make your brand easy to understand for that customer before you launch. This guide covers the brand side only, not legal, customs or tax.

By the Bunchasake team · Published 11 Nov 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways
  • Europe is not one market. Choose one country and one customer first, and build everything for them.
  • A European buyer should understand within ten seconds what the product is, who makes it and why it is worth the price.
  • Localizing is more than translating. Check the name, positioning, copy, visual identity, packaging information, website and about story.
  • A specific origin story with real places, people and methods earns more trust than postcard clichés.
  • Legal, customs, certification and tax questions belong with qualified advisers, not with a brand guide.

The short answer to how to enter European markets with a growing brand: choose one country and one customer, then make your brand easy to understand for that customer before the first shipment leaves. That means checking the name, sharpening the positioning, rewriting the copy, and preparing packaging, a website and a story a stranger can trust.

This guide covers the brand and communication side only. Legal, customs, certification and tax questions are outside it. They differ by product and by country, and they need qualified advisers.

How to enter European markets one country at a time

From far away, Europe can look like one place. For a buyer it is not. A shopper in Milan and a shopper in Hamburg speak different languages, shop in different kinds of stores and react to different messages. A brand that speaks to all of them at once is heard by none.

Choose the first country by where you already have an advantage, not by its size:

  • a buyer or distributor who has already shown interest
  • a founder or team member who has lived there and speaks the language

Then choose the first customer. A furniture maker in Bình Dương selling to Germany is probably not selling to "German consumers" at the start. The first customer is a purchasing manager at a retailer or importer, who wants clear specifications, consistent quality and a supplier who answers emails. A coffee roaster in Đà Lạt selling online to people at home has a very different first customer.

Write one sentence before going further: "We sell [what] to [who] in [country], and they choose us because [reason]."

What a European buyer needs to understand in the first ten seconds

A buyer walking past your stand, or a shopper landing on your website, gives you a few seconds. In that time they need three answers.

  1. What is it? Use the plain category word first. "Single-origin arabica coffee from Đà Lạt" is clear. "The essence of the highlands in every cup" is not.
  2. Who makes it? A real company, in a real place, with real people. A name, a town and a photo of your own workshop do more than a paragraph of adjectives.
  3. Why is it worth the price? Give specific reasons: the material, the method, the time it takes, the service after the sale. "Premium quality" is not a reason, because everyone writes it.

What to localize before launch

Localizing is not translating. It means making sure each part of the brand is understood the way you intend, by the customer you chose.

The name

Ask three or four native speakers in the target country to read the name aloud without help. Can they say it? What does it remind them of? If it uses accents or tone marks, check how it looks without them, because that is how keyboards, invoices and web addresses will write it. Whether the name is free to register is a legal question for a trade mark professional. The official places to read about it are the EU Intellectual Property Office and the WIPO Madrid System.

The positioning

Your place in the market may change when you cross a border. The affordable choice in your home market can land in the middle of the price range in Europe once shipping and retail margins are added. Then "good price" is no longer your reason to buy, and you need another: the craft, the material, the design, the reliability. The four questions in our guide to building a brand for your business help here, for a factory as much as for a shop.

English and local-language copy

English is usually enough to start talking with importers and distributors. People buying for themselves prefer their own language. Both should be written or edited by a native writer, starting from what the customer needs to know and not from the brochure you made for your home market. We explain why line-by-line translation fails in our article on bilingual marketing, one brand in two languages.

The visual identity

Put your product next to the ones it will sit beside on a shelf in that country. Does it look like it belongs at your price? Does the logo still work when the name is written without marks? You do not need to look European. You need to look like yourself, clearly, at the level you charge for.

Packaging information

The pack must tell a shopper what is inside, how much, how to use it and who made it, in their language and their units. What must legally appear on a label depends on the product and the country. Start reading on the European Commission's Access2Markets portal and confirm the list with a qualified adviser before you design.

The website

A buyer will look you up before replying to your email. They should find a site in English and, ideally, the local language, with product pages, specifications, photos of your own products, your company name and address, and one obvious way to contact you.

A credible "about" story

Say who founded the company, where it is, how the product is made and what you promise. Use real names and real photos. A buyer who cannot visit is deciding whether you exist and whether you will still be there next year.

Telling the origin story without clichés

Every origin has its clichés. For a brand from Southeast Asia they are conical hats, lanterns, bamboo, a sunset over rice fields and the word "exotic". These are easy to reach for, and ten other brands have reached for them too. They describe a postcard, not your company.

A better origin story is specific:

  • A place smaller than a country. Not "from Vietnam" but "from a pottery workshop in Bát Tràng".
  • A person. The founder, the head of the workshop, the farmer you buy from, with a name.
  • A method. What you do that takes longer or costs more, and why you do it anyway.

Before: "Bringing the exotic flavours of the mysterious East to your table."

After: "Fish sauce from Phú Quốc, made from anchovies and sea salt, aged in wooden barrels by the same family for three generations."

The second version names its origin with more confidence, and gives the buyer something to check and repeat. Only write what is true for your product. A buyer who catches one exaggeration will doubt the rest.

Choosing your first channels

  1. A clear website. This is home. Every business card, sample box and social profile points to it.
  2. One social channel. Choose the one your first customer uses. For trade buyers that is often LinkedIn, for consumer products often Instagram, but habits vary by country, so ask your contacts. One channel kept alive beats four left empty.
  3. Trade contacts. Fairs, business associations, importers in your category, and your own country's trade promotion agency, such as VIETRADE. Bring a company profile, a product sheet and a price list in English, all with the same look.
  4. A partner on the ground. A distributor, agent or representative who speaks the language and can meet people in person. Give them your brand guidelines, so they present you the way you present yourself.

Common mistakes

  • Leading with "cheap". A low price invites the question of what is wrong, and someone can always go lower.
  • Slow replies. With the time difference, an unanswered email can cost two working days. Decide who answers in English, and by when.

Frequently asked questions

Which European country should we start with?

The one where you already have an advantage: an interested buyer or a team member who knows the language. A smaller country where you have a real contact is a better start than a large one where you know nobody.

Do we have to change our brand name for Europe?

Not always. Keep it if native speakers can say it and it carries no unwanted meaning. If it fails that test, consider a shorter form or a new name for export, and have a trade mark professional check it before you print anything.

Is English enough, or do we need the local language?

English is usually enough for first talks with importers and distributors. For packaging and for selling to consumers, plan for the local language, written by a native speaker.

Should we highlight where the brand comes from?

Yes, as a reason to believe and not as the whole message. Lead with what the product is and why it is good, then let a specific place, person and method show where it comes from.
