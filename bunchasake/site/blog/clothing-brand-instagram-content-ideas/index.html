Clothing brand Instagram content ideas: drops, styling, making and fit

Build your feed on four kinds of post: the drop, the styling, the making, and the fit and fabric. Here are ideas for each, hooks to film this week and a two-week drop plan.

By the Bunchasake team · Published 11 Nov 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways
  • A clothing brand's Instagram has four jobs: announce the drop, show how to wear each piece, show how it is made, and answer the fit question before anyone asks.
  • Every product post should say the height of the person wearing it and the size she has on, because fit is usually the first doubt when buying clothes online.
  • Tell a drop as a short story over two weeks, with a teaser, the making, the reveal and the styling, instead of one post on launch day.
  • Customer photos are your most believable styling content, as long as you ask before you repost and credit the person.

The clothing brand Instagram content ideas that work for a small label do four jobs: they announce the drop, show how to wear each piece, show how it is made, and answer the fit question before anyone has to ask. Set up a profile that makes buying easy, then rotate those four kinds of post, with more of them in the two weeks around a drop.

This guide is about what to post. The look of your feed is covered in how to build a fashion brand identity, and the story behind your pieces in fashion brand storytelling.

Set up the profile so a stranger can shop

Someone who likes one Reel will open your profile and decide in seconds whether you are for her. Make these things easy to find:

  • Name field. Your brand name plus what you make: "Gió Studio | Linen clothing". Instagram uses this line in search.
  • Bio. What you make, who it is for, where you ship. "Linen shirts and trousers for hot cities. Sizes XS to XXL. Ships across the EU."
  • Link. Your shop or the current drop. Before a launch, the waitlist.
  • Professional account. A business or creator account is free and adds contact buttons and basic insights. Start at Instagram for Business. Product tagging and shopping features depend on your country and your shop setup, and they change over time, so check Meta for Business for what applies to you.
  • Highlights. At least four: Size guide, Shipping and returns, Drops and Worn by you.

Clothing brand Instagram content ideas in four pillars

A pillar is a kind of post you return to again and again. Each answers a question in a shopper's head:

  • The drop: what is new, and when can I buy it?
  • The styling: how would I wear it?
  • The making: who is behind this, and why does it cost what it costs?
  • The fit and fabric: will it fit me, and how does it feel?

Drop content: tell the story before launch day

A drop announced only on launch day reaches the few people who happen to be online that hour. Spread it over two weeks so followers have time to notice, save and plan.

  1. The detail teaser. Hook: "Something in washed linen. Friday." Film: a slow close-up of a button, a seam or the weave, never the whole piece.
  2. The swatch post. Hook: "Three colours made it. One did not." Film: the fabric samples on the table, and your hand pushing one aside.
  3. The reveal carousel. First image: the hero piece on a person. Then one piece per image, with its name. Last image: the date, the time and where to buy.
  4. The countdown. If your account offers a countdown sticker in Stories, use it so followers can ask for a reminder. Features differ between accounts, and the Instagram Help Center lists what is current.
  5. The honest update. "Size M in sand is gone. S and L are still here." Post scarcity only when it is true. Followers notice when "almost sold out" never sells out.

Styling content: one piece, many ways

People rarely buy a garment on its own. They buy the outfit they can picture it in, and styling posts do the picturing for them.

  1. One piece, three outfits. Hook: "One shirt, three Mondays." Film: the same shirt with trousers, a skirt and jeans, three seconds each.
  2. Morning to evening. Hook: "9am to 9pm in the same dress." Film: the dress with flats and a blazer, then with earrings and sandals.
  3. Same piece, different bodies. Hook: "Same dress, size S and size XL." Film: two people of different heights side by side, each with her height and size written on screen.

You do not need professional models or a studio. Your team, friends and customers who agree to it, filmed in daylight, are often more believable, as long as you always say who is wearing what size.

Behind the scenes: show the hands and the decisions

Making-of content explains your price without a lecture.

  • Sample one versus sample three. Hook: "Why we changed this collar twice." Film: the samples side by side, then the final piece.
  • The fitting. A piece pinned on a person, the pattern adjusted, the second try.
  • The sketchbook. Pages turned slowly from the first idea to the finished piece. A modern áo dài label might show how a traditional collar became one you can wear to work.

Show only what is true, and ask before you film anyone. If a partner workshop makes your pieces, film the fittings and quality checks you do yourself rather than implying you sew everything. Words such as "handmade", "sustainable" or "made in" carry weight with shoppers and, in many countries, legal rules, so use them only when you can explain exactly why they are true.

Fit and fabric: answer the questions before the messages arrive

Fit is usually the first doubt when buying clothes online, so answer it in every product post. Copy this checklist into your caption template:

  • The height of the person wearing it and the size she has on.
  • The fit in plain words: true to size, relaxed, cropped, or size down if between sizes.
  • One key measurement, such as the length from shoulder to hem.
  • Fabric content, exactly as it appears on the label.
  • How it feels: crisp, soft, stretchy or no stretch, and whether it is see-through in daylight.
  • Care in one line, then the price and where to buy.

A caption that follows it: "Ana is 165 cm and wears size S. Relaxed fit, true to size. 100% linen, softer after the first wash, no stretch. Not see-through. Wash cold, hang to dry. Link in bio."

Fabric deserves its own short videos too. Hold a piece up to the window: "Is it see-through? Let's check." Scrunch it to show how it creases, or pull a knit gently to show the stretch.

Stories and community: polls, questions and customer photos

  • Polls. "Sand or olive for the next run?" Then tell people which colour won.
  • Question box. "Ask us anything about sizing." Answer the best questions in a Story and save them to the Size guide Highlight.
  • Worn by you. When a customer tags you, thank her and ask before you share her photo. Credit her and keep the reply.
  • Gifted pieces. If you send clothes to local creators, ask them to say clearly that the piece was a gift. Advertising rules in many countries require it, and the FTC business guidance explains the rules in the United States.

Keep the feed recognisable

A new visitor reads your first nine posts as one picture. One kind of light, one setting and colours that match your labels let people recognise you before they read the name. Common mistakes on a clothing brand's Instagram

  • Flat lays only. A shirt on the floor tells nobody how it falls on a body. Show every piece worn.
  • No size information. Every post without the height and size worn sends the question to your inbox, or sends the shopper away.
  • Colour that lies. A filter that changes the shade leads to returns. The piece that arrives must match the screen.
  • Slow replies in drop week. A sizing question on drop day is a shopper standing at the till. Answer within hours.

Frequently asked questions

How often should a small clothing brand post on Instagram?

There is no right number. Choose a pace you can hold for months, such as three feed posts a week plus Stories, and post more in the two weeks around a drop.

Should we show prices on Instagram?

Yes, in the caption or on the product tag. "DM for price" adds a step that many shoppers will not take.

Can we use a customer's photo in an ad?

A tag is not permission to use her photo in an ad. Ask her clearly for that use, in writing, and keep her answer.

We are launching our first collection. Where should we start?
