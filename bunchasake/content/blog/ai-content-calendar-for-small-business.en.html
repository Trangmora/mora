An AI content calendar for small business is a simple thing: a 30-day plan that says what you will post on which day, with an AI assistant writing the first draft of each post and a person approving it. You can build your first one in an afternoon. This guide goes step by step, with a brief template and a sample week to copy. It does not depend on any particular app. A spreadsheet and the AI assistant you already use are enough.

What a content calendar is, and why 30 days

A content calendar is a table. Each row is one post: the date, the platform, the topic, the format (photo, short video, carousel, story) and the caption.

Its value is that you decide ahead of time. Without a calendar, every post starts with "what should we post today?", usually asked at the end of a long shift. With a calendar, you answer that once a month, when you are rested.

Why 30 days, and not a week or a year?

  • A month matches how a small business runs. Rent, payroll, seasonal menus and promotions already move in monthly cycles.
  • It is long enough to batch. You can take the photos and clips for a whole month in one or two sessions.
  • It is short enough to stay true. Plans made three months out go stale.

Thirty days does not mean thirty posts. Three or four good posts a week that you can keep up are better than a daily schedule you abandon in week two. That makes 12 to 16 posts for the month.

How to choose four or five content pillars

A content pillar is a theme you come back to every week. Pillars stop the calendar from becoming thirty versions of "come visit us". To find yours, write down the answers to three questions:

  1. What do customers ask before they buy or book?
  2. What do regulars say they love about you?
  3. What happens behind the counter that customers never see?

Then group the answers. For most owner-run businesses, the pillars end up close to these:

  • The work. What you make or do, shown clearly. A salon with four technicians in a strip mall: a finished set in daylight, with the technician's first name. A family phở restaurant: the broth pot at 6 a.m.
  • The people. One photo and two honest sentences about who is behind the counter.
  • Proof. A customer's kind words, shared with permission, or a before and after.
  • Helpful answers. Parking, walk-ins, how long a service takes, what to order the first time.
  • Offers and news. New items, seasonal specials, holiday hours. Keep this to about one post in five, so the page does not read like a flyer.

How to brief an AI assistant so it writes in your voice

An AI assistant with no brief writes like a brochure: polite, generic and easy to forget. The fix is a short written brief, the same one you would give a new employee who is going to write for you. Fill in this template once, save it, and paste it in at the start of every session.

Part of the briefWhat to writeExample (family phở restaurant)
Who we areWhat you do, where, and who runs it.A family phở restaurant near the business park. My mother cooks the broth, I run the front.
Who we talk toYour two main customer groups and what each wants.Office workers who need lunch in 30 minutes. Families from the neighbourhood at the weekend.
How we soundThree words for your tone, plus one sentence you would really say.Warm, plain, a little funny. "Come hungry, the bowl is big."
Words to use and avoidNames as you write them. Words you dislike.Write "phở" with the accents. Never "authentic" or "best in town".
LanguagesWhich language for which audience.English first. One line in Vietnamese on weekend posts.
Our pillarsYour four or five pillars.The broth, the family, our regulars, tips for first-timers, specials.
Facts it may useAddress, hours, how to book. Only what is true today.Open 11 a.m. to 9 p.m., closed Tuesdays. Walk-ins only.
Hard rulesWhat it must never do.Do not invent prices, discounts, ingredients or reviews. No health claims. If unsure, write [CHECK].
FormatLength, hashtags, call to action.Under 60 words. Three hashtags at most. One clear next step.

Two additions help a lot. First, paste in three to five of your own past posts that sound like you, and say "match this voice". Second, work in two steps: ask it to "propose 14 post ideas for the next 30 days, spread across my pillars, as a table", pick the ideas you like, then ask for the caption for each one.

If you serve customers in two languages, ask for each language to be written on its own, not translated word for word. Our guide to bilingual marketing for small businesses explains when to use which.

How to review and approve drafts quickly

The AI saves you the writing, not the judgment. A person should read every post before it goes out. Keep one fixed slot a week, for example Monday before opening, and ask five questions of each draft:

  1. Is it true? Hours, names, ingredients, services. AI can state a wrong detail with full confidence.
  2. Does it sound like us? Read it out loud. If you would not say it at the counter, change it.
  3. Is there one clear next step? Book, call, come by or send a message. One, not three.
  4. Does the picture match the words? And has everyone in it agreed to be shown?
  5. Is the timing still right? A post about the patio should not go out during a storm.

Mark each draft approve, edit or drop. Do not rewrite a weak draft yourself: tell the assistant what was wrong ("too formal, shorter, mention the lunch crowd"), ask again, and add that note to your brief. Let one person have the final yes, so nothing is published by accident.

What to never automate

Scheduling and automatic replies are good at simple, repeated tasks. These need a person every time:

  • Replies to complaints. An unhappy customer needs to feel heard by a human. Answer personally, then offer to continue by private message or phone.
  • Anything about prices or promises. Prices, discounts and claims about results are promises, and advertising rules apply to posts as well. A person checks every figure and every claim. In the US, the FTC's business guidance on advertising and reviews is the official place to read the rules. Other countries have their own.
  • Anything about health or safety. Allergens, ingredients, hygiene, skin or nail conditions. Never let an AI answer these alone. Say only what you know to be true, and when in doubt invite the customer to call.
  • Sensitive moments. Bad news in your town, a staff matter, a closure. Pause the schedule and write the post yourself.

Automatic replies are fine for facts that rarely change, such as your address, opening hours and how to book, as long as someone updates them.

Common mistakes

  • Skipping the brief. Asking for "30 posts for a nail salon" gets you posts that fit any salon, so they say nothing about yours.
  • Publishing without reading. One invented discount or wrong opening time costs more trust than a week of silence.
  • Using AI pictures of work you did not do. Customers come for what they saw. Show your real nails, food and team.
  • Setting it and forgetting it. A scheduled post still needs someone to answer the comments and messages it brings.
  • Never changing the plan. At the end of the month, see which posts brought messages or bookings, and give those pillars more room.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a special tool to make an AI content calendar?

No. A spreadsheet and a general AI assistant are enough to plan and draft a month of posts. The calendar and the brief matter more than the software.

How many posts should a small business plan for 30 days?

Plan what you can keep up. Three or four posts a week, so 12 to 16 in the month, is a realistic start for most owner-run businesses.

Can the AI write in two languages?

Yes, most AI assistants can draft in several languages. Ask for each language to be written naturally on its own, and have someone fluent in that language read the draft before it is approved.

Should AI answer my messages too?

Only the simple, repeated questions: opening hours, address, how to book. Complaints, prices and anything about health or safety should always reach a person.
