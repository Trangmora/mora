An AI content calendar for small business is a simple thing: a 30-day plan that says what you will post on which day, with an AI assistant writing the first draft of each post and a person approving it. You can build your first one in an afternoon. This guide goes step by step, with a brief template and a sample week you can copy. It does not depend on any particular app. A spreadsheet and the AI assistant you already use are enough.

What a content calendar is, and why 30 days

A content calendar is a table. Each row is one post: the date, the platform, the topic, the format (photo, short video, carousel, story) and the caption. That is all.

Its value is that you decide ahead of time. Without a calendar, every post starts with "what should we post today?", usually asked at the end of a long shift. With a calendar, you answer that question once a month, when you are rested and can think.

Why 30 days, and not a week or a year?

  • A month matches how a small business runs. Rent, payroll, seasonal menus and promotions already move in monthly cycles.
  • It is long enough to batch. You can take the photos and film the clips for a whole month in one or two sessions.
  • It is short enough to stay true. Plans made three months out go stale. One month ahead, you still know what is really happening in the shop.

Thirty days does not mean thirty posts. Three or four good posts a week that you can keep up are better than a daily schedule you abandon in week two. That makes 12 to 16 posts for the month.

How to choose four or five content pillars

A content pillar is a theme you come back to every week. Pillars stop the calendar from becoming thirty versions of "come visit us". Four or five is enough: with fewer the page repeats itself, with more nobody remembers them.

To find yours, write down the answers to three questions:

  1. What do customers ask before they buy or book?
  2. What do regulars say they love about you?
  3. What happens behind the counter that customers never see?

Then group the answers. For most owner-run businesses, the pillars end up close to these:

  • The work. What you make or do, shown clearly. For a salon with four technicians in a strip mall: a finished set in daylight, with the technician's first name. For a family phở restaurant: the broth pot at 6 a.m.
  • The people. Owner, staff, family. One photo and two honest sentences about who is behind the counter.
  • Proof. A customer's kind words, shared with permission, or a before and after.
  • Helpful answers. Parking, walk-ins, how long a service takes, what to order the first time.
  • Offers and news. New items, seasonal specials, holiday hours. Keep this to about one post in five, so the page does not read like a flyer.

How to brief an AI assistant so it writes in your voice

An AI assistant with no brief writes like a brochure: polite, generic and easy to forget. The fix is not a clever prompt. It is a short written brief, the same one you would give a new employee who is going to write for you. Fill in the template below once, save it, and paste it in at the start of every session.

Part of the briefWhat to writeExample (family phở restaurant)
Who we areTwo or three sentences: what you do, where, and who runs it.We are a family phở restaurant near the business park. My mother cooks the broth, I run the front.
Who we talk toYour two main customer groups and what each one wants.Office workers who need lunch in 30 minutes. Vietnamese families at the weekend.
How we soundThree words for your tone, plus one sentence you would really say.Warm, plain, a little funny. "Come hungry, the bowl is big."
Words to use and avoidNames of dishes or services as you write them. Words you dislike.Write "phở" with the accents. Never "authentic", never "best in town".
LanguagesWhich language for which platform or audience.English first. Add one line in Vietnamese on weekend posts.
Our pillarsYour four or five pillars, one line each.The broth, the family, our regulars, tips for first-timers, specials.
Facts it may useAddress, hours, how to book or order. Only what is true today.Open 11 a.m. to 9 p.m., closed Tuesdays. Walk-ins only.
Hard rulesWhat it must never do.Do not invent prices, discounts, ingredients or reviews. No health claims. If unsure, write [CHECK].
FormatLength, hashtags, call to action.Under 60 words. Three hashtags at most. End with one clear next step.

Two additions make a big difference. First, paste in three to five of your own past posts or messages that sound like you, and say "match this voice". Second, ask for the plan and the captions in two separate steps. Start with "propose 14 post ideas for the next 30 days, spread across my pillars, as a table". Once you have picked the ideas, ask it to write the caption for each one.

If you serve customers in two languages, ask for each language to be written on its own, not translated word for word. Our guide to bilingual marketing for Vietnamese businesses abroad explains when to use which language.

Once posts are approved, most platforms and scheduling tools let you set the date and time in advance, so the week publishes itself. What each platform allows depends on your account type and changes over time, so check what your own accounts offer before you rely on it.

How to review and approve drafts quickly

The AI saves you the writing, not the judgment. A person should read every post before it goes out. Keep one fixed slot a week for it, for example Monday before opening, and run each draft through five questions:

  1. Is it true? Hours, names, ingredients, services. AI assistants can state a wrong detail with full confidence.
  2. Does it sound like us? Read it out loud. If you would not say it to a customer at the counter, change it.
  3. Is there one clear next step? Book, call, come by or send a message. One, not three.
  4. Does the picture match the words? And has everyone in it agreed to be shown?
  5. Is the timing still right? A post about the patio should not go out during a storm.

Give each draft one of three marks: approve, edit or drop. Do not spend ten minutes rewriting a weak draft. Tell the assistant what was wrong ("too formal, shorter, mention the lunch crowd") and ask again. Then add that note to your brief, so the next batch starts better. And let one person have the final yes, so nothing is published by accident.

What to never automate

Scheduling and automatic replies are good at simple, repeated tasks. These need a person every time:

  • Replies to complaints. An unhappy customer needs to feel heard by a human, and an automatic reply usually makes things worse. Answer personally, then offer to continue by private message or phone.
  • Anything about prices. Prices, discounts and gift cards are promises. If an AI draft or an automatic reply gets a number wrong, you either honour it or disappoint someone. A person checks every figure.
  • Anything about health or safety. Allergens, ingredients, hygiene, skin or nail conditions, what is suitable during pregnancy. Never let an AI answer these alone. Say only what you know to be true, and when in doubt invite the customer to call.
  • Sensitive moments. Bad news in your town, a staff matter, a closure. Pause the schedule and write the post yourself.

Automatic replies are fine for facts that rarely change, such as your address, opening hours and how to book, as long as someone updates them when the facts change.

Common mistakes

  • Skipping the brief. Asking for "30 posts for a nail salon" gets you posts that fit any salon, which means they are not really about yours.
  • Publishing without reading. One invented discount or one wrong opening time costs more trust than a week of silence.
  • Using AI pictures of work you did not do. Customers come for what they saw. Show your real nails, your real food and your real team.
  • Planning more than you can keep up. Start with three posts a week. Add more when that feels easy.
  • Selling in every post. When every pillar turns into an offer, people stop reading.
  • Setting it and forgetting it. Read comments and messages every day. A scheduled post still needs someone to answer the people it reaches.
  • Never changing the plan. At the end of the month, see which posts brought messages, bookings or comments, and give those pillars more room next month.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a special tool to make an AI content calendar?

No. A spreadsheet and a general AI assistant are enough to plan and draft a month of posts. Scheduling tools save time later, but the calendar and the brief matter more than the software.

How many posts should a small business plan for 30 days?

Plan what you can keep up. For most owner-run businesses, three or four posts a week, so 12 to 16 in the month, is a realistic start. Regular and true is better than daily and rushed.

Will customers notice that AI wrote the posts?

They notice generic posts, whoever wrote them. When the brief carries your real voice, the facts are checked and the photos are your own, the post is still yours, because the decisions in it are yours.

Can the AI write in both English and Vietnamese?

Yes, most AI assistants can draft in both. Ask for each language to be written naturally on its own, and have someone fluent in each language read the draft before it is approved.

How much work is it each month once it is set up?

The first calendar takes the longest, because you are writing the brief and choosing the pillars. After that, each month needs one planning session, one or two photo sessions and a short weekly review of drafts.

Should AI answer my messages too?

Only the simple, repeated questions: opening hours, address, how to book. Complaints, prices and anything about health or safety should always reach a person.
