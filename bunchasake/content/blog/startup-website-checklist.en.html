A good startup website checklist is short: a home page that says what you do and for whom in one sentence, a page that shows how the product works, honest proof, a pricing page or a clear way to ask about price, a real person to contact, and pages that load fast and work for everyone. Get those right before you add a blog, a careers page or animations. Everything below is in the order we would fix it.

This guide is for founders who are about to launch, about to start selling, or about to send investors and early customers to a site that was put together in a weekend. It works for software, hardware and service startups, and for whichever website builder your team uses. It is a do-it-yourself guide: you can work through it with your co-founder in an afternoon and fix most of it within a week.

Three decisions to make before you touch the design

Most weak startup websites are not badly designed. They are unclear, because the founders have not yet agreed on three things. Write these down first.

  1. Who the site is for. Pick the one visitor who matters most right now. "Operations managers at small physiotherapy clinics" gives you someone to write for. "Businesses of all sizes" does not. Investors, journalists and future hires will visit too, but they can find what they need on the About page.
  2. The one sentence. What it is, who it is for, and what it replaces. For example: "Staff scheduling for small clinics, so the manager stops building next week's rota in a spreadsheet." If you cannot write this sentence yet, our guide on how to position a SaaS product walks you through it.
  3. The one action. Book a demo, start a trial, join the waitlist, or send a question. Choose one main action for this stage and use the same words for it on every page. Other actions can exist, but they should look secondary.

If your name, logo and colours are not settled either, start with our startup branding guide, which covers the minimum brand kit to have in place before launch.

The home page, section by section

Read your home page as a stranger who arrived from a link with no context. Within one scroll, they should know what this is, whether it is for them, and what to do next. A simple order that works for most startups:

  1. Headline. The outcome or the job, in your customer's words. Not "The future of work, reimagined" but "Build next week's clinic rota in one sitting".
  2. Subheadline. One sentence that names who it is for and how it works. "Scheduling software for small physiotherapy and dental clinics."
  3. Main action. One button with a plain label, such as "Book a 20-minute demo" or "Start free trial". Under it, one line that removes a worry, such as "No card needed", but only if that is true.
  4. The product itself. A real screenshot, a short silent clip, or a photo of the device. Visitors trust what they can see.
  5. The problem. Two or three lines describing life before your product, the way customers describe it to you.
  6. How it works. Three steps. If you need seven, the explanation is probably the problem, not the product.
  7. Proof. See the next section.
  8. Common questions. The four or five doubts you hear on sales calls, answered briefly.
  9. The main action again. The same words as at the top.

A quick test: show the top of the home page to someone outside your industry for five seconds, then ask what you do. If they cannot say it back, rewrite the headline before you change anything else.

Proof when you do not have many customers yet

Early-stage founders often leave proof out because they have no famous logos. Small proof that is true works better than big proof that is borrowed. Use what you have:

  • A named early user, with permission. One sentence in their words, with their name, role and type of business. Ask them to approve the exact text.
  • A short pilot story. Who tried it, what they needed, what you changed, what happened. If you have not measured the outcome, describe it in words.
  • The founders' background. "Built by a former clinic manager who did the rota by hand for years" is proof that you understand the problem.
  • Visible progress. A changelog with dates shows the product is being built and that someone is listening.
  • Facts you can stand behind. Integrations you really support, how you handle data, where your team is, how quickly you reply.

Never put a company's logo on your site because one person there signed up for a free trial. Never write a testimonial for a customer, even a friendly one. Visitors notice, and so do the companies whose names you used.

Pricing page basics

Visitors who reach the pricing page are often close to a decision, so it deserves care even if it is short. Whether to show prices is a business decision only you can make, but you can reason it through. If a customer could buy without talking to anyone, show the price. If every deal depends on size and setup, explain what the price depends on and how to get a quote quickly.

Either way, a good pricing page:

  • Names each plan after who it is for, such as "Single clinic" and "Group of clinics", not after metals or animals.
  • Lists in plain words what each plan includes, and what it does not.
  • Marks one plan as the usual starting point, so visitors do not have to work it out.
  • Explains trials, billing, cancelling, and what happens to the customer's data if they leave.
  • Shows the currency and says whether tax is included. Tax rules differ by country, so check with an adviser.
  • Ends with a short FAQ and the same main action as the rest of the site.

If you are still testing prices, say so honestly, and keep every promise you make to early customers.

Speed, accessibility and search basics

These are not polish for later. A page that loads slowly on a phone, or cannot be used without a mouse, loses people before they reach your headline.

Speed

Run your home page, pricing page and signup page through Google PageSpeed Insights, on the mobile setting. You do not need to chase a perfect score. Look at what it flags and fix the usual causes: images far larger than the size they display at, a heavy background video, too many fonts, and chat, tracking or analytics scripts that were added and forgotten. Then test on a real mid-range phone on mobile data, not only on the office Wi-Fi.

Accessibility

The W3C Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) are the official reference. For a small site, start with the checks anyone on the team can do:

  • Every meaningful image has alt text that says what it shows.
  • Text has enough contrast against its background, including light grey text on white.
  • Every link, button and form field can be reached and used with the keyboard alone.
  • Form fields have visible labels, not only placeholder text that disappears when you type.
  • Headings follow a logical order, and link text says where the link goes.
  • Videos have captions.

Legal requirements for accessibility differ by country and sector. If you sell to public bodies or large companies, ask an adviser what applies to you.

Search basics

Give each page its own title and description, one clear main heading, and the words people would actually type to find it. The Google SEO Starter Guide is the official place to learn the rest. We do not sell SEO as a service; these are basics a founder can handle.

Your startup website checklist before launch

Copy this list and go through it the day before you share the link.

  • Message. The headline says the job or outcome. The subheadline says who it is for. A stranger can repeat what you do.
  • Action. One main action, with the same words on every page. Every form sends to an inbox someone reads. You have tested it yourself.
  • Proof. Every quote, logo and name is real and approved. Screenshots show the current product.
  • Pricing. Prices, or a clear explanation of how pricing works. Trial, billing and cancelling explained.
  • People. Founders named, with real photos. A contact email, not only a form.
  • Phone. Every page checked on a real phone. Buttons are easy to tap. Nothing overlaps.
  • Speed. Key pages tested in PageSpeed Insights. Large images compressed.
  • Accessibility. Alt text, contrast, keyboard use, form labels and captions checked.
  • Search. Every page has its own title and description. Searching your company name finds the site.
  • Basics. Privacy and terms in place, a favicon, a sharing image for social links, no broken links, no placeholder text left behind.
  • Language. If your team thinks and writes in Vietnamese or another language first and the site is in English, a fluent reader has checked the main pages.

Common mistakes

  • Writing for investors on a page meant for customers. "AI-powered platform for the future of healthcare" may fit a pitch deck. A clinic manager wants to know whether it fixes her rota.
  • Listing features instead of jobs. Say what the customer gets done, then show the feature that does it.
  • Three competing buttons. "Book a demo", "Start trial" and "Join our community" side by side leave visitors choosing nothing.
  • Borrowed proof. Logos used without permission and made-up quotes cost more trust than they win.
  • Hiding the founders. A young company with no names and no faces looks like a risk.
  • Rewriting the message every week. Change it when customers teach you something, not because you are tired of reading it. Keep a note of what you changed and why.

Frequently asked questions

How many pages does a startup website need at launch?

Often five or six: home, product or how it works, pricing, about, contact or demo, plus the privacy and terms pages. A single strong page can work while you collect a waitlist, as long as it says what you do, for whom, and who is behind it.

Should we show prices if we are still working them out?

If customers can buy without a call, showing a price usually saves everyone time. If you are still testing, explain how pricing works and what it depends on, and say honestly that it may change. Keep every promise you make to early customers.

Is a waitlist page enough before launch?

It can be, if it explains the problem you solve, who it is for, roughly when you expect to launch, and what people get by joining. Add a line about the founders. An email field with a slogan and nothing else gives people little reason to sign up.

Which website builder should we use?

The one your team can update without waiting for a developer. A startup's message changes often in the first year, so being able to edit a headline in minutes matters more than the tool itself. Check that it lets you set page titles, alt text and redirects.

Do small startups really need to think about accessibility?

Yes. It makes the site usable for more people, including customers using a screen reader, a keyboard only, or a small screen in bright sunlight. Legal requirements differ by country and sector, so ask a qualified adviser what applies to you, and use WCAG as the reference for how to do it.
