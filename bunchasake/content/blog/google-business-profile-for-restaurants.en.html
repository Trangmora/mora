Restaurant Google Business Profile optimization means filling in the free listing that shows your restaurant in Google Search and Google Maps so that a hungry person can answer four questions in a few seconds: what do you serve, are you open now, what does the food look like, and how do I order or book. If the profile answers those four clearly and truthfully, it is doing its job. This guide walks through the sections that matter most for a restaurant, with examples for a family-run Vietnamese kitchen.

Two notes first. Google renames, moves and removes features often, and some exist only in certain countries or for certain categories. We describe each one in general terms, so follow what your own dashboard shows. And this is a do-it-yourself guide: Bunchasake does not sell Google Business Profile management, SEO or ads.

Before you start: one profile, owned by the right person

Search your restaurant's name on Google Maps. If a listing already exists, look for a line that lets you claim it. If not, add the business through Google Business Profile. Sign in with a Google account the owner controls, not a server's or a cousin's personal account, and add one trusted person as a backup manager.

Google will then ask you to verify that the restaurant is real. It chooses the method, so do what the screen asks. If you took over the premises from another restaurant, ask the previous owner to transfer the listing or mark it as closed, and never keep two profiles for one address. Guests who arrive at "the old name" and leave a confused review are a problem you can avoid on day one.

Restaurant Google Business Profile optimization, section by section

1. Category: say what kind of restaurant you are

The primary category is the strongest signal of what you are. Type "Vietnamese" into the category field and choose from Google's own list. If your restaurant is known for one thing, such as phở, and the list offers a more specific category for it, weigh which one describes you better. A family place with forty dishes is a Vietnamese restaurant. A shop that sells phở and almost nothing else may fit the narrower one.

Add secondary categories only for what you truly serve every day. A bánh mì counter inside the restaurant can justify a sandwich category. One vegetarian dish does not make you a vegetarian restaurant.

2. Hours: lunch, dinner and the days you close

Restaurants lose more guests to wrong hours than to bad photos. If you close between lunch and dinner, enter two time ranges for the day instead of one long one. If the kitchen stops taking orders before closing time, and your dashboard offers extra hour types for things like the kitchen, takeaway or delivery, use them.

Set special hours for public holidays in your country and for the days a Vietnamese family restaurant really closes: Tết, a family trip home, a private party that fills the room. Do this a week or two ahead. A guest who drives across town to a locked door rarely comes back.

3. Service options and attributes

Attributes are the small labels under your name: dine-in, takeaway, delivery, outdoor seating, wheelchair access, vegetarian options, good for groups, and so on. The list depends on your category and country. Go through all of them once and tick only what is true today. If the patio is closed in winter, untick it in winter.

4. Menu: the section guests open first

Depending on your dashboard, you may be able to add a link to the menu on your website, upload menu photos, type dishes into a menu editor, or all three. Do as many as you can keep up to date.

  • Menu link. Point it to a page on your own website where the menu is text, not only a PDF or a picture, so it opens fast on a phone.
  • Menu photos. Photograph the printed menu flat, in daylight, one page per photo, and replace the photos every time a price or dish changes.
  • Dish entries. Start with your ten best sellers. Write the Vietnamese name with accents, then a plain explanation: "Bún chả Hà Nội: grilled pork patties and pork belly in a warm dipping sauce, with rice noodles and fresh herbs."

An old menu with old prices is the most common cause of an argument at the till. If you cannot update a section, remove it.

5. Ordering and reservation links

Your profile may show buttons to order online or reserve a table. Some are links you add yourself. Others can appear because a delivery or booking company lists your restaurant. Open your profile on a phone as a guest would and tap every button. Check that each one leads to a page that works, shows current prices and belongs to a service you actually use.

If your dashboard lets you manage these links, set your preferred one, for example direct ordering on your own website, and remove any that are wrong. If a link you did not add cannot be removed from the dashboard, the Google Business Profile Help Center explains the current way to report it.

6. Description: three honest sentences

Say what you cook, where the cooking comes from, and who it suits. For example: "Family-run Vietnamese restaurant near the central station. We cook northern-style phở with broth simmered overnight, bún chả and fresh summer rolls. Lunch sets on weekdays, vegetarian options, takeaway available." Write it in the language of your neighbourhood. Do not stuff it with "best phở in town" or a list of nearby districts.

7. Photos: what a hungry person wants to see

Upload your own photos in five groups: the storefront as seen from the street, so people recognise the door; the dining room when it is tidy and lit; your best-selling dishes, one dish per photo; the menu; and the people who cook and serve. Use daylight near a window and photograph the food the way it arrives at the table, not a styled version nobody will receive. Our menu photography tips for restaurants cover the phone settings and angles.

Guests will add their own photos too. You cannot choose them, but you can outnumber the dark, blurry ones by adding a few good photos every month. If a photo shows something that is not your restaurant, report it from the profile.

8. Updates and offers

If your dashboard offers posts or updates, use them for things with a date: this week's special, the Tết menu, a change in hours, a new lunch set. One photo, two sentences, and how to order. One update when you have real news is better than a daily post nobody at the restaurant has time to write.

9. Questions and messages

People ask the same things: Is there parking? Do you take cards? Is the broth made with beef bones? Do you have gluten-free options? Where the profile has a place for questions or messages, check it weekly and answer plainly. For allergy questions, say exactly what you can and cannot guarantee in your kitchen, and invite the guest to call before ordering. Turn messaging off if nobody can reply the same day.

Google Maps for Vietnamese restaurants: two kinds of guests

A Vietnamese restaurant abroad is searched for in two ways. Vietnamese guests type dish names, often with accents: "bún bò Huế", "bánh xèo". Local diners type general words: "Vietnamese food near me", "noodle soup", "pho". Your profile should make sense to both.

  • Keep the business name exactly as on your sign. Do not add "Pho Noodle Soup Restaurant" after it. Editing the name to fit keywords breaks Google's rules and can get a profile suspended.
  • Write dish names correctly in Vietnamese, then explain them in the local language in the same line.
  • Put the regional identity in the description if you have one: northern, Huế, southern, home-style.
  • Reply to reviews in the language the guest wrote in. A reply in Vietnamese to a Vietnamese guest tells the next Vietnamese reader that the kitchen is the real thing.

Reviews, briefly

Ask guests for a review when they pay and say they enjoyed the meal, never offer a free drink or a discount in exchange, and reply to every review within a few days. That is the short version. The longer method, with a way to ask and a reply pattern, is in our restaurant local SEO checklist, together with the work outside the profile: your website, consistent contact details and local mentions.

A fifteen-minute monthly routine

  1. Open the profile on a phone and read it as a stranger.
  2. Check hours for the coming month, including holidays.
  3. Compare the menu on the profile with the menu on the table.
  4. Tap the ordering and reservation buttons.
  5. Add three new photos.
  6. Answer new reviews and questions.
  7. Look at the suggested edits Google shows you, and reject any that are wrong.

Common mistakes

  • Keywords in the name. The name on the profile should match the sign and your receipts.
  • One long opening time. If you close from mid-afternoon, show it.
  • An old menu. Prices from two years ago cost you trust at the till.
  • Stock photos or photos from another restaurant. Guests notice when the bowl that arrives is not the bowl in the picture.
  • Never checking the links. A dead ordering button sends the guest to the next restaurant on the map.
  • A profile owned by someone who left. Make sure the owner's account holds the top access level.
  • Ignoring edits from the public. Anyone can suggest a change to your hours or address, so look at the profile regularly.

Frequently asked questions

Is a Google Business Profile free for a restaurant?

Yes. Claiming, verifying and managing the profile costs nothing. Be careful with callers who say they are from Google and ask for payment to keep your listing active. Manage the profile yourself from your own Google account.

Should I choose "Vietnamese restaurant" or a more specific category?

Choose the one that describes most of what you sell. If guests come for a full menu, the general Vietnamese category fits. If you are known for one dish and Google's list has a category for it, that may describe you better. You can change it later if your menu changes.

Can I write the profile in Vietnamese?

Write the description in the language most of your neighbourhood reads, and use Vietnamese for dish names with a short explanation next to each. Reply to Vietnamese reviews in Vietnamese. That way both groups of guests feel addressed.

A delivery link I never added appears on my profile. What can I do?

Tap it to see where it leads. If your dashboard lets you manage ordering links, remove it or set your preferred link above it. If not, follow the reporting steps in Google's help pages, and contact the delivery company if it lists your restaurant without an agreement.

How often should I add photos?

A few each month is enough for most family restaurants. Add more when something changes: a new dish, a renovated room, a seasonal menu. Regular real photos matter more than one big upload.

Someone changed my hours on Google. How did that happen?

Google lets the public suggest edits, and it sometimes applies changes from other sources. Check the profile regularly, correct anything wrong in your dashboard, and keep your hours identical on your website and other listings so there is less conflicting information.
