A fashion brand identity is the set of signals that lets someone recognise your clothes before they read the name: the look of the photos, the colours around the product, the lettering on the hang tag, the label at the neck. For a small label it is not one design job but six decisions that have to agree: name, logo, colours, typefaces, photo style, and labels and tags. This guide takes them in the order that saves money and ends with a checklist you can copy.

Start with one sentence, not a mood board

Most founders start with a mood board of images they love. It is fun, and it often produces a brand that looks like five other brands. Start instead with a sentence you write for yourself:

We make [what] for [who], and wearing it should feel [how].
  • "We make linen workwear for women who travel for their job, and wearing it should feel calm and put together."
  • "We make modern áo dài for weddings and New Year, and wearing it should feel like a family memory made new."

Add one line about what the brand will never do: never loud logos, never chasing trends. If the story behind the brand is not in words yet, read fashion brand storytelling first. If you are still before your first collection, how to launch a clothing brand covers the order of work.

The name: say it, spell it, find it

  • Say it once. Tell the name to someone who has never heard it and ask them to type it. If they cannot, recommendations will not reach you.
  • Do not tie it to one product. "Mai Linen" is clear today and awkward on the day you make knitwear. A name that holds a feeling, a person or a place grows with the collections.
  • Check the handles. Search Instagram, TikTok and web addresses before you fall in love with the name.
  • A word from your own language can work. A short Vietnamese, Italian or Spanish word that is easy to say gives the brand a story. Decide once whether you write it with accents, and keep that spelling on every label.

Whether a name can be registered as a trade mark depends on the country and the name, so ask a qualified adviser before you order labels. If you sell in Europe, you can see how EU trade marks work at the EU Intellectual Property Office.

The logo: design it for the smallest place first

A fashion logo lives in difficult places: woven into a neck label a few centimetres wide, embroidered on a chest, stamped on tissue paper, shown as a small circle on a phone. None of them shows fine lines well.

  • A wordmark first. The name written in a distinctive way does most of the work. Add a small mark or monogram for a sleeve, a button or a profile picture.
  • Test it in thread. Weaving and embroidery lose detail. Ask your label maker for a sample before you approve the logo, not after a full run.
  • Test it in one colour. If it needs a shadow or gradient, it will fail on a rubber stamp.
  • Get the files. Vector files (SVG, AI or PDF), transparent PNGs, and versions in colour, black and white.

Colours: the brand palette is not the collection palette

This mistake belongs to fashion. Your garments change colour every season. If the brand colour is this season's sage green, next season's rust collection will look like another brand on your own website. Keep two palettes and write both down:

  • Brand palette: one main colour, one or two supporting colours and a neutral, used on labels, tags, packaging, the website and post templates. It must suit any collection, so it is often a deep neutral, off-white or one signature colour.
  • Collection palette: the colours of each drop. It changes freely and lives in the products and campaign images.

Colours shift between a screen, a printed tag and a woven label. When it matters, ask each supplier for a physical proof and compare it in daylight with your swatch.

Typography: two typefaces and the right licence

Use one typeface for the name and headings, and one for product pages, care cards, sizes and prices. Before you commit:

  • Read it small. Print the size and care text at real size on a tag and read it in dim fitting-room light.
  • Check every letter. If you write in two languages, the font must include every accented letter, or accents will fall back to another font and look broken.
  • Check the licence. Some fonts are free for a website but not for logos or products. Ask for written confirmation that the licence covers print, web and goods.

The look of the photos is your strongest identity

People see your photos far more often than your logo. A steady photo style is how someone scrolling recognises you before they see your name. Write a one-page photo guide and give it to everyone who shoots for you, yourself included:

  1. Light. Soft window light, direct sun or a hard flash at night. Pick one default.
  2. Places. A plain wall in one colour, the streets of your city or one studio corner. Repeat the same few.
  3. How people move. Still and calm, walking and laughing, or serious and direct. This says more about who the brand dresses than any slogan.
  4. Framing. Full body for fit, close-ups for fabric and stitching, and one fixed crop for product shots.
  5. Colour treatment. Natural, warm or cool, with the same preset every time.

For every drop, plan clean product shots, on-body shots that show fit, and a few mood images. A phone and good daylight are enough to start. Use the small mark as your profile picture and judge the grid as a whole. Instagram for Business is the official place to read about profile tools, which change from time to time.

Labels, hang tags and the parcel

  • Main label. The name or mark, nothing else. Woven labels feel finished; printed labels are softer on the skin. Choose by fabric, then keep one style across the range.
  • Hang tag. Front: the logo. Back: one line of the brand story, product name, size, price and your handle.
  • Care label. What it must say, such as fibre content, care instructions or country of origin, is set by law and differs by country. In the US, the FTC business guidance is the official place to read about clothing labelling rules; in the EU, start with the Your Europe Business portal. Check the details with a qualified adviser before you print.
  • The parcel. Mailer, tissue, sticker and a small card, consistent rather than expensive. Our guide to packaging design for small brands goes further.

Put it on one page and use it every drop

Gather the answers into a short brand guideline: the sentence, logo files, colour codes, fonts, photo guide and a picture of each label. Share it with your photographer, your label supplier and anyone who posts for you. One clear page makes a small label look established.

Common mistakes

  • Starting with the logo. Without the brand sentence, the designer guesses and you choose by mood on the day.
  • Making this season's colour the brand colour. The next collection then fights the website, tags and packaging.
  • A logo that only works on screen. Thin lines blur when woven or embroidered.
  • A new photo style every shoot. The feed starts to look like several brands.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a designer to build a fashion brand identity?

Not for every piece. You can write the brand sentence, test names and make the photo guide yourself. A designer earns their fee on the logo and the label set, where small details decide whether it works in thread and print.

Should my labels be woven or printed?

Both work. Woven labels feel durable; printed labels are softer and often better for fine fabrics. Order samples of both, sew them into a garment and wear it for a day.

Can I change my identity later?

Yes, but change it all at once: labels, tags, website and profiles together. A piece-by-piece change leaves old and new side by side and confuses returning customers.

What should come first on a small budget?

The brand sentence, the name check, a simple wordmark and the photo guide. They cost little and shape everything else. Custom tissue and printed mailers can wait until the first collections have sold.
