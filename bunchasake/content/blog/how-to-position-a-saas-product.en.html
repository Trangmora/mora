Knowing how to position a SaaS product comes down to five decisions, made in order: who the best-fit customer is, what they use today instead of you, what you do differently that matters to them, which category you belong to, and who the product is not for. Then turn those decisions into a one-line pitch and a homepage message.

This guide is for founders of software products, from a first app to a B2B tool with paying customers, and it covers the message side only. Pricing strategy, contracts and legal terms need their own experts.

What positioning is, and what it is not

Positioning is the context you set so buyers know how to judge your product. Call it "a CRM" and they compare it with every CRM they know. Call it "a job tracker for small cleaning companies" and they judge it on very different things.

Positioning is not a tagline, a logo or a feature list. If the team cannot agree on what the product is and who it is for, no headline will hide that.

How to position a SaaS product in five decisions

One fictional example runs through this guide: a small team building scheduling software for cleaning companies with a few crews. Swap in your own product as you read.

1. Who is the best-fit customer?

Not "small and medium businesses". The best-fit customer understands the value fastest, needs little help and stays. If you have users, find them in your own records: who kept using the product, who said some version of "finally", who recommended you. If you have not launched, talk to people with the problem and notice who feels it most.

Describe them in three lines: who they are, the moment the problem hurts, and what makes them search for help. For the example: owners of cleaning companies with a few crews, who rebuild next week's schedule in a spreadsheet every Sunday evening and start looking for a tool after a crew turns up at the wrong address.

2. What do they use instead of you?

Ask: what would they do if we did not exist? The honest answer is rarely the competitor that looks most like you. It is a spreadsheet, a group chat, a whiteboard, a large all-in-one system they half use, or one person who keeps it all in their head.

List every alternative you hear in interviews, sales calls and cancellation notes, and next to each one, what it does badly for your best-fit customer. This list matters most, because it is the comparison already in the buyer's head.

3. What do you do differently, and why does it matter?

For each alternative, write one difference and the value it creates. "Drag-and-drop calendar" is a feature. "Move a job in seconds and the crew gets a text" is a value. Then note the proof you can show: a demo, a free trial, a customer who agreed to be quoted.

Keep only the differences that matter to the best-fit customer and that you can prove. Two strong ones beat seven nobody remembers.

4. Which category do you belong to?

The category is the shelf buyers put you on. You have three options:

  • An existing category, such as "scheduling software". Easy to understand, but you are compared with the biggest names on that shelf.
  • A narrower version of an existing category, such as "scheduling software for cleaning companies". Still understood at once, and the best-fit customer sees themselves in it. For most early products, this is the strongest choice.
  • A new category, with a name you invent. It can work, but you must teach the market what it means, which takes time and money young companies rarely have.

Use the words buyers type when they look for a solution, not your internal words. Google's SEO Starter Guide is the official place to read how search works.

5. Who is it not for?

Write down who you will politely turn away: companies too big for the product, buyers who need a feature you will not build. Saying it openly makes the right buyer trust the rest of the page and saves your team from demos that go nowhere.

From positioning to a one-line pitch

The one-line pitch is the worksheet pressed into a sentence a customer could repeat to a colleague. A simple pattern:

[Category] for [best-fit customer] that [main value], without [the main pain of the alternative].

For the example: "Scheduling software for cleaning companies that plans the whole week on one screen, without the Sunday-night spreadsheet."

Test it out loud. Say it to someone who fits the description and ask them to explain it back. If they use roughly your words, keep it. If they ask "so it is like a calendar?", the category or the difference is not clear yet.

Turning positioning into the homepage message

The homepage states the positioning; it does not decorate it. A practical order from the top:

  1. Headline: the category and the customer, or the main value. "Scheduling software for cleaning companies."
  2. Sub-headline: the value and the alternative it beats. "Plan the week on one screen. Your crews get a text when a job moves."
  3. Call to action that matches how your buyers buy: a free trial for a product people set up alone, a demo for one that needs a conversation.
  4. One section per difference, each with its proof.
  5. Who it is for, and who it is not for.
  6. The call to action again.

Compare two headlines for the same product. "Empowering service businesses to work smarter" could belong to any product. "Scheduling software for cleaning companies" tells the right buyer they are in the right place. Comparison pages and claims about competitors

Once your alternatives are clear, honest comparison pages are a natural step: you and spreadsheets, you and the big all-in-one suite. Write them for the buyer, and say where the alternative is the better choice. Google's guidance on helpful, people-first content is the official place to read about writing that way.

Claims about competitors must be true, current and backed up. If you sell in the US, the Federal Trade Commission's business guidance is the official place to read about advertising rules. In any market, a qualified adviser should check comparative claims before they go live.

Common mistakes

  • Positioning for everyone. "For businesses of all sizes" usually means for nobody in particular.
  • Leading with technology. "AI-powered" says how it works, not why a buyer should care.
  • Ignoring the spreadsheet. Comparing yourself only with named competitors misses what many buyers actually use.
  • Inventing a category too early. A category name nobody searches for leaves the homepage explaining itself.
  • Different messages in different places. The homepage says one thing, the pitch deck another, the founder's LinkedIn a third.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between positioning and messaging?

Positioning is the set of decisions: who the product is for, what it replaces, how it is different and which category it belongs to. Messaging is how you put those decisions into words on the homepage, in emails and in a pitch. Without positioning, messaging is guesswork.

Should we name competitors on our homepage?

Usually not on the homepage itself. Name the alternative in general terms, such as "still planning in spreadsheets?", and keep named comparisons for dedicated pages, written fairly and checked by an adviser.

How often should a startup change its positioning?

Only when the evidence says so: wrong-fit sign-ups who leave, sales calls spent explaining what the product is, deals won for a reason that is not on your homepage, or a product that has changed. Then repeat the five decisions and update the homepage, the pitch and the sales deck together. Changing it constantly only confuses the market and the team.

Does positioning matter before product-market fit?

Yes, perhaps more. Clear positioning tells you who to talk to and whose feedback to weigh, which makes the search for product-market fit more focused.
