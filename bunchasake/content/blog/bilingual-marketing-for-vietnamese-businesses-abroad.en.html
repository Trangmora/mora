Bilingual marketing for small business does not mean writing every post twice. It means deciding on purpose which language a customer meets in each place, then writing each version the way a native speaker would say it, so the business sounds like the same people in both. If many of your regulars speak Vietnamese, Spanish, Korean or another language at home while your neighbours search in English, this guide shows how to do it when you are also the one running the shop.

The two audiences a bilingual business serves

Stand at the counter of a family phở restaurant for a day and you will meet both.

At noon the lunch crowd comes in from the offices nearby. They found the place on a map app, read a few reviews, looked at the photos and decided in two minutes. On Sunday, three generations of one family fill the big table, chatting in the same language as the kitchen. Nobody searched for anything. An aunt heard about the bún bò from a friend at the temple.

  • Customers who share your language often search and talk in that language, ask relatives and friends, and trust word of mouth more than any advert. They want to know who the owner is, whether the taste is the one they grew up with, and whether they can phone and be understood.
  • Local customers and the younger generation, including children of immigrant families who grew up locally, search in English or the local language, read reviews, compare photos and expect to book online.

The two groups overlap. A daughter who reads the English reviews books the table for a mother who reads only her first language. That is why the two versions of your business have to agree with each other. The same is true for a nail salon or a bakery.

Why translating word for word fails

When the sentence already exists in your first language, it is tempting to paste it into a translation tool and post the result. Four things usually go wrong.

Tone

In many languages, warmth lives in small words that English does not have. In Vietnamese it sits in dạ, nha and ạ, and in calling the customer chị, anh, cô or chú. Translate them literally and you get nonsense. Drop them and the sentence is correct but cold. English shows warmth differently: short sentences, the customer's first name, a thank you that sounds meant.

Humour

Wordplay almost never survives the trip. If a joke works in one language only, use it there and write something plain and friendly in the other.

Dish, product and service names

Do not translate the name of a dish or a signature product. Keep it in its original language, with its accents or marks, and add one line that tells a new customer what will arrive at the table: "Bún chả: grilled pork with rice noodles, herbs and dipping sauce, Hanoi style." A literal version such as "vermicelli with grilled meat" says less and makes the dish sound ordinary.

Services work the other way round. On an English price list, use the words local customers type into a search box, such as "gel manicure" or "acrylic full set", not a translation of what your team calls it among themselves.

Formality

Customer-facing English is casual and polite at once. Many other languages need the right form of address, or they sound careless. And if your local language is German, French or Italian, there is a formal and an informal "you". Ask someone who grew up there which one a business like yours would use.

How to decide which language goes where

One rule covers most cases: the language follows the reader, not the owner. Ask who is looking at this sign, page or message, and what they can read without effort. In the table, "English" may be German, French or Italian depending on where you are, and "community language" is the language your regulars speak at home.

How to keep one brand voice in two languages

A voice is a personality, not a set of words. So do not start from sentences. Start from three words that describe how your business talks, then decide what each one sounds like in each of your languages.

Say the three words are warm, straightforward and a little playful. A short voice guide fits on half a page. Here is one for the language pair in the tone example:

  • How we address customers. Community language: anh chị, cả nhà. English: "you", and first names when we know them.
  • How we refer to ourselves. Community language: tiệm or quán, and em in replies. English: "we".
  • Words we avoid. Stiff phrases such as kính mời quý khách or "dear valued customer".

Then test it on real messages. The facts must be identical. The wording should not be.

Look at the new dish row. The community line does not explain bún bò Huế, because that reader knows it. It says the dish is cooked the Huế way, which is what that reader cares about. The English line explains the dish. Same news, same friendly voice, different details for a different person.

Bilingual marketing for small business: a weekly workflow

  1. Write the news once, in the language you think in. One plain sentence: what is happening, when, and why a customer should care.
  2. Rewrite, do not translate. Ask how you would say it out loud to an English-speaking customer at the counter, and write that down.
  3. Have one checker for each language. A son, daughter or staff member who grew up locally reads the English. You or an older relative reads the community version.
  4. Keep a shared note of fixed phrases. Dish descriptions, service names, opening hours, the five replies you send most. Write each once in both languages and reuse them.
  5. Batch it. One quiet hour a week is easier to protect than ten minutes a day.
  6. Use AI for the draft, people for the decision. A tool can give you a first English version quickly. A person still checks names, prices, tone and forms of address before anything goes out. One odd dish description on a printed menu stays there for a year.

Common mistakes

  • Two languages crammed into one image. The text becomes too small to read on a phone. Make two versions, or put the second language in the caption.
  • Versions that disagree. The community post says closed Monday, the English one says Tuesday. Check dates, prices and hours side by side.
  • Leaving one audience out. If Instagram is all in your first language, local customers scroll past. If everything is English, the community starts to feel the place is no longer for them.
  • Two personalities. Relaxed and funny in one language, stiff in the other. Customers who read both will wonder which one is real.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to post everything in both languages?

No. Post each piece of news in the language of the people it is for. Opening hours, holiday closures and price changes matter to everyone, so those go out in both. A Lunar New Year greeting can lead in the community language, and a post about a new online booking link can be English only.

Should my website be in two languages?

Lead with the language most of your customers search in. Add a second version if the community is a real share of your trade and you will update both. One accurate language is better than two where one is out of date.

My English is not strong. Can I still market in two languages?

Yes. Write in the language you think in first, get a draft from a translation or AI tool, then ask someone who grew up locally to fix it before it goes out. Save the corrected phrases so you can reuse them.

We are in a country where English is not the main language. What changes?

The local language takes the place of English in this guide. Signage, menu, website and listing lead in whatever customers search in, and your first language stays for the community. English becomes a useful third language if your area has tourists.
