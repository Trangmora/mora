If you are asking how to get more nail clients, start with what a stranger sees before ever sitting in your chair: your photos, your reviews, how easy it is to book and how the salon looks from the street. Most salons do good work. Fewer make that work easy to find, easy to trust and easy to book. This guide puts the fixes in order, from the free basics to the habits that turn a first visit into a regular, and ends with a 30-day checklist you can copy.

One honest note first: nothing here is a guarantee. How quickly things change depends on your area, your prices and the work itself. What each step does is remove one reason for someone to pick the salon down the road instead of yours.

What makes people choose a nail salon

Picture a new client. They search "nails near me" on a phone, or a friend sends them your Instagram. They decide quickly, usually on four things.

  • Photos of real work. People want to see nails your technicians did, on real hands, in styles they like. Stock photos and designs reposted from other artists tell them nothing about you.
  • Reviews. Not only the star rating. People read the recent ones to check on cleanliness, on how long a set lasted, and on how the salon answers when something went wrong.
  • How easy it is to book. If the only way is to call and wait while a technician finishes a pedicure, some will give up. A booking link, or a clear "walk-ins welcome, call or text this number", removes the doubt.
  • How the place looks and feels. The sign, the front window, the chairs, the welcome at the door. People judge all of this from photos before they come, and again in the first minute inside.

None of this is about being the cheapest. It is about looking like a safe, pleasant choice.

How to get more nail clients: a prioritised action list

Work through these in order, because each one makes the next work better. There is little point in printing flyers while your Google listing still shows last year's hours.

1. Fix the basics customers see first

  • Google listing. Claim your Google Business Profile, check the name, address, phone and hours, list your services and add a booking link. Our guide to Google Business Profile for nail salons goes through every section.
  • Instagram profile. On your Instagram for Business account, the bio should say what you do, where you are and how to book. For example: "Gel, dip powder and nail art. Northgate Plaza, next to the grocery store. Walk-ins welcome, booking link below." Pin three posts of your best work to the top.
  • Signage. Stand where your customers park and look at your own storefront. Can you read the name from a passing car? Is every letter lit at night? A clean window with the name, the hours and "walk-ins welcome" does more than ten faded posters.
  • Price menu. One menu, identical on the wall, on your website and on Google. Group it simply (manicure, pedicure, enhancements, nail art, extras) and say what each price includes. Nobody likes a surprise when it is time to pay.

2. Give people a reason to remember you

Many towns have several salons with nearly the same name, menu and sign. If a happy client cannot tell a friend which salon it was, the recommendation is lost. A clear brand fixes that, and it has three plain parts.

  • A name people can say and search. If yours is close to other salons nearby, make everything around it distinct, and write the name the same way everywhere.
  • A consistent look. The same logo, colours and style on the sign, the menu, the business card and Instagram, so someone who sees you twice knows it is the same place.
  • A signature service. One thing you want to be known for, with a name: "the 45-minute lunch manicure", "builder gel for weak nails", "hand-painted floral art". It gives clients something specific to talk about.

This is the part we work on at Bunchasake: brand and marketing for nail salons, from positioning to the logo, signage and post templates. You can start on your own today by writing one sentence that says who your salon is for and what it does best.

3. Turn first visits into regulars

Winning a new client takes effort. A regular simply comes back. Three small habits help.

  • Rebook at the chair. Before the client pays, the technician asks: "This set will need a fill in two to three weeks. Shall I hold the same time for you on the 14th?" Asking at the chair works better than hoping they remember.
  • Send a simple follow-up message. A day or two later, to clients who gave their number and agreed to hear from you: "Hi Sarah, thank you for visiting Lily Nails. If anything is not right with your nails, tell us and we will look at it." Keep it short. Rules on marketing messages differ by country, so check what applies where you are.
  • Thank people for referrals. When a new client says who sent them, write it down. Next time that friend comes in, thank them by name, with a card or a small extra such as a free nail art accent.

4. Ask for reviews the right way, and reply to them

The best moment is when the client is admiring the finished nails. Say it plainly: "If you are happy with them, a Google review really helps a small salon like ours." Hand over a small card with a QR code that opens your review page.

  • Ask every client, not only the ones you expect to be happy.
  • Do not offer a discount or gift in exchange for a review. Google does not allow it, and other platforms, such as Yelp for Business, have their own rules, so read them before you ask.
  • Never write reviews yourself, and never ask family to pose as clients.
  • Reply to every review, briefly and politely. Thank happy clients by name. With an unhappy one, apologise for the experience, do not argue, and invite them to call so you can put it right. Future clients read your replies as closely as the reviews.

5. Build local partnerships

Your neighbours already serve the people you want to meet. Walk over and introduce yourself to the hair salon, the bridal shop, the gym, the florist. Start small: swap business cards at the counter, plan a bridal party booking with the dress shop, or share a holiday-season offer with the hairdresser next door. One partner who really mentions you is worth more than twenty who took a flyer.

6. Post what a new client wants to see

You do not need to post every day. You need a profile that answers a stranger's questions: what does your work look like, what does it cost, who will do my nails, how do I book? A simple weekly mix:

  • Two posts of real work, shot in daylight, naming the service and the technician.
  • One post that answers a common question, such as "How long does dip powder last?" or "Do you take walk-ins?"
  • One post about the people or the place: a technician's speciality, the pedicure chairs, how you clean your tools.

Common mistakes

  • Discounting first. A deep price cut tends to bring people who leave when the offer ends. Fix what people see before you lower prices.
  • Posting work that is not yours. A client who asks for the design in the photo and gets something different will not trust the rest.
  • A big push, then nothing. A small routine you keep every week beats one busy month.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to get more nail clients?

There is no single honest answer. Fixing your listing, photos and menu can be done in days. Reviews, regulars and word of mouth build over months. Treat it as a routine, not a campaign.

How can I get more bookings for a nail salon on slow weekdays?

Give weekdays their own reason. Offer a weekday service that suits people nearby, such as an express lunch manicure for office workers, post your open times in Stories that morning, and suggest a weekday slot when you rebook regulars at the chair.

Should I offer discounts to attract new clients?

Use them carefully. A small first-visit extra, such as a free nail art accent, costs little and keeps your prices intact. A large discount can attract people who only come for the deal.

What if my English is not strong?

Prepare your lines once, in writing: the rebooking question, the review request, the follow-up message and two or three review replies. Ask someone you trust to check them, then reuse them. Short, clear and polite matters more than perfect grammar.
