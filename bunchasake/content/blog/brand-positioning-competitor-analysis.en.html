Brand positioning is the decision about who your brand is, who it is for and how it is different from the other places a customer could choose. It is the second step in building a brand, right after listening to the founder's story. Discovery tells you what is true about you. Positioning tells you which part of that truth to lead with, given the customers you want and the competitors around you.

A clear position makes every later decision easier. When you know you are "the careful, unhurried salon for working women who hate rushed appointments", you already know what the photos should show, what the booking page should promise and what to say when someone asks for a discount. This guide walks through how we approach positioning, with a competitor analysis table you can copy and a formula for writing your own positioning statement.

What brand positioning means for a small business

Positioning is not a slogan and it is not a list of services. It is a short set of answers:

  • Who you are: the kind of business, and the character behind it.
  • Who it is for: one main group of customers, described as real people with a real need.
  • Who you differ from: the alternatives the customer is really choosing between, which are not always your direct competitors.
  • How you differ: the one or two reasons to choose you that matter to that customer and that you can prove.

For a small business, the biggest mistake is trying to be for everyone. A family restaurant that wants to be fast, cheap, romantic and healthy at once ends up being remembered for none of them. Choosing a position means choosing what you will not chase, and that can feel uncomfortable. It is also what makes word of mouth possible, because customers can only recommend you for something specific.

How we run the competitor analysis

In our projects, positioning starts with an in-depth competitor analysis. The goal is to see where the brand stands today and which directions are open, then to recommend a direction that fits what the founder told us in step one. The founder's wishes decide; the analysis informs. A gap in the market that the founder has no interest in filling is not a good position for that business.

You can do a simpler version yourself in an afternoon. Here is the method:

  1. List the real alternatives. Pick four to six places your ideal customer might choose instead of you. Include at least one that is not like you at all: for a nail salon, a home kit; for a lunch restaurant, the supermarket meal deal.
  2. Visit as a customer. Book, order or browse. Notice the welcome, the wait, the price list, the cleanliness, how you feel when you leave.
  3. Read what their customers say. Reviews on Google, social media comments and messages are the most honest market research you have. You can find competitors' public reviews through their Google Business Profile listings on Google Maps. Look for words that repeat, both praise and complaints.
  4. Study their channels. What do their website and social pages say first? Which photos do they choose? What do they never talk about?
  5. Fill in the table below, then look across each row for patterns: what everyone says, and what no one says.

The two most useful rows are usually "what customers complain about most" and "what they never mention". If every salon nearby gets reviews about rushed appointments and none of them talks about time, an unhurried service is an open position. If every noodle shop says "authentic" and none explains where the recipe comes from, a real family story is an open position.

How to choose your direction

Once the table is full, you will usually see two or three possible directions. Test each one against three questions:

  • Do customers care? The difference must matter to the people you want, not only to you. Reviews and conversations with regulars are your evidence.
  • Can you prove it every day? A position you cannot deliver on a busy Saturday will turn into bad reviews. Choose something your team already does well or can learn to do consistently.
  • Does it match what the founder wants? If your goal is to stop working weekends, a position built on being open late every night is the wrong one, however open the gap looks.

A direction that passes all three is worth writing down. In our process, we present the analysis and the recommended direction to the founder, and nothing moves forward until the founder approves it.

Positioning statement formula

A positioning statement is an internal sentence, not an advertisement. It keeps you, your staff and anyone who helps you with marketing pointed the same way. Use this formula:

For [specific customer] who [need or frustration], [brand name] is the [type of business] that [main difference], because [proof you can show]. Unlike [main alternative], we [what you do that they do not].

Three examples:

  • Nail salon: For working women who hate rushed appointments, Lumi Nails is the neighbourhood salon that gives every manicure the time it needs, because we book fewer clients per technician and clean every tool in front of you. Unlike the walk-in salons nearby, we never squeeze in an extra client.
  • Family restaurant: For office workers with a short lunch break who want a proper meal, the family kitchen is the noodle shop that serves a slow-cooked broth in minutes, because the broth starts before sunrise. Unlike the supermarket meal deal, every bowl is cooked fresh.
  • Small fashion brand: For women who want a few good pieces instead of a full wardrobe, the brand is the label that makes linen basics in small batches, because each piece is cut and finished by the same small team. Unlike fast fashion, we tell you who made it.

If you sell software or a digital product, the questions are similar but the alternatives are different; our guide on how to position a SaaS product covers that case.

From position to brand story

The final part of step two is the brand story: a few sentences that every channel will retell. It combines the founder's story from step one with the position you just chose. Write it in three lengths:

  1. One line for a sign, a social media bio or a business card.
  2. One paragraph for the website home page or the back of a menu.
  3. A longer version for the About page and for training new staff.

All three must say the same thing. When a customer reads the bio, walks in, and hears a staff member describe the business, the story should feel like one voice. That consistency is what the next step, identity, will turn into a logo, colours, type and a way of speaking. Common positioning mistakes

  • Positioning on price alone. Someone can always be cheaper. Price can be part of a position, but rarely the whole of it.
  • Using words that prove nothing. "Best quality" and "friendly service" appear on every competitor's page. Replace them with something a customer can see.
  • Ignoring the indirect alternatives. Your real competitor may be cooking at home or a home nail kit, not the shop across the street.
  • Choosing a gap the founder does not want. A position you do not believe in will be abandoned within months.
  • Keeping it in your head. Write the statement and the brand story down and share them with your team.

Frequently asked questions

What is brand positioning in simple words?

It is the clear answer to who your brand is, who it is for and why that customer should choose you over the alternatives. Everything you say and show afterwards should repeat that answer.

How many competitors should I analyse?

Four to six is enough for most small businesses, as long as at least one is an indirect alternative. Depth matters more than numbers: visiting and reading reviews teaches more than a long list of names.

What is the difference between a positioning statement and a slogan?

The positioning statement is internal and complete, so your team knows exactly what you stand for. A slogan is a short public line that may come later, built from that statement.

Can I change my positioning later?

Yes, but not every few weeks. Repositioning makes sense when your customers, your market or your own goals have truly changed, and it usually means refreshing the identity and touchpoints too.

Who decides the final direction, the agency or the owner?

The owner. Our role is to analyse competitors deeply and recommend directions; the founder chooses, based on what they want for the business, and approves the result before we move on to identity.
