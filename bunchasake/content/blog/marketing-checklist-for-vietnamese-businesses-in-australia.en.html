This marketing checklist for Vietnamese-owned businesses in Australia covers five areas an owner can check in one afternoon: the online listing, reviews, language, signage and community channels. Each item is a yes or no question. You will not find rules or numbers about Australia here, because those change and differ by state and council. You will find what to look at, in what order, and what to ask an adviser before you spend money.

How to use the checklist

Pick a quiet hour. Take your phone, stand across the road from your shop and pretend you have never been inside. Then go through the five areas below in order, because that is the order a new customer meets you: they search, they read reviews, they read your words, they arrive at the door, and later they hear about you from someone else.

Give each item one of three marks:

  • Done. It is correct today and someone is responsible for keeping it correct.
  • Half done. It exists but is out of date, incomplete or different from one place to another.
  • Not yet. It does not exist.

Fix the half-done items first. A listing with the wrong Sunday hours costs you customers every week and takes five minutes to correct.

1. The listing: can a stranger find you and trust what they see?

Picture a bánh mì bakery on a suburban shopping strip. A worker from the building site nearby searches "banh mi near me" at half past eleven. What appears decides where he eats. Check these points on your own business.

  • You control the listing. You have claimed your Google Business Profile with an email address the business owns, not a former employee's or a relative's personal account.
  • The name matches the sign. Same spelling, same order of words. If the sign says "Sen Nails and Beauty", the listing does not say "Sen Nail Spa".
  • The category is right. Choose the category that describes what most customers come for. The Google Business Profile Help Center explains how categories and other fields work, and the options can change, so look again once or twice a year.
  • Hours are true, including public holidays. Public holidays differ between states and territories. Before each one, set special hours so nobody drives to a closed door.
  • The phone number is answered. Call it yourself from another phone during opening hours.
  • Photos are recent and yours. The shopfront as it looks today, the inside, and your five best products or services. No stock photos.
  • Other places agree. Your Facebook page, Instagram bio, website and any delivery or booking apps you use show the same name, address, phone and hours.

Search your own business name and your main product plus your suburb on a phone that is not logged in to your account. What you see is what a stranger sees.

2. Reviews: are you asking, and are you answering?

  • You have a way to ask. A small card at the counter with a QR code to your review page, and one sentence staff are comfortable saying: "If you were happy today, a short review really helps a small shop like ours."
  • You ask everyone the same way. Do not ask only the customers you expect to be kind, and do not offer a discount or gift in return for a review. Platforms have rules about this and so may the law, so ask an adviser if you are unsure.
  • Every review gets a reply. Thank the good ones by name and mention what they had. For a poor one, stay calm, say sorry for the experience, state one fact if needed and invite them to contact you directly.
  • Replies sound like you. Reply in English to English reviews and in Vietnamese to Vietnamese reviews.
  • You read them for lessons. If three reviews mention the wait on Saturday, that is a staffing note, not a marketing problem.

A copyable reply for a good review: "Thanks so much, Emma. Glad you loved the lemongrass chicken roll. See you next time." For a poor one: "Hi Mark, we are sorry your order took so long on Friday. That is not how we want lunch to go. Please call the shop and ask for Linh so we can make it right."

3. Language: do your words fit the people reading them?

Most Vietnamese-owned businesses in Australia serve two groups at once: the wider local public and the Vietnamese community. Each needs to feel the business is talking to them.

  • Your English uses local words and spelling. Listen to how customers ask for things and copy that on your menu and posts. If your text was written by a cousin in another English-speaking country, read it aloud to a local regular and ask what sounds odd.
  • Vietnamese names keep their marks and get a short description. "Bún bò Huế, spicy beef and lemongrass noodle soup." The name stays yours, and the newcomer knows what to order.
  • Prices and units are clear. Dollars written the same way everywhere, and sizes or durations stated: "Shellac manicure, 45 minutes".
  • You have chosen where Vietnamese goes. Usually in community groups, in messages to Vietnamese customers and on posts around Tết. The shopfront and the listing lead with English.
  • One person checks both languages. A post with a spelling mistake in either language tells that group they come second.

4. Signage and shopfront: does the street know what you sell?

  • Readable from a passing car. The name and what you are, in a few large words. "Phở Mận, Vietnamese noodle house" beats a full menu printed on the window.
  • The sign shows your name. Not the previous owner's, and not a faded version of yours.
  • Hours on the door match hours online. Check them side by side.
  • The window is not a noticeboard. One current offer at most. Remove handwritten notes from last year.
  • The first thing inside is useful. A menu or price list a new customer can read without asking, in the same colours and lettering as the sign.
  • It works after dark. If you trade in the evening, look at the shopfront at night.

Before you change a sign, add a footpath board or light anything up, ask your landlord and your local council what is allowed. Rules differ from place to place, and a sign you have to take down is money lost twice.

5. Community channels: do the people near you know you exist?

Online tools matter, but an owner-run business grows through people who already trust each other. Check both communities.

The Vietnamese community

  • You know which Facebook groups, Zalo groups or community pages Vietnamese people in your city actually read, and you have read their rules about business posts.
  • You have considered community newspapers, radio and newsletters in Vietnamese where they exist in your area.
  • You show up at community moments such as Tết and Mid-Autumn events, with a stall, a sponsorship or simply your staff in uniform.
  • You have a simple way for Vietnamese customers to message you, for example WhatsApp Business or the app your regulars already use. Do not guess. Ask ten customers which app they prefer.

The local neighbourhood

  • You are in the suburb's community group and you answer when someone asks for a recommendation, without pushing.
  • You know the nearby schools, sports clubs, offices and building sites, and when their people are free.
  • You have one neighbour business you swap recommendations with: the hairdresser next to the nail salon, the café next to the grocer.

One more check: the calendar is upside down

If you or your content helper learned marketing from pages in Vietnam, the United States or Europe, remember that the seasons in Australia are reversed. Summer falls over December and January, so Christmas and Tết arrive in hot weather, and winter comes in the middle of the year. A bubble tea shop that copies a "cosy winter drinks" post from an American account in December looks lost.

  • Your content plan follows the local seasons, school terms and holidays in your state.
  • You plan one cultural moment per season: Tết in summer, Mid-Autumn in spring.
  • You know your own busy and quiet weeks from last year's sales, and you schedule offers for the quiet ones.

What to confirm with an adviser

This guide deliberately does not state Australian law. Rules differ by state, territory and council, and they change. Before you act, ask an accountant, a solicitor, your industry association or your local council about:

  • registering and protecting your business name;
  • what your signs, footpath boards and window displays may show and where;
  • how prices must be displayed and what an advert may claim;
  • running competitions, giveaways and discounts;
  • asking for reviews and using customer photos;
  • sending marketing texts, emails or messages, and keeping customer details.

Common mistakes

  • Doing the fun part first. A new logo or a paid advert before the listing and hours are right sends people to a wrong first impression.
  • Copying a relative's marketing from another country. The words, the seasons and the rules are not the same. Borrow the idea, then check each detail locally.
  • Speaking to only one community. All Vietnamese loses the neighbourhood. All English with no Vietnamese warmth loses the people who would have recommended you first.
  • Nobody owns the checklist. If every item belongs to "someone", none get done. Write a name and a date beside each.
  • Checking once. Hours, staff and menus change. Repeat the afternoon every three months.

Frequently asked questions

How long does this marketing checklist take?

The check itself takes about an afternoon for a single shop. The fixes take longer, so sort them into three piles: five-minute corrections you do today, jobs for this month such as new photos, and bigger decisions such as a new sign that need a quote and advice first.

Should my posts be in English or Vietnamese?

Lead with English where the public finds you, such as the listing, the shopfront and your main feed, and use Vietnamese where the community gathers and around cultural moments. The best guide is your own customer list: look at who comes in each week and write for them.

Which social platform should a Vietnamese-owned business in Australia use?

Use the one your customers already open, and you find that out by asking them at the counter. Start with one platform you can keep up every week, and add a second only when the first has become routine.

Do I need a website if I have a Google listing and Facebook page?

Many small shops start without one. A simple one-page site becomes useful when customers need information the listing cannot hold well, such as a full menu, a price list or a booking link, and it gives you one place you fully control.

Can I offer a discount for a review?

Do not, unless a qualified adviser has confirmed it is allowed and the platform's own rules permit it. Asking every customer politely, with a QR code at the counter, is safer and builds reviews that sound real.
