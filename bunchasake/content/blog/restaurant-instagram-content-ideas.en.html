The best restaurant Instagram content ideas are not the clever ones. They answer what a hungry person nearby wants to know: what does the food look like, who makes it, and can I get a table tonight? This guide gives you 24 ideas in six groups, each with a hook or caption you can borrow, plus a plain way to film with a phone and a 2-week plan. The examples use dishes like phở and bánh mì, but they work in any kitchen.

What your Instagram profile must contain first

Most people who find you look at the profile for a few seconds, then decide. Before you post anything new, check five things.

  • Bio. What you cook and where, in one line: "Family-run Hanoi kitchen near Central Station. Phở, bún chả, cà phê sữa đá." Add a second line in another language if many of your regulars speak it.
  • Hours. Put them in the bio or a pinned post and update them for holidays. A closed door after a long drive is hard to forgive.
  • Address. Switch to an Instagram business account, which is free, so the address and a call or directions button can show. Add a landmark too: "next to the post office".
  • Menu and reservation link. One link that opens the current menu and the way to book or order. In some countries, and with some booking tools, Instagram also offers reserve or order buttons, so check what your account shows, or look it up in the Instagram Help Center.
  • Highlights. Four are enough: Menu, Find us, How to eat it, Reviews.

24 restaurant Instagram content ideas, grouped by type

Six groups, four ideas each. You do not need them all: one from each group fills a week. Write captions in English or the local language first. If many of your regulars speak Vietnamese, Spanish or another language, add a short line for them when the post concerns them, such as "Hôm nay có bún bò Huế, cả nhà ghé nhé." The dish

  • The hero bowl. One phở, steam rising, nothing else on the table. Hook: "This broth started at 5 this morning."
  • The pour. Broth over noodles, or coffee dripping onto condensed milk. Caption: "Cà phê sữa đá, the slow way."
  • What is inside. Cut a bánh mì in half and name each layer: pâté, pickles, herbs, grilled pork. Hook: "Five layers, one bite."
  • How to eat it. Show bún chả done right: noodles dipped into the bowl, a little at a time. Hook: "First time with bún chả? Do this."

The making-of

  • The morning pot. Bones, charred ginger and onion going into the stockpot. Hook: "What happens six hours before your lunch."
  • The grill. Pork over charcoal with the real sound and no music. Caption: "You can smell this from the street."
  • Hands at work. Rolling gỏi cuốn or picking herbs, filmed close. Hook: "Rolled by hand, every morning."
  • One ingredient. The fish sauce, the baguette or the coffee beans, and why you chose it. Hook: "Why we will not change our fish sauce."

The people

  • Meet the cook. A photo at the stove and three lines: name, home town, proudest dish. Hook: "The person behind your phở."
  • Staff meal. What the team eats before the doors open. Hook: "What we eat before you arrive."
  • Staff pick. One server, one favourite, one reason. Caption: "Minh's order: bún chả, extra herbs."
  • A day in ten clips. From lifting the shutters to wiping the last table. Hook: "A Saturday in ten seconds."

The story and origin

  • Why this dish. The owner on camera for twenty seconds. Hook: "My mother sold this bánh mì from a cart in Đà Nẵng."
  • The region. A map or an old photo, and what makes Hanoi, Huế or Saigon cooking different. Hook: "Phở in the north and in the south is not the same bowl."
  • Then and now. A family photo beside today's kitchen. Caption: "Same recipe, different continent."
  • The name. What the restaurant's name means and how to say it. Hook: "How to pronounce our name."

Only write what is true for your kitchen. A real detail beats a pretty sentence.

The guests

  • First bite. A guest trying a dish for the first time, filmed with their permission. Hook: "His first bún chả."
  • A regular's order. No face needed, only the tray. Caption: "Every Friday: phở tái, no onions, extra lime."
  • A review on screen. A kind review with your thank-you. Caption: "This made our week. Thank you."
  • Guest photos. Repost a guest's photo or Story after asking, and credit them. Caption: "Thank you for this one, @name."

Practical info and specials

  • Today's special. One photo, the price and the limit. Caption: "Today only: bún bò Huế, until the pot is empty."
  • How to order. Walk in, call or use the link, shown in three steps. Hook: "Three ways to get our bánh mì."
  • Lunch for the office. What travels well and how to order for a group. Caption: "Order by 11, pick up at 12."
  • Hours and closures. Holiday hours, Tết, a private event. Caption: "Closed Monday for Tết. Back Tuesday."

How to film food with a phone

  1. Wipe the lens. Kitchen grease makes every video look foggy.
  2. Go to a window. Put the dish on a table beside it and let daylight come from the side. Switch off ceiling lights that turn the food yellow, and skip the flash.
  3. Choose one hero dish. One bowl, chopsticks, a clean table. A crowded table has no star.
  4. Catch the steam. Film in the first seconds after the dish lands, with something dark behind it so the steam shows.
  5. Hold the phone upright and steady. Elbows on the table, five to eight seconds per clip.
  6. Take three angles. From above, from where a guest sits, and close on one detail such as the herbs or the pour.
  7. Keep the real sound. The sizzle, the ladle, the ice.

Stories routines for lunch and dinner service

Stories disappear after a day, which suits one message: we are open and cooking right now. Give the job to one person and keep to three Stories per service.

Lunch

  • Before opening: the pot or the prep table. "Broth is ready. Doors open at 11:30."
  • First order out: the first bowl of the day, with the special written on screen.
  • Mid-service: one honest update. "Bánh mì nearly gone" or "Tables free after 1:30".

Dinner

  • Late afternoon: the grill being lit, with tonight's special and a link to book.
  • During service: the room filling up, filmed from behind so no faces show.
  • Closing: a thank-you and tomorrow's hours.

Save the best ones to your highlights.

Common mistakes

  • Only finished plates. People follow people. Show hands and faces.
  • The menu as a photo of tiny text. Nobody can read it on a phone. Link to it instead.
  • Filming guests without asking. Always ask, and never post children without a parent's clear yes.
  • Ten posts in one week, then a month of silence. Steady wins.
  • One language only. If half the street reads English, a caption only in your home language leaves them out.

Frequently asked questions

How often should a restaurant post on Instagram?

Three feed posts a week and Stories on the days you are open is a rhythm most small teams can keep. Posting steadily matters more than posting a lot, so choose a pace you can hold in your busiest month.

What should a restaurant post when nothing is new?

Go back to the six groups. The same bowl of phở can be shown as a dish, as a making-of, through the cook, through its origin, through a guest and as today's special. Nothing has to be new, it only has to be shown from another side.

Do I need a photographer or a professional camera?

No. A recent phone, a clean lens and a window are enough for daily content. A professional shoot can be worth it for the menu and the website, but everyday posts feel more real when they come from your own kitchen.

Can I post photos and videos of my guests?

Ask first, every time. Rules about people's images differ from country to country, and a simple "Is it okay if we post this?" keeps you on the safe side and keeps the guest happy. Leave children out unless a parent clearly agrees.
