A nail salon marketing budget is not one number you look up. It is a short plan with three parts: what you will fix for free, what you will pay for once, and what you will spend each month on small tests. This guide shows how to build that plan from your own numbers and how to check each month whether the money did anything.

A plain note before we start: Bunchasake does not run ads or manage marketing spend for clients, and you will not find benchmark figures or price lists here. Every salon has a different rent, area and team, so a figure borrowed from somebody else would be a guess. You will learn a way to decide instead.

Why no single number is right

Picture two salons with four technicians each. One is new, in a strip mall where nobody knows it yet, with chairs empty most weekdays. The other has been on the same corner for years and is full by Thursday. The first needs people to find it. The second needs its regulars to keep coming and its slow Monday filled. The same amount spent the same way would be wrong for at least one of them.

What matters is what your salon is missing and what it can afford. Both answers come from counting.

Step 1: Count before you spend

For four weeks, keep a sheet at the front desk and write down four things:

  • New clients each week. People visiting for the first time.
  • Returning clients each week.
  • How new clients found you. Ask everyone, "How did you hear about us?" and tick a column: Google, Instagram, walked past, a friend, other.
  • Empty chair hours. Which days and times technicians sit without a client.

This costs nothing and it is the most valuable part of the plan. It shows where clients already come from, so you can do more of that, and when you have room for more.

Step 2: Work out what a regular is worth to you

Take what a typical regular spends per visit and multiply it by how many times they come in a year. Use your own booking records. Then remember that supplies and a technician's pay come out of that figure.

You now have a reference point. When an idea costs a certain amount and brings in a certain number of clients who stay, you can see whether it was worth it. Without this, every expense feels either too expensive or "probably fine".

Step 3: Sort every cost into three buckets

Fix: free, costs only time

Claiming and completing your Google Business Profile, setting up your Instagram for Business profile, taking real photos with a phone, making prices match everywhere, replying to reviews, asking clients to rebook before they pay. Our guide on how to get more nail clients lists these in order. Until this bucket is done, the other two work badly.

Foundation: paid once, used for years

A sign that can be read from the car park, a clear printed price menu, a logo and colours that match on everything, a simple website or booking page, business and referral cards. These are not monthly costs. Plan them one at a time and spread them across the year if you need to. For the brand part, see nail salon logo and branding.

Fuel: small amounts each month

Reprinting cards, a booking or messaging tool with a monthly fee, a seasonal window display, a local sponsorship, a small paid ad test. This is the part most people mean when they say "marketing budget", and it should come last.

What to spend on first

  1. Anything broken that every client sees. A sign with a dead letter, a menu with handwritten corrections, a booking link that fails.
  2. Keeping the clients you have. Rebooking, reminders and a thank-you for referrals. A client who returns costs far less effort than a stranger you still have to convince.
  3. Being recognisable. The same name, look and tone on the sign, the menu, the cards and your profiles.
  4. Low-cost local ideas. Referral cards, neighbours, community groups.
  5. Paid ads, as a small test. Only when you have empty chairs and a way to count. Our nail salon advertising ideas guide explains how to run that test.

How to set the monthly amount

  1. Start from what is left. Take a normal month's income and subtract rent, pay, supplies, insurance and what you set aside for tax. Marketing comes out of what remains, never out of rent or payroll. Your accountant can help you see this figure clearly.
  2. Choose an amount you could lose. Some tests will bring nobody. Pick a figure that would be disappointing to lose, not dangerous.
  3. Keep it steady. Hold the same amount for a few months. A budget that jumps up after a good week and disappears after a bad one teaches you nothing.
  4. Split it in two. Put the larger part into things you have already seen work in your own count, and a small part into one new test.
  5. Give every item a job and a count. If you cannot say what an expense should change and how you will see it, do not spend it yet.

Review it once a month

Take twenty minutes with the worksheet and the front desk tally. For each item, ask three questions. Did new clients mention it? Did those clients come back? Did it fill the empty hours you wanted filled?

  • Keep what brought clients who returned.
  • Change one thing where the result is unclear, then give it another month.
  • Stop what brought nobody after a fair try, and move that money to something that worked.

Count hours as well as money. If the owner spends every Sunday evening on posts, that is a cost. Decide who does what, and how many hours a week the salon can honestly give.

Common mistakes

  • Copying a number from someone else. Another salon's budget fits another salon's rent, area and clients.
  • Paying for ads while the free bucket is unfinished.
  • Spending everything on new clients and nothing on regulars.
  • Buying tools nobody uses. Before adding a monthly fee, decide who will use the tool every week.
  • Not counting. Without the tally sheet, every decision is a feeling.
  • Stopping all marketing in a slow month. Cut the tests that did not work, and keep the habits that bring clients back.

Frequently asked questions

How much should a nail salon spend on marketing?

There is no honest figure that fits every salon. Start from what is left after your fixed costs, choose an amount you could afford to lose, and adjust it each month according to how many new and returning clients it brings.

Should a new salon spend more than an established one?

Often a new salon has more one-time foundation costs, such as a sign, a menu and a logo, because nothing exists yet. An established salon usually spends more on keeping regulars and filling slow times. Your own count tells you which case you are in.

What if I have no money for marketing at all?

Then work only on the fix bucket. Profiles, photos, matching prices, review replies and rebooking before the client pays cost time, not money, and they come first even for salons that do have a budget.

Is it worth paying for a logo and brand before paying for ads?

In most cases the order makes sense, because ads only send people to look at you, and what they see decides whether they book. It does not need to be large. A clear name, a consistent look and a readable menu are enough to start.

Can I deduct marketing costs from my taxes?

Tax rules differ by country and by how your business is set up. Keep every receipt and ask your accountant or a qualified tax adviser.
