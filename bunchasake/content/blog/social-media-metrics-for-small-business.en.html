Social media metrics for a small business do not need to be many. Each week an owner only needs five numbers: reach, saves and shares, action taps to contact you or get directions, messages and bookings that came from social media, and the best and worst post of the week. Fifteen minutes is enough. This guide explains each number, shows where to find it, gives you a table to copy, and says plainly which numbers to ignore so you do not get lost.

5 social media metrics a small business should check every week

1. Reach

The number of different people who saw your content during the week. It tells you whether you are present at all. Each platform uses its own name, such as reach, accounts reached or views, and the names change over time. Do not focus on the absolute figure. Look at whether it is up or down compared with the last few weeks.

2. Saves and shares

A like takes half a second. A save means the customer wants to see it again. A share means they want someone else to see it. These two numbers tell you whether the content is truly useful, and they often predict which posts will bring customers in.

3. Action taps

Customers tap through to your profile, a link, the call button, the message button or directions. If you have a physical location, do not forget the performance section of your Google profile: calls, direction requests, website visits. These are people who have moved from "saw it" to "planning to come".

4. Messages and bookings from social media

This is the number closest to revenue. Count the new conversations in the week, and the bookings or orders that started there. The most reliable measurement is the manual one: ask every new customer "How did you hear about us?" and write it down.

5. The best post and the worst post

Each week pick the post that performed best and the one that performed worst, and ask why. Topic, image, opening line, time of posting. After a month you have eight posts to compare, and you start to see what your customers like.

Where to find these metrics

Facebook, InstagramThe insights section in Meta's business tools. Instagram needs a business or creator account to show data.
TikTokThe analytics section of your account; see also TikTok for Business.

Platforms often rename sections and move menus. If you cannot find the exact name above, look for the section called insights, analytics or performance. All of them are free.

The 15-minute weekly review table

Copy this table into a notebook or a spreadsheet. Fill it in once a week, on the same day, at the same time.

MetricThis weekAverage of the last 4 weeksNotes
ReachWhat was different this week: a holiday, rain, a promotion?
Saves and sharesWhich post was saved most?
Action tapsCalls, messages, directions, link taps
New messages and bookingsHow did new customers say they found you?
Best post, worst postWhy?

The notes column matters as much as the numbers. Three months from now you will not remember which week you were closed for repairs.

How to read the numbers and decide

  • Compare with yourself. Compare this week with the average of your last four weeks. Do not compare with a large brand that has a different team and budget.
  • Three weeks make a trend. One weak week may just be rain. Three weak weeks in a row deserve an explanation.
  • One post means nothing. A single post that suddenly does well is usually luck. Three posts of the same kind doing well is a signal.
  • Make exactly three decisions a week. Do more of one kind of post that is winning, drop one kind that is losing, and try one new thing.

Looking at numbers without changing anything is not worth the time. The purpose of these fifteen minutes is those three decisions, and they go straight into next week's content calendar.

The numbers to ignore at the start

  • Total page likes. It grows slowly and has almost nothing to do with whether customers came this week.
  • Followers day by day. A few up or down each day is normal. Monthly is enough.
  • Video views in the first few seconds. People scrolling past are counted too.
  • Unsourced "industry averages". You do not know who was measured, or when.
  • Competitors' numbers. You see only the surface, not what they spent to get there.

A simple A/B test for a small business

An A/B test means making two versions of the same idea and seeing which does better. For a small business, keep three rules.

  1. Change one thing at a time. Same topic, same image, a different opening line. Or the same words with a different image.
  2. Post under the same conditions. The same time slot, the same day of the week, in two consecutive weeks.
  3. Three pairs before you conclude. One pair is not enough to trust.

How AI helps with reading the numbers

  • Summaries. Paste four weeks of your table into an AI assistant and ask: what was unusual this week, which post stood out, and why.
  • Suggestions. Ask it to propose three actions for next week based on the data. You choose, not the AI.
  • Message summaries. What customers asked most in a week is data too; see our guide to the AI chatbot for customer messages.

There are two limits. AI does not know what happened in the real world, such as rain, roadworks or a holiday closure, so you have to tell it. And never paste customers' names, phone numbers or private messages into an AI tool. Common mistakes

  • Checking every day. Daily numbers only make you anxious. Look weekly.
  • Chasing likes. The post with the most likes is not necessarily the one that brings customers.
  • Looking without writing it down. Without the table there is nothing to compare.
  • Concluding from a single post.
  • Forgetting to ask customers. The most accurate number is at the counter, not in an app.

Frequently asked questions

Which social media metric matters most for a small business?

Messages and bookings that came from social media, because that is closest to revenue. The other metrics help you understand why that number goes up or down.

How often should I check the numbers?

Once a week, for about fifteen minutes, on the same day. Checking daily tends to make you react to movements that mean nothing.

Do likes still matter?

They show that a post was noticed, but it is a weak signal. Saves, shares and messages say more about whether customers intend to come.

Is a small follower count a problem?

Not necessarily. A neighbourhood business needs the right people nearby, not a large crowd. A few hundred followers who live in the area are worth more than a few thousand somewhere else.

Do I need a paid tool to track these metrics?

Not at the start. Each platform's free insights and a spreadsheet are enough for these five numbers. A paid tool only becomes worthwhile when you manage many channels or many branches.
