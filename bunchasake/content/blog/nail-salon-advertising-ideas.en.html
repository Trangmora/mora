The most useful nail salon advertising ideas are small and local: a storefront people can read from the car, a card a happy client hands to a friend, a post in the neighbourhood group, and only then a paid ad shown to people who live nearby. This guide goes through them in that order, from free to paid, and shows how to tell whether each one worked.

One thing to say plainly: Bunchasake does not run ads for clients. This is a do-it-yourself guide. What we do is brand and marketing for nail salons, which is the part that decides what your ads have to show.

Fix the free things first

An ad does one job. It makes a stranger look at your salon. If what they find is an old Google listing, a price menu that does not match and three blurry photos, you paid to show them a reason to go elsewhere.

So before any of the ideas below, check four things: your Google Business Profile is claimed and correct, your Instagram shows recent real work, your prices are the same everywhere, and a new client can book without waiting on the phone. Our guides to how to get more nail clients and Google Business Profile for nail salons cover these step by step, so they are not repeated here.

Eight low-cost nail salon advertising ideas

1. Treat the storefront as your biggest ad

People pass your door all day, for free. Give them one message they can read in three seconds: "Walk-ins welcome today", or "Gel manicure, pedicure, nail art". If your landlord and local rules allow it, a standing sign on the pavement with today's open times works well in a strip mall, where people are already parked.

2. Print referral cards that do the talking

Give every happy client two small cards. One side shows the salon name, address and booking QR code. The other has a line to write on: "Sent by ____". When a new client brings the card, you know who to thank, and you have counted one result. Decide the thank-you in advance and keep it simple, such as a free nail art accent for both.

3. Say hello to the neighbours within walking distance

Apartment buildings, offices, the gym, the dental practice. Ask the front desk whether you may leave a few cards or a small printed menu. Always ask first, and never put flyers on cars or in mailboxes without checking local rules. A short personal visit beats a stack of paper.

4. Post in local community groups

Many neighbourhoods have a Facebook group or a similar local app. Read the group rules, because many allow business posts only on certain days. Then post like a neighbour, not like an advert: "Hi, we are the nail salon next to the bakery on Main Street. We have opened Monday evenings for people who work late. Here is this week's work." Add two real photos.

5. Use Vietnamese community channels, and the local language too

If Vietnamese families live nearby, a notice at the Vietnamese grocery, the temple or church bulletin, or a community newsletter reaches people who will feel at home with you. Write that notice in Vietnamese. Everything facing the street stays in the language of the area.

6. Plan one small campaign per season

Prom, wedding season, summer holidays, back to school, the winter holidays. Pick one, choose one service to feature, set start and end dates, and use the same photo and sentence on the window, on Instagram and on the card at the register. One message repeated in three places works better than three different ones.

7. Give your slowest day its own offer

Look at your booking book and find the slowest half-day. Create a named, limited reason to come then, for example "Tuesday afternoons: a free hand massage with any pedicure". An added extra protects your prices better than a discount, and a named day makes it easy to see whether the chairs filled up.

8. Sponsor something small and local

A school raffle, a charity run, a youth sports team. A gift certificate as a prize puts your name in front of local families and brings one new person through the door. Choose causes your own clients care about.

Partnerships with nearby shops belong on this list too. They are covered in the more-clients guide linked above.

When paid ads make sense

Paid ads are neither a must nor a mistake. They suit some situations. Go through this list honestly:

  • You have empty chairs at times you can name. If you are already full on Saturdays, an ad that brings Saturday walk-ins only creates waits.
  • The free things above are done.
  • You have one clear offer or service to show, with a real photo of your own work.
  • The ad can send people to a booking link or a phone number that someone answers.
  • You have a way to count who came because of it.
  • The money is an amount the salon can lose without trouble if nothing comes of it.

If any line is a "no", fix that first. A newly opened salon, a new location or a new service that locals do not know about are the usual moments when a paid test is worth trying.

How to test a paid ad small

  1. Pick one platform. Choose the one your clients already use. The ad tools in Meta for Business cover Facebook and Instagram, and TikTok for Business covers TikTok. Menus and options change often, so follow the platform's own instructions.
  2. Keep it local. Show the ad only to people in the area your clients really come from. Ask yourself how far someone will drive for a manicure.
  3. One photo, one sentence, one action. A real set from your salon, what it is, where you are, and "Book online" or "Call us".
  4. Fix the amount and the end date before you start. Write both down. Do not add money halfway because the numbers on the screen look exciting.
  5. Add a way to count. Put a phrase in the ad, such as "mention Spring Set when you book", and ask every new client: "How did you hear about us?" Keep a tally sheet at the front desk.
  6. Judge it by people, not by likes. Views and likes are not clients. Count the new clients who came from the ad, then check how many booked again. Compare what they spent with what the ad cost, and with a normal week without an ad.
  7. Decide. Stop, repeat as it is, or change one thing and test again. Change only one thing at a time, or you will not know what made the difference.

Keep every ad honest

  • Show only work done in your salon.
  • Ask clients before you use photos of their hands or faces.
  • If you state a price or an offer, state the conditions and the end date as well.
  • Do not promise how long a set will last on every client, and do not make health claims.

Advertising rules differ by country. In the US, the FTC business guidance is the official place to read about them. Elsewhere, or if you are unsure, ask a qualified adviser.

Common mistakes

  • Paying for ads before the basics are fixed. The ad works, the profile does not, and the visitor leaves.
  • Trying five ideas in the same week. When more clients come, you cannot tell which idea brought them.
  • Counting likes as results. Only people in chairs count.
  • Advertising a deep discount. It tends to bring people who come once, for the price.
  • Showing the ad to a whole city. Most clients come from nearby.
  • Stopping the count after the first visit. An idea that brings regulars is worth far more than one that brings one-time visitors.

Frequently asked questions

What is the cheapest way to advertise a nail salon?

Your storefront and your existing clients. A clear window message and referral cards cost little, reach people who are already near you, and are easy to count.

Should I use Facebook and Instagram ads or Google ads?

There is no single right answer. Ads on social platforms show your work to people who were not looking, while search ads appear when someone is already searching. Start with the platform your current clients say they use, test small, and count.

How long should I run a test?

Long enough to cover your normal booking rhythm, including a weekend and a slow day, and long enough for first-time clients to come due for their next visit. Set the end date before you begin.

Do flyers still work?

They can, when they go to the right few places with permission and carry something you can count, such as a phrase to mention. A flyer left everywhere with no way to track it teaches you nothing.

How do I know whether an ad worked?

Count the new clients who mention it, then count how many of them come back. If what those clients bring in over time is more than the ad cost in money and hours, keep it. If not, change one thing or stop.
