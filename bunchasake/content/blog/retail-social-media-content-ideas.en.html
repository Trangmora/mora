The social media content ideas for retail stores that keep working are the ones only your shop can make: the box that arrived this morning, the question a customer asked at the till, the window you changed on Monday. You do not need trends or a studio. You need five kinds of posts to rotate, a weekly plan that fits around opening hours, and captions that tell people how to get the thing. Here are 20 ideas with examples, a plan to copy and the mistakes to avoid.

Five kinds of posts every shop can rotate

When you have five minutes between customers, ask one question: which kind is it today?

  • New in. What arrived, what is back, what is nearly gone.
  • Help choosing. How to pick, how to use, what goes with what.
  • The shop and its people. Who chooses the products and what happens before opening.
  • Customers and the street. What people bought, local makers, neighbours, small events.
  • Practical information. Hours, holidays, how to reserve, how to find you.

Most shops post only the first kind. A new customer needs all five before they make the trip.

20 social media content ideas for retail stores, grouped by kind

Each idea comes with what to film and a line you can adapt. Change the products, prices and names to your own.

New in

  1. The delivery unboxing. Your hands opening the box and lifting out three items. "Just arrived. Which one goes in the window?"
  2. Back in stock. The empty shelf, then the full one, in two clips. "You asked, it is back. Message us to keep one aside."
  3. The last few. The final pieces on the shelf with the price. Only when it is true.
  4. The close-up. The stitching on a bag, the grain of a wooden bowl, the nib of a pen, filmed by the window light.

Help choosing

  1. This or that. Two similar products side by side. "Same size, different paper. Left for ink, right for pencil."
  2. How to use it. A 20-second demo: seasoning a new pan, folding a reusable bag, repotting a plant.
  3. Gift ideas by person. A carousel: "For a new neighbour", "For a teacher", "For someone who has everything". Refresh it before each holiday.
  4. The question of the week. Answer something a customer asked at the till. "Can this go in the dishwasher? Here is the honest answer."

The shop and its people

  1. Before opening. Lights on, shutter up, first coffee, ten seconds. It shows a real place with real people.
  2. Staff pick. One person, one product, one reason, in their own words.
  3. Why we stock it. The owner explains why a product made it onto the shelf, or why another one did not.
  4. The window change. A time-lapse of the old window coming down and the new one going up.

Customers and the street

  1. What people bought this week. Products on the counter, no faces.
  2. Customer photos, shared with permission. Repost what customers tag you in, after asking, and thank them in the caption.
  3. Local makers and neighbours. Introduce the bakery next door or the local maker whose candles you stock, and tag them.
  4. Small events. A pen-testing afternoon, a tasting, a drawing corner for children in the holidays. Post before, during and after.

Practical information

  1. Hours and holiday hours. One clear graphic in your colours, pinned to the top of the profile.
  2. How to find us. The walk from the bus stop or car park to your door, in fifteen seconds.
  3. How to reserve. Show the steps: send a photo of the item, we keep it until closing time tomorrow.
  4. Payment, delivery and returns. Answered once, pinned, so you stop typing the same reply.

How to turn a post into a visit

A shop post works when the viewer knows what to do next. Use a three-part caption:

  1. What it is. Name the product plainly.
  2. Why it is here. One line in your voice: why you chose it, who it is for.
  3. How to get it. The price if you can show it, "in the shop now", "message us to reserve", the street or the hours.
Linen tea towels, new this week. We chose them because they get softer with every wash. 9.50 each, in the shop now, or message us and we will keep one for you.

If many of your regulars read Vietnamese, Spanish or another language, add one short line for them at the end, for example "Hàng mới về, nhắn tin để giữ hàng." One line is enough; a full second caption doubles your work.

Pin your hours and "how to reserve" posts to the top of your profiles. If people reserve by message, a business messaging app helps: WhatsApp Business offers tools such as quick replies, and Instagram and Facebook have their own business inbox tools. Shopping and product-tag features also exist on some platforms, but what is available depends on your country and changes over time, so check the official pages for Instagram for Business and TikTok for Business before you plan around them.

Filming in a shop: what to watch

  • Light. Film before opening or in a quiet hour, near the window.
  • One background. Keep one tidy shelf or table as your filming spot. After a few weeks it becomes recognisably yours.
  • Hands and products. They tell the story, and nobody has to agree to be on camera.
  • Customers. Ask first, and take a post down the same day if someone changes their mind. With children, ask the parent, or simply leave them out.
  • Private details. Keep the till screen, card machine, receipts and delivery labels out of the frame.
  • Music. Business accounts often have a smaller library of sounds cleared for commercial use, and the rules differ by platform, so check the help pages before you use a popular song.
  • Supplier photos. Ask before you use them. Your own photos usually look more like your shop anyway.

Unboxing posts work best when your own bags and stickers look good on camera. Our guides to retail store branding and packaging design for small brands cover that side.

Common mistakes

  • Only posting offers. A feed of discounts teaches people to wait for the next one.
  • Hiding the price. "Message for price" makes people do work before they are interested. If you can show it, show it.
  • Photos with no shop in them. If every picture could come from the supplier's catalogue, nothing tells people why to buy from you.
  • Posting in bursts. Ten posts in a sale week, then a month of silence.
  • An empty profile. Hours, street and how to reserve belong in the bio and the pinned posts.

Frequently asked questions

How often should a small shop post?

Three posts a week that you keep up all year is a good start, with Stories on busy days. A steady rhythm matters more than the number.

Which platform should a shop start with?

The one your customers already use. Ask at the till for a week and note the answers. Start with one platform, do it well, and add a second when posting has become routine.

Should I show prices in posts?

Where you can, yes. A price lets people decide before they make the trip, and those who come already know what to expect. If prices change often, show "from" prices or put them in Stories.

Can I post photos of customers or what they bought?

Products, yes. People only after you ask, and children only with the parent's clear agreement. Remove any photo the same day if someone asks you to.

I sell the same brands as other shops. How can my posts stand out?

Through your choices and your voice. Show why you picked the item, how you would use it and how it looks on your own shelf, rather than reposting the supplier's photos as they are.
