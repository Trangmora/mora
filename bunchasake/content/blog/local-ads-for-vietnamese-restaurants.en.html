If you want to know how to run local ads for a Vietnamese restaurant, start with the question behind it: do you need ads at all? An ad does one thing. It puts your restaurant in front of people nearby who were not looking for you. What they do next depends on what they find when they tap: your photos, your menu, your reviews, your hours. This guide explains when paying for that attention makes sense, how to run one small test yourself, and how to tell whether it worked by counting guests.

To be clear about our role: Bunchasake does not run ads, and does not sell SEO or Google Business Profile management. This is a do-it-yourself guide. Ad platforms change their buttons, formats and rules often, so we describe them in general terms. Follow what your own account shows. You will also find no prices or "typical results" here, because honest ones do not exist for a restaurant we have never seen. You will find a method for working out your own.

When local ads are worth it, and when they are not

Ads are worth testing when all four of these are true.

  1. The free basics are done. Your Google listing is complete, your photos are your own, the menu online is current and you reply to reviews. If not, start with Google Business Profile for restaurants and the restaurant local SEO checklist. Paying to send people to a profile with wrong hours wastes money twice.
  2. You have room for more guests. If Friday and Saturday dinner are already full, an ad that brings people on Friday only creates a queue and slow service. Ads should fill the quiet times: Tuesday dinner, weekday lunch, the gap after the lunch rush.
  3. You have one specific thing to say. A new opening, a weekday lunch set, a Tết menu, catering for offices, a new regional dish. "We exist" is not an offer.
  4. You can afford to lose the test money. The first tests are how you learn. Some will bring nothing.

Ads are not the answer when guests come once and do not return. That is about the food, the service or the price, and more first-time visitors will not fix it.

Where a restaurant can advertise locally

Each option reaches people in a different moment. In general terms:

  • Search and map ads. Your restaurant appears when someone nearby searches for food. These reach people who are already hungry and choosing. You usually pay when someone taps or calls. Setup takes more care, and the ad leans on your Google listing, which you manage through Google Business Profile.
  • Facebook and Instagram ads. A photo or short video shown to people within a distance of your address. These reach people who are scrolling, not searching, so the picture has to make them hungry. The tools are explained at Meta for Business. The simple "boost" button on a post is the easiest start. The full ads tool gives more control over who sees the ad and where.
  • TikTok promotion. Paying to show one of your videos to more people in your area. It suits restaurants that already make short videos people watch to the end. See TikTok for Business for the current options.
  • Promotions inside delivery apps. Paid placement or discounts within the app. Read the terms closely, because the cost comes on top of the commission you already pay.
  • Offline, nearby. A flyer with a lunch offer delivered to the offices within walking distance, a sign on the pavement, an advert in the Vietnamese community newsletter. Old-fashioned, and very easy to measure if the flyer has to be shown at the till.

Pick one. A family restaurant running three kinds of ads at once cannot tell which one brought the guest.

How to run local ads for a Vietnamese restaurant: one small test

Step 1. Choose one goal and one offer

Write it as a sentence: "More guests at weekday lunch, with the phở and iced coffee lunch set." The offer does not need to be a discount. A set, a new dish or a limited menu gives people a reason to come now without teaching them to wait for cheaper prices.

Step 2. Choose who sees it

Almost every platform lets you limit an ad to an area around your address. Think about how far people really travel for your kind of meal. For a quick lunch, that means the offices and homes within a short walk or drive. For a weekend dinner, people come from further. Start narrow. Beyond location, keep the audience broad at first: adults in the area. If the platform offers language targeting and you have a message for Vietnamese speakers, run that as a separate, later test.

Step 3. Make the ad from what you already have

  • One real photo or a short video of the dish, steam and all, taken in daylight. No stock images, no heavy text on the picture.
  • Two or three lines of text. What it is, why it is good, when and where. "Northern-style phở with broth simmered overnight, plus Vietnamese iced coffee. Weekday lunch set, 11.30 to 2.30. Five minutes from Central Station."
  • A dish explained for newcomers. Do not assume people know what bún chả is. "Grilled pork with rice noodles and herbs" does the work.
  • One action. Get directions, call, book, or order. Choose the one that matches your goal and make sure the page behind the button works on a phone.
  • A phrase to mention. "Say 'lunch set' when you order." This is how you will count.

A Vietnamese version for the community can be as simple as: "Set trưa phở và cà phê sữa đá, thứ Hai đến thứ Sáu. Nhắn 'set trưa' khi gọi món nhé."

Step 4. Set the budget and the dates

Decide the total you are willing to spend on the whole test before you open the ad tool, and write it down. Choose an amount whose loss would not hurt the business. Enter it as a total budget with an end date, or as a daily amount with an end date, so the ad stops by itself. Two weeks is a reasonable first test for a restaurant, because it covers each weekday twice. Do not accept suggestions in the tool to raise the budget during the test.

Step 5. Tell the staff

Everyone who takes orders must know the offer, the phrase, and that they should mark the tally sheet. An ad that sends a guest to a server who has never heard of the lunch set is worse than no ad.

Judge results by counting new guests

The ad tool will show you numbers: how many people saw the ad, how many tapped. These tell you whether the ad is being noticed. They do not tell you whether anyone came to eat. For that, you count at the till. Use three simple signals together.

  • The phrase. Each time a guest mentions it, the staff make a mark.
  • The question. Ask new faces "How did you hear about us?" and mark the answer.
  • The comparison. Look at covers or receipts for the advertised time slot during the test, next to the same weekdays in the weeks before. Note anything else that could explain a change: weather, school holidays, a street closure.

At the end, do one calculation with your own numbers. Divide the total you spent by the number of new guests who came because of the ad. That is your cost for one new guest. Then compare it with what a new guest is worth to you: what a typical table leaves after the cost of the food, and how often a guest who likes you comes back. If one new guest costs less than they bring you over a few visits, the ad is paying. If it costs more than a guest could ever bring, it is not. Only you have these numbers, which is why a benchmark from someone else's restaurant is no use.

What to do after the first test

  • It brought guests at a cost you accept. Run it again, and change one thing only: the photo, or the area, or the offer. That way you learn which part matters.
  • People tapped but nobody came. The ad is fine, and something after the tap is not. Check the page behind the button, the hours, the menu, the offer itself.
  • Almost nobody tapped. The photo or the first line is not doing its job. Try a different dish or a short video.
  • Nothing happened at all. Stop. Put the effort back into free work for a while: photos, reviews, the neighbourhood.

Increase the budget only for a test that has already paid for itself at the small size.

Rules to respect

Everything in an ad must be true: the dish as served, the hours, the terms of the offer. Rules about advertising alcohol, discounts, competitions and health claims differ by country and sometimes by city. In the United States, the FTC's business guidance is the official place to read about advertising rules. For anything you are unsure about, ask a qualified adviser. This guide does not cover legal or tax questions.

Common mistakes

  • Advertising before the basics. Wrong hours and an empty profile make an expensive first impression.
  • Advertising the busiest night. You pay to make regulars wait.
  • No offer, only a logo. People need a dish and a reason.
  • An area that is too wide. People an hour away will like the photo and never come.
  • Judging by likes. Likes do not pay rent. Count guests.
  • Changing everything at once. Then you cannot tell what worked.
  • Leaving an ad running with no end date. Always set one.
  • Constant discounts. They train guests to come only when it is cheap.
  • Staff who were not told. The count fails, and so does the welcome.

The ad is the last step, not the first

An ad can only repeat what your restaurant already says about itself. When the name, the story, the menu and the photos are clear, a two-line ad is easy to write and easy to believe. Frequently asked questions

How much should a restaurant spend on local ads?

There is no correct figure that fits every restaurant. Choose a total for one test that you could lose without harm, run it for a fixed period, and count the new guests it brings. Your own cost per new guest, compared with what a guest is worth to you, tells you whether to spend more, less or nothing.

Is it better to advertise on Google or on Instagram?

They reach people at different moments. Search and map ads reach people who are looking for somewhere to eat right now. Instagram and Facebook reach people who are scrolling and may be tempted by a good photo. Start with the one you already understand best, test it alone, and try the other one later.

Should I boost a post or use the full ads tool?

Boosting a post that already did well is the simplest way to begin, and it is enough for a first test. The full tool gives more control over area, placement and budget, and takes longer to learn. Whichever you use, set the area, the end date and the total yourself, and do not accept the default settings without reading them.

How do I know a guest came because of the ad?

You will not know for every guest, and you do not need to. A phrase to mention, a question to new faces and a comparison with the same days in earlier weeks give you a count that is good enough to make a decision.

Should the ad be in Vietnamese or in the local language?

Write it in the language of the people you want at that time slot. For office workers at lunch, use the local language and explain the dish. For a Tết menu or a regional speciality aimed at the Vietnamese community, a Vietnamese ad makes sense. Run them as separate tests so you can count each one.

Do I need an agency to run ads for my restaurant?

Not for a small local test. The steps in this guide are within reach of an owner or a family member who is comfortable with a phone. If your spending grows and the setup becomes complex, a specialist can help, and you will be a better client because you already know how to count the results.
