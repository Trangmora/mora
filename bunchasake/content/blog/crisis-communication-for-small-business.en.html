Crisis communication for small business is how you respond when something about you starts spreading and threatens trust in your brand. It might be a bad review that goes viral, a customer saying your food made them ill, or a staff member posting something they should not have. Below are three common situations, a first-hour checklist, a holding-statement template and a way to prepare your team. This is step six of our six-step process.

Why every brand meets a crisis sooner or later

Running a business always brings things no one can foresee. A new technician has a bad day, a supplier delivers a bad batch, a private message ends up in a public group. None of this means your brand is bad. It means your brand is in contact with real people.

What turns a problem into a crisis is usually the response: silence for two days, a defensive reply at midnight, a deleted comment someone already saved. The response is the part you control.

What counts as a communication crisis for a small business

Not every bad review is a crisis. Ask three questions:

  • Is it spreading beyond the person who posted it (shares, local groups, other reviews mentioning it)?
  • Does it touch safety, health, discrimination or honesty?
  • Could it change whether regulars or new customers trust you this week?

If any answer is yes, follow the steps below. If not, reply as you would to a normal review. Our review response examples cover the everyday cases.

Your first-hour response checklist

Copy this list and keep it where the whole team can find it. The goal of the first hour is to stop the situation from getting worse.

  1. Pause before you reply. Nobody posts anything in anger. Take ten minutes.
  2. Decide who speaks. One person answers in public for the brand. Everyone else stays out of the comments, including family members.
  3. Capture what is out there. Take screenshots of the post, the comments and any shares, with the time. Do not delete anything.
  4. Collect the facts internally. Who was involved, what happened, what records exist. Separate what you know from what you think.
  5. Remove any ongoing risk. If it is about safety, act first: stop serving the dish, set aside the product, take the post down if it is your own staff's post.
  6. Post a short holding statement where the conversation is happening, using the template below.
  7. Reach out privately to the person affected, with a phone number or a direct message, and offer to talk.
  8. Brief your team in two sentences: what happened, what we are saying, and that all questions go to the one spokesperson.
  9. Check whether you need outside advice. If injury, illness, legal claims, employment issues or official reporting may be involved, speak to a lawyer. This guide is not legal advice.
  10. Set a time for the next update and write it down, so you keep your promise.

A holding-statement template you can copy

A holding statement buys you time to find the facts without looking as if you are hiding. It has four parts: we have seen it, we take it seriously, here is what we are doing now, here is when you will hear from us.

"We have seen [the review / the post / the concern] about [short description]. We take this seriously. Right now we are [what you are doing: speaking with the guest, checking with our team, reviewing what happened on that day]. We have reached out to [name or "the person involved"] directly. We will share an update here by [time or day]. If you have any information, please message us at [contact]."

Keep it in your brand voice. Do not admit or deny details you have not checked, and do not blame the customer. If the matter could become a legal claim, have a lawyer check the wording first.

Three common crises and how to handle each one

1. A bad review that goes viral

A client posts a one-star review of your salon with photos of a lifted set and the words "never again". A local group shares it, and new reviews start appearing from people who have never visited.

  • Reply once, publicly, under the original review: thank them, say you are sorry about the experience, and invite them to contact you directly to put it right.
  • Offer a real fix in private: a redo, a refund, a conversation with the owner.
  • If reviews appear from people who were never customers, use the platform's own reporting tools rather than replying to each one. The Google Business Profile Help Center and Yelp for Business explain what you can report and how. Rules and tools differ by platform and may change.

2. A food safety complaint

A guest writes on Facebook that they felt ill after eating at your restaurant last night, and others ask if they should worry.

  • Contact the guest privately, ask how they are, and ask for details: what they ate, when, and whether they saw a doctor.
  • Check your records for that service: suppliers, temperature logs, staff on shift. If in doubt about a dish, stop serving it until you know.
  • Follow your local rules on food safety. Depending on where you are, you may need to inform or cooperate with the health authority. Ask a lawyer or your local authority what applies to you; do not guess.
  • In public, post a holding statement. Never say "it could not have been our food" or suggest the guest is lying. Equally, do not admit a cause before you know it.

3. A staff mistake on social media

A team member posts a joke about a customer on their personal account, mentioning your shop. Someone screenshots it and shares it with the customer's friends.

  • Ask the staff member to take the post down, and keep your own screenshot as a record.
  • Apologise publicly as the business, without naming or shaming the staff member.
  • Handle the employment side privately and fairly. Employment rules differ by country and state, so talk to a lawyer or an employment adviser before any disciplinary step.
  • Afterwards, add a staff social media guideline to your crisis plan (see below).

After the first hour: the first day and the first week

Once the situation is stable, keep your promise to update people. Share what you found and what you changed. Keep replies consistent: the same facts, the same tone, from the same spokesperson. Pause any cheerful automatic replies in your inbox. Our guide to social media inbox automation explains what should never be automated.

A week later, hold a short team review of what to change. A loyal community helps here too. Customers who know you are more likely to give you the benefit of the doubt. Our guide to brand community building explains how to grow that group before you need it.

Prepare before anything happens

Write a one-page crisis plan in advance with:

  • who speaks for the brand, and who replaces them if they are away;
  • who to call: your lawyer, your insurer, your key supplier;
  • the holding-statement template above, adapted to your voice;
  • where screenshots and records are kept;
  • a staff social media guideline: what they may share about work, what they never share (customers, photos without consent, private messages) and who to ask.

Then practise one scenario with the team a few times a year. Check the reporting tools for your business pages on Meta for Business in advance, so you are not searching during a crisis.

Common mistakes in a communication crisis

  • Deleting criticism. Someone has usually saved it already, and deleting makes it look like you are hiding.
  • Going silent. Silence lets other people tell the story for you.

Frequently asked questions

What is crisis communication for a small business?

It is how you respond in public and in private when something spreads that could damage trust in your brand.

How quickly should I respond to a crisis?

Aim to post a short holding statement within the first hour or as soon as you can, even before you know all the facts, then give a fuller update at the time you promised.

Do I need a lawyer in a communication crisis?

If there is any question of illness, injury, legal claims, employment issues or official reporting, yes. Speak to a lawyer before you say anything beyond a simple acknowledgement. This guide does not give legal advice.

Can my team learn to handle crises themselves?

Yes. With a short written plan, a holding-statement template and a few practice runs a year, most small teams can handle the first hour calmly and know when to call for help.
