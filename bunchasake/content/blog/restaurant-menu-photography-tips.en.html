The most useful restaurant menu photography tips fit in one sentence: use daylight from a window, plan which dishes you need, pick the angle that suits each dish, keep the same surface for the whole set, and edit only so the food looks the way it does on the table. You can do all of it with the phone you already own, in the quiet hours between lunch and dinner.

This is a do-it-yourself guide. At Bunchasake we work on brand and content systems and we do not do photo or video shoots, so everything here is written for an owner and one helper. If you only need quick tips for filming a dish for Reels, the short version is in our restaurant Instagram content ideas. This article is about building a full, consistent set of photos for your menu and listings.

What a menu photo has to do

A social post can be playful. A menu photo is closer to a promise. Someone who has never eaten bún bò Huế looks at the picture to answer three questions: what is in it, how big is it, and does it look like something I want. That gives you three rules:

  • Show the dish as served. Same bowl, same portion, same garnishes the guest will get. An extra-full bowl for the photo creates a small disappointment at every table afterwards.
  • Make the main ingredient visible. If the dish is grilled pork with rice noodles, the pork should not be hidden under herbs.
  • Keep the set consistent. Same light, same surface, similar distance. A menu where every photo has a different colour looks like pictures borrowed from the internet.

Advertising rules differ by country, but one principle is safe everywhere: only use photos of your own food, cooked in your own kitchen.

Plan the session before you cook

  1. Write a shot list. Start with the dishes that sell most, the dishes you want to sell more of, and the ones newcomers ask about. Ten to fifteen dishes is a realistic first session.
  2. Choose the time. The gap after lunch service usually has good daylight and a free kitchen. Avoid evenings for the first session.
  3. Choose the table. One table next to a window, away from direct sun. This is your studio for every future session too.
  4. Prepare the surface and tableware. Use your real bowls, plates, chopsticks and spoons on your real table, or on one plain board if the tables are very reflective.
  5. Agree the order with the kitchen. Cold dishes and drinks first, hot soups last and one at a time, so nothing waits and wilts.
  6. Bring a small kit: a clean cloth for plate rims, tweezers or chopsticks for moving herbs, a white card to bounce light, a small spray bottle of water for herbs and glasses, and kitchen paper.

Light: one window, lights off

Put the dish on the table with the window to one side. Side light gives food shape: you can see the shine on the broth and the texture of the grilled meat. Light from behind the dish, with the window at the back of the frame, is also beautiful for soups and drinks because it shows steam and makes liquids glow.

  • Turn off the ceiling lights near the table. Warm bulbs mixed with daylight turn rice and noodles yellow.
  • Stand a white card or a folded white tablecloth opposite the window to lift the dark side of the dish.
  • If sunlight falls directly on the table, move the table back or tape baking paper over that part of the window.
  • Never use the flash. It makes broth look greasy and flattens everything.
  • If you have no usable window, choose the brightest spot with one kind of light, keep every photo in that spot, and correct the warmth afterwards.

When you are unsure, sit where a guest would sit and take the photo from their eye level. It is the view people recognise.

Plate for the camera, honestly

  • Wipe the rim. A splash of broth on the edge of the bowl is the first thing the eye finds.
  • Build height. Lift the noodles into a small mound before adding broth so the toppings sit on top and do not sink.
  • Add fresh things last. Herbs, lime, chilli and bean sprouts go on seconds before the photo. Spray herbs lightly with water if they look tired.
  • Pour broth at the table. Hot broth poured just before the photo gives steam and a clean surface. If the dish cools, replace the broth rather than photographing it flat.
  • Turn the best side to the camera. Rotate the plate until the grilled edge, the egg yolk or the prawn faces the lens.
  • Keep the sauce in the frame. For many Vietnamese dishes the nước chấm is half the dish. Show it in its real bowl.

Stay away from tricks that change what the guest gets, such as doubling the meat or using ingredients that are not in the recipe.

Background and props

For menu photos, less is better. The dish, its sauce, chopsticks or a spoon, and perhaps one drink. Use the same table or board for every dish. If your restaurant has brand colours, bring them in quietly through a napkin or a placemat; our restaurant branding guide explains how to choose them.

Before each photo, look at the four edges of the frame. Remove sauce bottles with torn labels, the napkin dispenser, the order pad and the phone charger.

Phone settings and framing

  • Wipe the lens. Kitchen air leaves a film on it.
  • Use the main camera at 1x, or 2x from a little further back for side-on shots. The wide lens bends bowls into ovals.
  • Tap the main ingredient to focus, then adjust the brightness slider that appears. Details differ between phones.
  • Turn on the grid and keep table edges straight.
  • For shots from above, hold the phone flat and level. Stand on a stable chair if you need to, and check that your shadow is not on the dish.
  • Leave space around the dish. You will crop the same photo into different shapes later.
  • Take each dish three ways: one wide, one normal, one close on the best detail. Take several frames of each.

Simple editing

Use the editor in your phone's photo app and apply the same small steps to every picture:

  1. Straighten and crop. Keep one master copy uncropped.
  2. Brightness. Lift slightly so white rice and white bowls look white, not grey.
  3. Warmth. Correct yellow or blue casts until the tabletop looks like it does in real life.
  4. A little contrast, if the photo looks flat.

Do not push saturation. Herbs that glow neon green and broth that turns orange look fake, and guests notice. Skip filters, borders and text on menu photos. Compare the edited picture with the real dish before you save it.

Short videos from the same session

While the dish is in front of you, film five to eight seconds, phone upright, elbows on the table:

  • The pour. Broth going into the bowl, or coffee dripping through the phin.
  • The lift. Chopsticks raising noodles out of the bowl, with steam.
  • The sizzle. Meat on the grill or bánh xèo in the pan, with the real sound.
  • The cut. A bánh mì cut in half, a spring roll dipped in sauce.
  • The table. A slow move across a full family table.

These clips become weeks of posts. For what to do with them, see our restaurant TikTok content ideas.

Where to use the photos

  • The menu, printed and online. Not every dish needs a photo. Photograph the ones newcomers hesitate over.
  • Your Google Business Profile. Add your own clear photos of popular dishes, the room and the storefront. Our guide to Google Business Profile for restaurants covers the listing itself.
  • Review and travel sites. Owners can add official photos through Tripadvisor for owners and Yelp for Business, depending on which sites matter in your country.
  • Delivery and ordering apps. Each app has its own photo rules and sizes, and they change. Check the requirements in your account before you upload.
  • Social media, where the same photo can be cropped tall.

Save the originals in one shared folder, named by dish, for example "pho-tai-top.jpg". When the recipe or the bowl changes, photograph the dish again.

Common mistakes

  • Using photos from the internet or a supplier. The guest receives something different, and the picture may not be yours to use.
  • Photographing at night under yellow light. No edit fully rescues it.
  • Letting the dish wait. Noodles swell, herbs wilt, fat sets on the broth. Photograph within a minute or two.
  • A crowded frame. Six dishes in one menu photo means no one knows which is which.
  • One angle for everything. A bánh mì from above is just a piece of bread.
  • Heavy editing. Colours that do not exist in food make people doubt the kitchen.
  • Different styles across platforms. The menu, Google and the delivery app should clearly show the same restaurant.
  • Never updating. A photo of a dish you no longer serve that way is a small broken promise.

Frequently asked questions

Can a phone really be good enough for menu photos?

Yes, for a printed menu at normal size, online menus, listings and social media. Good daylight and a clean, well-plated dish matter much more than the camera. For very large prints such as a wall-sized poster, test one print first.

Should every dish on the menu have a photo?

No. A photo for each section's most popular dishes, and for dishes newcomers do not know, is usually enough. Too many pictures make a menu hard to read.

How do I photograph steam?

Photograph the dish the moment hot broth is poured, with the window behind or beside it and something darker in the background. Steam shows against dark and disappears against white.

What if my restaurant has no natural light?

Choose one spot with one kind of light, switch off other lamps nearby, soften the light with white paper or cloth held at a safe distance, and always photograph there. Then correct the warmth in editing so whites look white.

How often should I retake menu photos?

Whenever a recipe, portion or bowl changes, and whenever you add dishes. Many owners also add a small seasonal session for specials.

Can I use the same photos on the menu, Google and delivery apps?

Yes, and you should, so guests see the same dish everywhere. Keep the uncropped original and make a separate crop for each place, because every platform prefers a different shape and may change its requirements.
