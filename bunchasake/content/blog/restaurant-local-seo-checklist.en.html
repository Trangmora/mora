This restaurant local SEO checklist is for owners who want to be found when someone nearby searches for a place to eat, and who would rather do the work themselves than pay for a mystery. Local SEO sounds technical. For a restaurant it is mostly housekeeping: making sure the same true facts about you appear, written the same way, in every place a guest or a search engine might look.

One thing to say plainly: Bunchasake does not sell SEO, Google Business Profile management or ads. This is a guide for doing it yourself. Search engines and listing sites also change their features often, so where we describe a screen or a setting, follow what your own dashboard shows.

How local search works, in plain words

When someone types "Vietnamese restaurant" or "phở near me", a search engine is trying to answer three questions. Does this place match what the person wants? Is it close to them? Is it known and trusted? You cannot move your restaurant closer to the searcher. You can make it very clear what you serve, and you can build the signs of trust: accurate details, reviews, and other websites that mention you.

Nobody outside the search engines knows the exact recipe, and anyone who promises you first place is guessing. The checklist below is simply the work that makes a restaurant clear and trustworthy to both people and machines.

Part 1: the Google profile

Your listing on Google Search and Maps is the first item, and it has its own guide: Google Business Profile for restaurants covers the category, hours, menu, photos and ordering links section by section. Do that first. Everything below is the work around it.

Part 2: website basics for a restaurant

You do not need a large website. You need a small one that you control and that states the facts. A social media page helps, but the platform decides who sees it, and it can disappear with a locked account. Check your site against this list.

  • Name, address and phone as text. Typed on the page, usually in the footer and on a contact section, not hidden inside an image of your flyer. The phone number should start a call when tapped.
  • Opening hours as text, including the afternoon break and the weekly closing day.
  • The menu as text. A PDF or a photo of the menu is slow on a phone and hard for a search engine to read. Type the dishes on a page: Vietnamese name with accents, a short explanation in the local language, the price if you can keep it current.
  • A page title that says what and where. For example "Phở Hà Nội, Vietnamese restaurant in Carlton". Ask whoever built the site where the page title and description are set.
  • One page per location if you have more than one restaurant, each with its own address, hours and map.
  • Fast on a phone. Open the site on mobile data, not on the restaurant wifi. If the first screen takes long to appear, the photos are probably too heavy. Ask for them to be resized.
  • A clear way to order or book near the top: a phone button, a booking link, an ordering link.
  • A secure address. The browser should show the site as secure, with no warning.
  • Your languages. The local language first, with Vietnamese where it helps your Vietnamese guests.

If someone looks after your site, ask them one more question: "Does our site have structured data for a restaurant?" This is a small piece of code that tells search engines your address, hours and cuisine in a format they read easily. You do not need to understand it, only to ask. Google's own SEO Starter Guide explains the basics in plain language and is worth an evening.

Part 3: the same name, address and phone everywhere

Search engines compare what different websites say about you. If your name, address and phone match everywhere, they can be confident the information is right. If one site has your old phone number and another has a different spelling, that confidence drops, and so does the guest's when a call goes nowhere.

Start by writing a master record. Open a note on your phone and type:

  • The name exactly as on your sign, with one fixed spelling. Decide once whether it is "Phở Hà Nội" or "Pho Ha Noi" in listings, and whether "Restaurant" is part of the name.
  • The address in the format your postal service uses, including the unit or suite number.
  • One main phone number, written in one format.
  • Your website address, your hours, and a two-sentence description.

Then copy and paste from that note every time you fill in a form. Check the places where restaurants are usually listed:

  • Google, Apple Maps and Bing
  • Yelp for Business and Tripadvisor for owners, where they are used in your country
  • Your Facebook page and Instagram bio
  • Delivery apps and reservation platforms you work with
  • Local directories: the city or tourism site, the chamber of commerce, the shopping street association
  • Vietnamese community directories and association pages

Search your restaurant's name and your phone number separately and look at the first few pages of results. Typical problems are a listing from before you moved, a number from the previous owner, and duplicates of the same restaurant under slightly different names. Claim the listings you can, correct them, and ask the site to merge or remove duplicates. It is dull work. You do it properly once, then check again only when something changes.

Part 4: reviews, the honest way

Reviews help strangers decide, and they show search engines that real people visit. The method is simple and has three rules.

  1. Ask everyone the same way. A small card with the bill, or a line on the receipt, with a QR code to your review link. When a guest says the meal was good, the server can add: "It would help our family a lot if you wrote that on Google." Do not ask only the guests who look happy and skip the rest. Selecting who gets asked is against the rules of most platforms.
  2. Never pay for it. No free dessert, no discount, no prize draw in exchange for a review, and no reviews written by family or staff. In many countries fake or rewarded reviews are also a legal matter. In the United States, the FTC's business guidance is the official place to read about it. If you are unsure what applies to you, ask a qualified adviser.
  3. Read each platform's rules. They differ. Some platforms discourage asking for reviews at all. Follow what each one says.

Then reply to every review, in the language the guest used. A pattern that works:

  • Good review: thank the guest by name, mention the dish they ordered, invite them back. "Thank you, Anna. We are glad the bún chả reminded you of Hà Nội. See you again soon."
  • Bad review: thank them, say sorry for the specific thing, say what you changed or will check, and offer a direct contact. "We are sorry your phở arrived lukewarm on Friday. That is not how we want to serve it. Please ask for Lan next time so we can make it right."
  • Unfair or fake review: reply calmly with the facts in two sentences, then report it through the platform. Never argue.

Part 5: local mentions

A mention is any other website that names your restaurant, ideally with a link. Mentions from places near you tell a search engine that you are part of the neighbourhood. You earn them by being part of it.

  • Neighbours. The hotel down the street, the gym, the language school. Ask to be included on their "where to eat nearby" page, and recommend them in return.
  • Suppliers. The baker who makes your bánh mì or the local farm you buy herbs from may list the restaurants they supply.
  • Community groups. The Vietnamese association, the temple or church, the student group, the local football club you give a meal voucher to for its raffle.
  • Local media and food writers. Send a short, friendly email when you have real news: a new regional dish, an anniversary, a Tết menu. Offer facts and a good photo. Do not offer money.
  • Events. A street festival, a Lunar New Year market or a cooking class usually comes with a page that lists the participants.

Avoid anyone who sells packages of links or hundreds of directory listings. A few real mentions from real local sites are what you want.

How to tell whether it is working

Do not chase a ranking position. It changes with where the searcher is standing. Watch three plain things instead. First, the numbers your Google dashboard shows for calls, direction requests and website visits, compared month to month. Second, the answers when staff ask new guests "How did you find us?", written on a tally sheet by the till. Third, how many reviews arrive each month. If those move the right way over a season, the housekeeping is paying off.

Common mistakes

  • A menu that exists only as an image. Neither a phone on slow data nor a search engine reads it well.
  • Different names in different places. The sign says one thing, the delivery app another, the Facebook page a third.
  • An old phone number left online after a move or a change of owner.
  • Rewarding reviews. It breaks platform rules and reads as fake to anyone who notices.
  • Buying links or listings in bulk. At best it does nothing.
  • Doing it once and forgetting. Hours, menus and prices change. Listings must follow.
  • Believing a guarantee. Nobody can promise the top spot on a map.

What search cannot do for you

All of this helps people find you. Whether they choose you, and remember you, depends on what they find: a name that sticks, a story, a menu that makes sense, photos that look like one place. Frequently asked questions

Do I need a website if I already have Google and Facebook?

A simple one is worth having. It is the only place where you control every word, it gives listings and search engines a source to confirm your details against, and it stays yours if a social account is locked. One page with your facts, menu and a way to order is enough to start.

How long does local SEO take to show results?

There is no honest fixed answer. Corrections to listings can show up quickly, while trust built from reviews and mentions grows over months. Judge it over a season by counting calls, direction requests and new guests, not by checking a position every day.

Should my restaurant's name be written with Vietnamese accents online?

Use the name as it appears on your sign, and choose one fixed spelling for listings. Many owners keep the accents on the sign, the website and the menu, and make sure the unaccented version appears somewhere in the text so people who type without accents still find them. What matters most is using the same form everywhere.

Can I give guests a small gift for leaving a review?

No. Rewards for reviews break the rules of the main platforms and can be a legal problem in some countries. Ask every guest politely, make it easy with a QR code, and let the food earn the words.

Is it worth paying for directory listings?

Usually not. Claim the free listings on the sites people in your country really use, keep them accurate, and spend your effort on real local mentions. If a directory calls you to sell a listing, ask yourself whether you have ever seen a guest use that site.
