Community marketing for small business starts with one idea: customers who share your language and background move on trust, and trust travels through people and places, not through adverts. The practical work is to be present where your community already gathers near you, online and in person, to be easy to contact in the language people speak at home, and to give happy customers something simple to repeat. Whether you run a Vietnamese bakery, a Polish deli or a Filipino nail salon, the steps are the same. This guide walks through each part, with a table and a 30-day checklist you can copy.

How a community finds a business

Think about how you found your own dentist, accountant or hairdresser after you moved here. Most likely someone told you. A cousin, a friend from church, a woman at the community grocery store, a post in a Facebook group where somebody asked, in your shared language, "Does anyone know a hairdresser nearby who speaks our language?"

That is still how it works. A recommendation passes along a short chain: someone asks, someone answers with a name, and the person who asked looks the name up to check that it is real. Your job has two parts. Be the name that gets mentioned, and look trustworthy when someone checks.

Decide which part of the community you mean

"The community" is at least four groups, and they do not use the same channels.

  • Elders and long-settled families. They phone instead of messaging, read community newspapers, listen to radio in their own language where it exists, and meet at the temple, the church, the mosque and the market. They value being greeted properly and spoken to in their own language.
  • Working families. Busy, practical, active in Facebook groups and group chats. They ask for recommendations in public and book by message.
  • Students and recent arrivals. New to the city, short on contacts, and looking for a taste of home. They live in student groups and chat apps, and they share finds quickly.
  • The generation raised abroad. Comfortable in the local language, on Instagram and TikTok, and often the ones who choose where the whole family eats or who does grandma's nails before a wedding.

Pick the one or two groups that matter most to your trade and start there. For the question of which language to use with each group, see our guide to bilingual marketing for small businesses.

How to post in community Facebook groups without being removed

Community groups are run by volunteers who are tired of adverts. Read the pinned rules before you write anything. Many groups allow business posts only on certain days or in a certain thread, and some do not allow them at all. Rules differ from group to group and change over time, so check each one.

Then behave like a neighbour.

  1. Help first. When someone asks where to buy rice paper or how to renew a document, answer if you know. People notice names that keep being useful.
  2. Introduce yourself once, as a person. Say who you are, where you are from, what you opened and why.
  3. Post news, not slogans. A new dish, holiday opening hours, a job opening, a free tea for students during exam week.

An introduction that works, written in the language the group uses, reads something like this in translation:

Hello everyone, I'm Hạnh, from Hải Phòng, and I've lived here for eight years. My husband and I have just opened a small crab noodle soup shop near the central station, cooked the Hải Phòng way. We're open Tuesday to Sunday. If you drop by, we'd love your feedback. Thank you to the admins for letting me post.

A name, a hometown, what the couple opened and where, a request for feedback and a thank you to the admin. No price war, no "best in town". If you run a page as well as a personal profile, Meta for Business explains the tools available for pages.

Be easy to reach in your customers' language

Many community customers will not fill in a booking form. They will send a short message, something like "Any space tomorrow?", and wait. If the answer comes quickly and warmly, you have a customer.

  • Choose the chat apps your community uses. Many communities have a favourite, often the app their families back home use, such as Zalo, KakaoTalk, WeChat or Viber. Messenger and WhatsApp are common almost everywhere. Ask ten customers which one they open first.
  • Use a business number, not your personal one, so staff can share the work. WhatsApp Business lets a small business set up a profile and saved replies. Features vary by country and change, so look at what is offered where you are.
  • Write five saved replies in the language your customers write in: opening hours, address and parking, prices or menu, how to book, and what to do for a large group.

Plan around the community calendar

Demand in a community rises at predictable moments. Mark these and prepare two to three weeks ahead. Many follow a lunar or religious calendar, so check the dates each year.

  • The new year your community celebrates. Lunar New Year, Nowruz or another new year is often the busiest season for food, gifts, hair, nails and anything to do with looking good and eating together. Announce opening hours and pre-orders early.
  • Religious holidays and family days. From Vu Lan to Eid or Diwali: special or vegetarian dishes, offerings, flowers, family meals.
  • Harvest and children's festivals. The Mid-Autumn Festival is one example: mooncakes, lanterns, children's events.

Make word of mouth easy

People recommend what they can describe in one sentence. Give them that sentence. "The noodle shop near the station where the owner is from Hải Phòng." "The salon where they speak our language and never rush you." If you are not sure what your sentence is, our guide on how to build a brand for a small business starts there. Then put your chat number on a card that customers can hand to a friend.

Community marketing for small business: your first 30 days

  • Week 1: Choose your one or two customer groups. Claim and correct your Google Business Profile so people who check your name find real information.
  • Week 1: Set up one business chat number and write five saved replies in your customers' language.
  • Week 2: Join the two or three most active local community groups. Read the rules. Answer three questions before you post anything about yourself.
  • Week 3: Post your introduction where the rules allow. Reply to every comment.
  • Week 3: Contact one temple, church, mosque, school or association and ask what they need for their next event.
  • Week 4: Mark the next community date on your calendar and plan one offer for it.
  • Week 4: Ask ten customers how they heard about you, and write the answers down.

Common mistakes

  • Spamming groups. The same advert in ten groups on the same day gets you removed and remembered for the wrong reason.
  • Competing only on price. A discount brings people once. In a small community, undercutting neighbours also costs you goodwill you will need later.
  • Arguing in public. One heated reply to a complaint is screenshotted and passed around. Answer calmly, then move the conversation to a private message.
  • Counting on the community alone. In most cities it is not large enough to fill a business every day. Local customers need attention too.

Frequently asked questions

Is it better to advertise or to rely on word of mouth?

For a close-knit community, start with word of mouth and presence, because that is how people decide. Paid adverts can add reach later, but they work poorly if nobody in the community has heard of you or if your listing looks empty when people check.

Which chat app should my business use?

Use the one your customers already open every day. Ask them. In many places that means the app their families back home use, plus Messenger or WhatsApp for everyone else. One number that is always answered is better than three that are not.

There are very few people from my community in my town. Is this still worth doing?

Yes, on a smaller scale. A small community is tightly connected, so one good impression travels far. Keep the chat number, the saved replies and the calendar, and put most of your remaining effort into local customers.

How do I know which channel is working?

Ask every new customer how they heard about you and keep a tally on paper at the counter. After a month the pattern is usually clear, and you can drop the channel nobody mentions.
