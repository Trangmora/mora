# Episode 2: Samurai, final English script (translated from the approved Vietnamese script)

**OPENING** *(4 s music only: battlefield at dawn, banners, a flash of steel)*
Hello everyone. For almost seven hundred years, one class of people held power in Japan. They wore two swords at their hip, yet they also wrote poetry and drank tea, and they were ready to die to protect their honor. And here's the interesting part: their name began with a very humble meaning. Samurai means "those who serve."

**WHERE THEY CAME FROM**
Our story begins around the tenth century. The imperial court sat far away in Kyoto, while out in the provinces, bandits were rising everywhere. So landowners hired men who could ride and shoot to protect their fields. And the weapon that first made the samurai famous wasn't the sword, as we usually imagine. It was the bow.

**FROM SERVANTS TO RULERS**
But the more they were needed, the stronger the samurai became, until the court could no longer control them. The two greatest samurai clans, the Taira under red banners and the Minamoto under white, fought for years, until a final sea battle in 1185, when the Minamoto won. From then on, Japan had a military ruler called the shōgun. The emperor still sat on the throne, but real power now belonged to the samurai.

**CHAOS, THEN PEACE**
A few centuries later, the country shattered again. This was the Warring States period: more than a hundred years of lords fighting without end, with castles rising everywhere. Only in 1603, when the Tokugawa family unified the country, did Japan enter two hundred years of peace. And with no more battles to fight, many samurai became officials, sitting at desks and being paid in rice. Still, they were the only ones allowed to wear two swords, one long and one short, as a sign of their rank.

**SURPRISING FACTS**
And there are plenty of surprising stories about the samurai. Not every samurai was a man: legend says the woman warrior Tomoe Gozen rode into battle as an equal of the generals. In 1581, there was even an African man named Yasuke serving at the side of the warlord Oda Nobunaga. Or take the legendary swordsman Miyamoto Musashi, who claimed he fought more than sixty duels and never lost a single one. And the katana they carried was forged and folded many times, making it both razor sharp and flexible.
A samurai without a master was called a rōnin. The most famous rōnin story took place in 1703, when forty-seven of them avenged their master together, and then accepted death together. To this day, the Japanese still tell it as a lesson in loyalty.

**THE END OF THE SAMURAI**
But times changed. In 1876, the Meiji government banned carrying swords in public, and one by one, the samurai cut off their topknots and put their swords away. A year later, the last samurai, led by Saigō Takamori, rose up one final time, and they lost. The age of the samurai was over.

**ENDING**
Yet their spirit never truly disappeared. It lives on in kendo, in films, and in the way the Japanese value discipline and honor. People who began as mere "servants" ended up becoming the symbol of an entire nation. Next time, I'll tell you about the Japanese tea ceremony. Thanks for watching, and see you next time!
